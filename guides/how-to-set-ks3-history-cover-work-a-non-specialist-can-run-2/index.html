How to Set KS3 History Cover Work a Non-Specialist Can Run

A step-by-step guide to setting no-tech KS3 History cover lessons that any cover supervisor can deliver. It includes a fill-in cover sheet template, a checklist and a complete worked example on the Black Death.

You're off tomorrow, or a colleague is, and the cover lesson needs to be ready by 8am. The person running it may be a cover supervisor, a supply teacher or a PE specialist. They may not know the difference between a primary and a secondary source. The projector might not work, and the room might not even have one.

This guide shows you how to set History cover that works anyway. It covers the structure, the cover sheet, the tasks that hold up without a specialist, and a complete example you can copy.

Why History cover often goes wrong

Most History cover goes wrong in a few predictable ways:

  • It depends on a slideshow. If the login fails, the lesson fails with it.
  • It assumes subject knowledge. "Discuss the causes" means nothing to an adult who can't model an answer.
  • It's one long task. Students finish in 15 minutes or give up after 5.
  • There's nothing to collect. Without a written outcome, the lesson doesn't feed back into your teaching.

Good cover fixes all four. It runs on paper, holds the subject knowledge in the student sheet rather than in the adult, comes in short timed chunks, and ends with work you can mark.

The 6-part structure for a 50-60 minute cover lesson

Use the same structure every time. Students and cover staff get used to it, and you can write a new lesson quickly.

  1. Settle task (5 min). Students copy the title and date, then answer three quick recall questions on the board. The cover supervisor writes these up from the cover sheet.
  2. Read (10 min). A one-page information text of about 400-500 words, written at a reading level the whole class can manage. Bold the key words.
  3. Check understanding (10 min). Five to eight short questions answered from the text. Put the answers on the cover sheet so the supervisor can go through them.
  4. Source work (10-15 min). One or two short sources: an extract, a description of an image, or a simple data table. Ask structured questions such as "What does Source A tell us about…?" and "Why might this person have written this?"
  5. Extended writing (10-15 min). One paragraph that answers a big question, with sentence starters given.
  6. Finish-early and plenary (5 min). A challenge task for fast finishers, plus a one-line exit question.

Checklist: before you leave the cover

  • [ ] Everything is on paper. No task needs a screen, the internet or a textbook the room might not have.
  • [ ] Copies are printed, or the reprographics request went in with time to spare. Print a few extra.
  • [ ] The cover sheet fits on one page.
  • [ ] The answers to the comprehension questions are on the cover sheet.
  • [ ] Every task has a time next to it.
  • [ ] A finish-early task is included.
  • [ ] Instructions are written for students, not the adult: "Answer in full sentences," not "Get them to explain."
  • [ ] Seating plan and any SEND or EAL notes are attached or easy to find.
  • [ ] You've said where the work should be collected and left.
  • [ ] Nothing depends on the previous lesson. If the class is behind schedule, the lesson still makes sense.
  • [ ] Glossary words are defined on the student sheet.
  • [ ] You've said what happens if students refuse to work (the department's normal behaviour route, by name).

Worked example: Year 7, The Black Death

This is a complete lesson that follows the structure above. You can adapt it straight away.

Settle task (on the board):

  1. Which century was the medieval period's "high point" for castles: 11th or 21st?
  2. What does "peasant" mean?
  3. Name one thing medieval people believed caused illness.

(Any reasonable answer to question 3 is fine. This is a warm-up, not a test.)

Information text (summary of what to include on the sheet):

  • The Black Death was a plague that reached England in 1348, arriving at ports on the south coast.
  • Historians estimate that it killed somewhere between a third and a half of England's population.
  • Medieval people didn't know about germs. They blamed God's punishment, bad air ("miasma") or the position of the planets.
  • People responded in different ways: praying, carrying sweet-smelling herbs, fleeing towns and whipping themselves as flagellants.
  • After the plague, there were fewer workers, so surviving peasants could ask for higher wages. The government tried to stop this with the Statute of Labourers in 1351.
  • Bold key words: plague, miasma, flagellant, peasant, Statute of Labourers.

Check understanding questions:

  1. In what year did the Black Death reach England? *(1348)*
  2. Give one thing people blamed for the disease. *(God, bad air or miasma, the planets)*
  3. What was a flagellant? *(Someone who whipped themselves to ask God's forgiveness)*
  4. Why did some peasants get higher wages after the plague? *(There were fewer workers, so their labour was in demand)*
  5. What did the Statute of Labourers try to do? *(Stop wages rising)*

Source work: Write a short, plainly worded description of a medieval doctor's advice, for example a passage recommending prayer, avoiding bad smells and carrying herbs. Then ask:

  • What does this source tell us about what people believed caused the plague?
  • Would this advice have worked? Explain using what you now know.
  • Why might a doctor in 1348 have given this advice?

Use a short extract you've checked yourself, or label your paraphrase clearly as "adapted." Students should know whether they're reading the original words.

Paragraph task: "How did people in medieval England respond to the Black Death?" Sentence starters: "One way people responded was…" / "This shows that…" / "Another response was…"

Finish-early task: Design a warning poster for a medieval town, using only ideas people had at the time. Then add one sentence at the bottom saying what we now know caused the disease.

Exit question: "Was the Black Death good or bad for surviving peasants? One sentence."

Adapting the structure to other KS3 units

The same six parts work for any unit with a clear story and a debatable question:

  • Year 7, Norman Conquest: Why did William win at Hastings? (Sources: descriptions of scenes from the Bayeux Tapestry.)
  • Year 8, Henry VIII and the Reformation: Why did Henry break with Rome?
  • Year 8, Industrial Revolution: What was life like for child workers in factories?
  • Year 9, WWI trenches: What were conditions like on the Western Front?

Tips that save the lesson on the day

  • Put the subject knowledge on the page, not in the adult. If an answer requires the supervisor to explain something, rewrite the sheet.
  • Keep sources short. Three to six lines is enough at KS3 in a cover lesson.
  • Use the same layout every time. A consistent format means less explaining and fewer "what do we do?" hands.
  • Number everything. "Do question 4" is easier to manage than "do the source bit."
  • Make the writing collectable. Use a sheet students hand in, not exercise books that go home.
  • Keep a bank. Set up three to five standalone cover lessons per year group at the start of term so that you never write one at 10pm.

Building that bank is the most useful thing you can do for your department's cover. With a standard structure and a one-page cover sheet, any adult in the room can run a calm, purposeful History lesson.
