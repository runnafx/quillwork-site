Places of Worship Sub Plan: Free Grades 6-8 / KS3 Lesson + Answers

A complete no-tech lesson that compares six places of worship: church, mosque, gurdwara, mandir, synagogue and Buddhist temple. A non-specialist substitute or cover supervisor can run it from the script, reading, tasks and answer key.

This lesson runs for 45 to 60 minutes and suits grades 6-8 or KS3 RE. The substitute needs no subject knowledge, no technology and no preparation beyond printing. Students learn what is inside six places of worship and why each feature matters to the people who use it. They are not asked to pray or join in any religious practice.

Script for the Substitute

Start (5 minutes). Write on the board: "If you designed a building where people go to feel close to God or to think about life, what would you put inside it?" Say:

"Today we're looking at six places of worship from six religions. We're learning what each building contains and why. Nobody has to believe anything or share anything personal. Take two minutes to write down three ideas."

Take three or four answers. Any sensible idea is fine.

Reading (12 minutes). Students read the six sections aloud or in pairs. Pronunciation guide:

  • Mihrab: MEE-rahb
  • Minbar: MIN-bar
  • Gurdwara: GURD-wah-rah
  • Guru Granth Sahib: GOO-roo GRUNTH SAH-hib
  • Langar: LUNG-ar
  • Mandir: MUN-deer
  • Murti: MOOR-tee
  • Bimah: BEE-mah
  • Aron Hakodesh: ah-RON hah-koh-DESH
  • Rupa: ROO-pah

If you aren't sure how to say a word, say so and carry on.

Matching quiz (8 minutes). Students complete Part A alone. Read the answers out from the key.

Comparison grid (10 minutes). Students copy the grid in Part B and fill it in with a partner.

Written answer (10-15 minutes). Students write Part C alone and in silence. In a 45-minute period, cut it to one paragraph. In a 60-minute period, use the full time and add the early finisher task.

Exit question (3 minutes). Students write down one feature that several places of worship share and one feature found in only one. Collect all the work.

Behaviour and Sensitive Topics

  • Keep the language descriptive. Say "Muslims believe" or "for Sikhs, this means". Don't present beliefs as facts.
  • Never single out a student. Don't ask anyone to speak for their religion. If a student offers something, thank them and move on.
  • Don't ask about home or personal worship. Some students have no religion, and some may not want to share theirs.
  • Don't add activities. No acting out prayers, no copying sacred writing for display, and no drawings of the Prophet Muhammad. Many Muslims find images of him deeply offensive.
  • Stop mockery straight away. Say: "We talk respectfully about what people believe. Back to your work." Then follow the school's behaviour policy.

If students ask awkward questions:

  • "Which religion is right?" Say: "People disagree about that. Today we're learning what these buildings mean to the people who use them."
  • "Do Buddhists worship the Buddha as a god?" Say: "Most Buddhists say no. They show respect to the Buddha as a teacher. Check the reading."
  • "Have you been inside one?" Say: "Today isn't about me. What does your reading say?"
  • A question you can't answer: "Good question. Write it down and I'll leave it for your teacher."

Student Reading

Church (Christianity) A church is where Christians gather for worship, often on Sundays. Many churches have an altar or communion table where bread and wine are blessed. Most have a font used for baptism, which welcomes people into the Christian faith. The pulpit is a raised stand for preaching sermons, and the lectern holds the Bible for readings. A cross reminds Christians of Jesus' death and resurrection. Some older churches are built in the shape of a cross.

Mosque (Islam) A mosque, or masjid, is where Muslims pray together. The largest weekly gathering is the Friday midday prayer, called Jumu'ah. The mihrab is a niche in the wall that shows the direction of the Kaaba in Makkah, which Muslims face when they pray. The minbar is a set of steps from which the imam gives the sermon. Many mosques have a minaret, a tower used for the call to prayer. Worshippers wash in a set way before prayer, called wudu. They remove their shoes and pray on carpet rather than chairs. Mosques show no pictures of people or animals and often use patterns and Arabic calligraphy instead.

Gurdwara (Sikhism) Gurdwara means "doorway to the Guru". The most important object inside is the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh holy scripture. Sikhs treat it with the respect given to a living Guru, and it sits on a raised platform under a canopy. Everyone covers their head and removes their shoes. Every gurdwara has a langar, a free community kitchen. Anyone of any religion or background can eat there, usually sitting together on the floor as equals. Outside, a tall flag called the Nishan Sahib shows that the building is a gurdwara.

Mandir (Hinduism) A mandir is a Hindu temple, a home for the deities. Inside are murtis, sacred images of gods and goddesses. Hindus believe God can be present in them. Worship is called puja. It can include offering flowers, food and light. In the arti ceremony, a lamp is waved in front of the murtis. Food that has been offered and blessed is called prasad, and it is shared with worshippers. Shoes are removed before entering. Some worshippers ring a bell as they arrive.

Synagogue (Judaism) A synagogue is where Jews pray, study and meet as a community. The main weekly services happen on Shabbat, which runs from Friday evening to Saturday evening. The Aron Hakodesh, or Ark, is a cupboard holding the Torah scrolls, the most holy objects in the synagogue. It is usually on the wall facing Jerusalem. Above it hangs the ner tamid, an "eternal light" that is never put out. The Torah is read from a raised platform called the bimah. Many Jewish men, and some women, cover their heads.

Buddhist Temple (Buddhism) A Buddhist temple, sometimes called a vihara, is a place for meditation, learning and showing respect. The shrine has a Buddha rupa, a statue or image of the Buddha. Most Buddhists see the Buddha as a teacher who showed the path to enlightenment, not as a god. People leave offerings such as flowers, candles and incense. Flowers wilt, which reminds Buddhists that nothing lasts forever. Candles stand for the light of wisdom. Shoes are removed, and many temples have a quiet hall for meditation.

Student Tasks

Part A: Match the feature to the place of worship. Write the letter: A church, B mosque, C gurdwara, D mandir, E synagogue, F Buddhist temple.

  1. Mihrab
  2. Langar
  3. Font
  4. Ner tamid
  5. Murti
  6. Buddha rupa
  7. Bimah
  8. Minaret
  9. Prasad
  10. Nishan Sahib

Part B: Comparison grid. Draw a table with six rows, one for each building, and three columns:

  • Most important object or feature
  • How people show respect
  • What happens there

Part C: Written answer. "A place of worship is more about the people than the building." Do you agree? Use at least two places of worship in your answer. Give one argument for, one argument against, and your own conclusion.

Early finisher: Design a respectful information sign for visitors to one of the six buildings. List five things a visitor should know.

Part B (sample answers; accept any sensible answer based on the reading):

  • Church: altar or cross; quiet, respectful behaviour; Sunday worship, baptisms, sermons.
  • Mosque: mihrab; shoes removed, washing (wudu); daily prayers, Friday Jumu'ah and sermon.
  • Gurdwara: Guru Granth Sahib; head covered, shoes removed; worship and shared langar meal.
  • Mandir: murtis; shoes removed, offerings; puja, arti, sharing prasad.
  • Synagogue: Torah scrolls in the Ark; heads often covered; Shabbat services, Torah reading, study.
  • Buddhist temple: Buddha rupa; shoes removed, offerings; meditation, learning, offerings.

Part C (what a good answer includes):

  • For: Langar shows that a gurdwara is about community and equality. A synagogue is a place to meet and study as well as pray. Worship can also happen at home.
  • Against: Some objects are treated as holy, such as the Torah scrolls and the Guru Granth Sahib. The direction a mosque faces matters, and murtis make a mandir special. The building shapes how people worship.
  • Conclusion: Any view is acceptable if it gives a reason. The strongest answers use correct key words and compare two religions directly.
