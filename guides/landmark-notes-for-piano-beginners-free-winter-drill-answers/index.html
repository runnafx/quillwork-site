Landmark Notes for Piano Beginners: Free Winter Drill + Answers

A step-by-step way to teach landmark notes (Middle C, Treble G and Bass F, plus the other Cs) to Primer and Level 1 piano students. It includes a print-free winter drill you can read aloud or copy out, plus an answer key.

Landmark notes, also called guide notes, are a small set of staff positions a student learns so well that they can name them instantly. Every other note is then read as a step or a skip from the nearest landmark. Many students try to read with mnemonics alone ("Every Good Boy..."), and that tends to break down once the music moves. Landmarks give them something faster to rely on.

This page covers which landmarks to teach first, the order to introduce them, a 5-minute lesson routine, a winter-themed drill you can use straight from this page, and an answer key.

The landmark notes, and where they live

Start with the first three. Add the others once those three are automatic.

First three (Primer):

  • Middle C: one ledger line between the staves. Written just below the treble staff or just above the bass staff, it's the same note and the same key.
  • Treble G: line 2 of the treble staff, counting up from the bottom. The treble clef curls around this line, which is why it's also called the G clef.
  • Bass F: line 4 of the bass staff, counting up from the bottom. The two dots of the bass clef sit on either side of this line, which is why it's also called the F clef.

Next four (late Primer to Level 1):

  • Treble C: space 3 of the treble staff.
  • Bass C: space 2 of the bass staff.
  • High C: two ledger lines above the treble staff.
  • Low C: two ledger lines below the bass staff.

Optional "edge" landmarks (Level 1 to Level 2):

  • Treble F: the top line (line 5) of the treble staff.
  • Bass G: the bottom line (line 1) of the bass staff.

The Cs make a symmetrical pattern. From Middle C, treble C is up an octave and High C is up two octaves. Going down, Bass C is down an octave and Low C is down two octaves. Students who see this pattern tend to remember all five Cs together.

Why three first, not all of them

Treble G and Bass F come with a built-in memory aid: the clef sign itself points to them. Middle C ties both staves to the most familiar spot on the keyboard. If a student knows these three solidly, they can reach most beginner-book notes in one or two steps or skips. Adding more landmarks before the first three are automatic usually leads to guessing.

Step-by-step: introducing landmarks over 4 lessons

  1. Lesson 1: Middle C on the staff and the keys. Show it written both ways, above the bass staff and below the treble staff. Have the student play it and say "Middle C" every time it appears in their piece. End with a quick check: you point at it on the page, and they play it without looking at their hands.
  2. Lesson 2: Treble G. Trace the treble clef with a finger and notice where the curl ends, on line 2. Play the G five keys above Middle C. Then do "neighbor" practice: line 2 goes up a step to space 2 (A) and down a step to space 1 (F).
  3. Lesson 3: Bass F. Point to the two clef dots and the line between them. Play the F five keys below Middle C. Neighbors: down a step to space 3 (E) and up a step to space 4 (G).
  4. Lesson 4: Mixed recognition plus steps and skips. Mix all three landmarks, then add one move: "Treble G, skip up" (line 3, B) or "Bass F, skip down" (line 3, D). This is where real reading starts.

Rule of thumb: a landmark counts as "learned" when the student names and plays it within about two seconds, three times in a row, on different days. Use your own judgment about pace. Some students need two weeks for one landmark, and that's fine.

The 5-minute landmark routine (every lesson)

  • 1 minute: flash. Point to landmarks in their current piece. The student names each one aloud.
  • 2 minutes: find and play. Name a landmark, and the student plays it on the correct key and in the correct octave.
  • 2 minutes: step or skip from it. Give a landmark plus a move ("Bass F, step down") and the student names and plays the new note.

Keep it brisk and the same every week. Students do better with a short routine every lesson than with a long session once a month.

Free winter drill: "Snowman Landmarks"

Read these aloud at the lesson, or copy them onto a blank staff sheet for homework. Each item gives a staff position. The student names the note, then plays it. Line and space numbers count from the bottom of the staff.

Part A: Name the landmark (Primer)

  1. The snowman stands on line 2 of the treble staff. What note is he on?
  2. A mitten hangs on the line between the two dots of the bass clef. What note?
  3. A gingerbread cookie sits on one ledger line between the staves. What note?
  4. A snowflake lands on line 4 of the bass staff. What note?
  5. A gift box sits on the line the treble clef curls around. What note?

Part B: Step or skip from the landmark (Primer to Level 1)

  1. Start on Treble G. Step up. What note?
  2. Start on Treble G. Step down. What note?
  3. Start on Bass F. Step down. What note?
  4. Start on Bass F. Skip up. What note?
  5. Start on Middle C, written below the treble staff. Step up. What note, and where is it on the staff?

Part C: The C family (Level 1)

  1. The snowman sits in space 3 of the treble staff. Which C is this?
  2. A sled rests in space 2 of the bass staff. Which C is this?
  3. An icicle hangs on two ledger lines above the treble staff. Which C?
  4. A snowball rolls onto two ledger lines below the bass staff. Which C?

Part D: Edge landmarks (Level 1 to Level 2)

  1. A star sits on the top line of the treble staff. What note?
  2. A boot sits on the bottom line of the bass staff. What note?
  3. Start on the top line of the treble staff. Step down. What note?

Bonus (keyboard): For items 1 to 5, the student plays each note, then says whether it's above or below Middle C and how many white keys away it is.

Answer key

  1. G (Treble G)
  2. F (Bass F)
  3. C (Middle C)
  4. F (Bass F)
  5. G (Treble G)
  6. A, in space 2 of the treble staff
  7. F, in space 1 of the treble staff
  8. E, in space 3 of the bass staff
  9. A, on line 5 (the top line) of the bass staff
  10. D, in the space just below the treble staff (below line 1)
  11. Treble C (one octave above Middle C)
  12. Bass C (one octave below Middle C)
  13. High C (two octaves above Middle C)
  14. Low C (two octaves below Middle C)
  15. F (Treble F)
  16. G (Bass G)
  17. E, in space 4 of the treble staff

Bonus answers: Treble G is above Middle C, 4 white keys up (C to D, E, F, G). Bass F is below Middle C, 4 white keys down (C to B, A, G, F). Middle C is 0 keys away.

Common landmark mistakes (and quick fixes)

  • Counting lines from the top. Students often say "line 2" and mean the second line from the top. Fix: always say "from the bottom" and have them touch the bottom line first.
  • Mixing up Treble G and Bass F. These two sit in similar-looking places on different staves. Fix: before naming a note, have the student say the clef out loud ("treble clef... G").
  • Playing the right letter in the wrong octave. They name Bass F correctly and then play the F above Middle C. Fix: in the find-and-play step, always ask "above or below Middle C?" first.
  • Ledger-line confusion with Middle C. The note above the bass staff and the note below the treble staff look different, and students may think they're two different notes. Fix: write it both ways side by side and have them play both. It's the same key.
  • Using mnemonics instead of landmarks. If a student mutters "Every Good Boy..." for every note, hide the mnemonic for a week and allow only "nearest landmark plus step or skip."

Quick checklist: is this student ready to move on?

  • [ ] Names Middle C, Treble G and Bass F within about two seconds
  • [ ] Plays each one in the correct octave without hunting
  • [ ] Names a step up or down from each landmark
  • [ ] Names a skip up or down from each landmark
  • [ ] Finds landmarks in their current piece without prompting
  • [ ] (Level 1) Names all five Cs and says which octave each is

When a student ticks all of these, they're ready for intervals beyond a skip and for reading by shape. That moves into whole and half steps and simple 4ths and 5ths, which most Level 1 and Level 2 method books introduce next.
