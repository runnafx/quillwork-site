How to Turn a Zoom Transcript Into Board Meeting Minutes

A step-by-step method, a fill-in template and a short worked example for turning a Zoom, Teams or YouTube transcript into draft minutes for an HOA, nonprofit, club or PTA board.

You have a 40-page transcript of last night's board meeting. Your board needs two or three pages of minutes. Neither a raw transcript nor an AI summary counts as minutes, so the work is in getting from one to the other.

This guide walks through that process. It suits secretaries and clerks of small boards: HOAs, nonprofits, clubs, PTAs and small committees.

One note first: the rules for minutes depend on your bylaws, your parliamentary authority (often Robert's Rules of Order) and, for some bodies, state law such as open-meetings rules. This page is general guidance, not legal advice. When your bylaws and this guide disagree, follow your bylaws.

The one rule that makes this easier

Minutes are a record of what was done, not what was said. Robert's Rules says this outright, and most small boards follow it.

So most of the transcript gets left out. You don't need to summarize every opinion. You need to find:

  • Who was there, and whether you had a quorum
  • Each motion: its exact wording, who made it, and what happened to it
  • How each vote came out
  • Reports given, and any action taken on them
  • Assignments and deadlines the board agreed to
  • When the meeting started, recessed, went into executive session and adjourned

Step-by-step: transcript to draft minutes

  1. Get the cleanest transcript you can. On Zoom, Teams or YouTube, download the text file instead of copying from the screen. If speaker labels show device names like "iPad (2)", write down who each one really is while you still remember.
  1. Do a find pass before you read. Search for: *move*, *motion*, *second*, *all in favor*, *opposed*, *abstain*, *carries*, *fails*, *table*, *postpone*, *quorum*, *adjourn*, *executive session*, *recess*. Highlight each hit. These are the parts of the meeting you have to get right.
  1. Build the skeleton from your agenda. Use the agenda's headings in order. If the board changed the order during the meeting, follow what actually happened, and note the change if your board does that.
  1. Fill in the header facts. Organization name, type of meeting (regular, special, annual), date, start time, place or "held by video conference", presiding officer and recorder. List members present and absent if your board does that. State whether a quorum was present.
  1. Write each motion in a fixed format. For every highlighted motion, record the maker, the seconder (if your board records seconders), the motion's wording and the result. If the wording changed through amendments, record the final version as voted on, and the amendments if your board's practice calls for it.
  1. Check every vote against the audio or video if anything is unclear. Transcription software often gets "aye" vs. "I", "nay" vs. "neigh", names and numbers wrong. A dollar amount or a vote count in your minutes should never rest on transcript text alone.
  1. Summarize reports in one or two sentences. For example: "The Treasurer presented the October financial report. It was received and filed." Attach the written report if your board does that. Leave out the discussion unless your bylaws require it.
  1. Record action items separately. Write each one as who, what and by when. Many boards list these at the end. Some put them under the agenda item where they came up.
  1. Handle sensitive material carefully. Executive session content, personnel matters, legal advice and owners' or members' personal details often should not appear in open-session minutes. Record only that the board went into executive session, the general reason if required, and any action taken when it came back out.
  1. Label it as a draft. Put "DRAFT – Not yet approved" at the top. Minutes become official only when the board approves them, usually at the next meeting.
  1. Send it for review. Ask the chair or presiding officer to check it before it goes in the board packet. Corrections should go through the approval vote, not quiet edits afterward.

Fill-in template

Copy this and replace the brackets.

[ORGANIZATION NAME] Minutes of the [Regular / Special / Annual] Meeting of the Board of Directors DRAFT – Not yet approved

Date: [Month Day, Year] Location: [Address / "Held by video conference"] Called to order: [time] by [name, title] Recorder: [name, title]

Present: [names] Absent: [names] Also attending: [manager, guests, number of members] Quorum: [A quorum was present / not present]

1. Approval of agenda [Approved as presented / approved as amended to ___.]

2. Approval of previous minutes The minutes of the [date] meeting were [approved as presented / approved as corrected: ___].

3. Reports

  • Treasurer: [one-sentence summary; action taken, if any]
  • [Committee] report: [one-sentence summary]

4. Old business

  • [Topic]: MOTION by [name], seconded by [name], to [exact wording]. [Carried / Failed], [vote count or "by voice vote"].

5. New business

  • [Topic]: MOTION by [name], seconded by [name], to [exact wording]. [Carried / Failed], [vote].

6. Member comments / open forum (if held) [Number of speakers and topics only, if that is your board's practice.]

7. Executive session (if held) The board went into executive session at [time] to discuss [general category, e.g. a legal matter] and came back into open session at [time]. [Action taken, or "No action was taken."]

8. Action items

  • [Person]: [task], by [date]

9. Adjournment The meeting adjourned at [time].

Submitted by: [name, title] Date: [date] Approved by the board on: [date, filled in after approval]

Worked example (fictional)

Below is an invented transcript excerpt from a made-up HOA, followed by the minutes written from it.

Transcript excerpt:

> Speaker 3: So the pool fence quote came in at forty-two hundred, which, honestly, I think we should just do it before spring. The old one's leaning. > Speaker 1: Okay. Do we have a motion? > Speaker 3: I'll move we accept the Hillside Fencing quote for forty-two hundred dollars. > Speaker 5: Second. > Speaker 2: Can we cap it? I don't want change orders. > Speaker 3: Fine, not to exceed forty-five hundred. > Speaker 1: Okay, so the motion is to accept the quote, not to exceed forty-five hundred. All in favor? … Opposed? … Motion carries, four to one. Dana, can you get the contract signed by the fifteenth? > Speaker 4: Sure.

Draft minutes for that item:

> Pool fence replacement: MOTION by J. Ruiz, seconded by P. Okafor, to accept the Hillside Fencing quote for pool fence replacement at a cost not to exceed $4,500. Carried, 4–1. > Action item: D. Lee to arrange the signed contract by [month] 15.

What was left out, and why:

  • "The old one's leaning" and the remark about change orders are discussion, not action.
  • The $4,200 figure became "not to exceed $4,500". The board voted on the final wording, so that is what goes in.
  • "Speaker 3" and the other labels became real names, which the secretary confirmed from the attendance list.
  • The 4–1 count came from the chair's announcement. If the transcript had been unclear, you would check it against the recording.

Quick checklist before you send the draft

  • Header lists the meeting type, date, time and place
  • Quorum is stated
  • Every motion has a maker, wording and result
  • Vote counts and dollar amounts are checked against the recording
  • No quotes or blow-by-blow discussion unless your bylaws require it
  • Executive session details are left out, and only actions are recorded
  • Speaker labels are replaced with real names
  • Action items list a person and a date
  • "DRAFT" appears at the top
  • The chair has reviewed it before it goes in the board packet

Can AI do this for me?

Partly. AI tools can find motions and summarize reports quickly, and that saves real time. But they often get names, vote counts and amended motion wording wrong. They also tend to write a narrative summary when what you need is a record of actions. If you use one, treat what it produces as a first draft and run the checklist above against the recording. Your board is responsible for what it approves, whoever or whatever wrote the first draft.
