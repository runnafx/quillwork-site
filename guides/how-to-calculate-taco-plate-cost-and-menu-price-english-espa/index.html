How to Calculate Taco Plate Cost and Menu Price (English/Español)

A step-by-step bilingual method to find what one taco order really costs you and what to charge for it, with a full al pastor example you can copy.

Many taquerias and food trucks set prices by checking what the place down the street charges. The trouble is that their pork, tortillas and rent cost something different from yours. This guide shows how to work out the real food cost of one order and turn it into a menu price. Each step has English and Spanish labels, so the cook and the bookkeeper can use the same sheet.

Important: every price below is a made-up example to show the math. Replace each one with what you actually pay, using your own invoices or receipts.

The 4 numbers you need / Los 4 números que necesita

  • Purchase price / Precio de compra: what you paid and for how much. Example: $28.00 for 10 lb.
  • Yield / Rendimiento: how much usable product is left after trimming, cooking and shrinkage.
  • Portion / Porción: how much goes on one taco or plate.
  • Target food cost % / Porcentaje meta de costo de comida: the share of the menu price you are willing to spend on ingredients.

Step 1: Cost the batch / Costo de la tanda

List everything that goes into one batch of the protein, with what each item cost.

Example: al pastor, one batch

  • Pork shoulder / Paleta de cerdo: 10 lb at $2.80/lb = $28.00
  • Marinade (dried chiles, achiote, spices, vinegar, pineapple) / Adobo: $6.00 total
  • Batch cost / Costo de la tanda: $34.00

Tip: cost the marinade as a single line by adding up the receipts for one batch. You don't need to price every gram of oregano.

Step 2: Weigh the yield. Don't guess. / Pese el rendimiento

This is the step people skip most often, and it causes the biggest pricing mistakes. Ten pounds of raw pork does not give you ten pounds of meat to serve. Some is lost to trimming, fat and cooking.

  1. Weigh the raw meat before you trim it.
  2. Cook, trim and chop it exactly as you do for service.
  3. Weigh what you can actually serve.
  4. Yield % = cooked weight ÷ raw weight.

Example: 10 lb raw gives 6.5 lb ready to serve. Yield = 65%.

Your number will be different. It depends on the cut, how you cook it and how hard you trim. Weigh it on two or three separate days and use the average.

Now find the cost per serving ounce:

  • 6.5 lb × 16 = 104 oz
  • $34.00 ÷ 104 oz = $0.327 per oz / por onza

If you had divided by the raw 160 oz instead, you'd get $0.21 per oz. That would understate your meat cost by about a third.

Step 3: Cost one taco / Costo de un taco

Use your real portion sizes. Weigh a few tacos the way your cooks actually build them, not the way the recipe says they should.

Example: one al pastor taco

  • Meat / Carne: 2 oz × $0.327 = $0.65
  • Tortillas, doubled / Tortillas dobles: pack of 80 for $4.00 = $0.05 each, × 2 = $0.10
  • Onion and cilantro / Cebolla y cilantro: a $3.00 batch covers about 60 tacos = $0.05
  • Salsa: a $5.00 batch makes 64 oz, 1 oz per taco = $0.08
  • Cost per taco / Costo por taco: $0.88

Step 4: Cost the full plate / Costo del plato completo

An order is more than the tacos. Count everything that goes out with it.

Example: order of 3 al pastor tacos

  • 3 tacos × $0.88 = $2.64
  • Lime wedges / Limones (2 wedges, a lime at $0.25 cut in 6) = $0.08
  • Radish and garnish / Rábano y guarnición = $0.05
  • Subtotal = $2.77

Add a small buffer / Margen de merma. Free extra salsa, a dropped tortilla, a slightly heavy scoop. These small losses add up. Many owners add a few percent to cover them. Here we use 5%:

  • $2.77 × 1.05 = $2.91 plate cost / costo del plato

Packaging / Empaque: if the order goes out to-go, add the foil, the container and the salsa cups as a separate line. Example: + $0.20 = $3.11 for to-go. Keeping it separate shows you whether dine-in and to-go need different prices.

Step 5: Set the menu price / Precio de venta

The formula:

Menu price = Plate cost ÷ Target food cost % Precio = Costo del plato ÷ Porcentaje meta

The target is your decision. It depends on your rent, your labor and your neighborhood. A range you'll often hear for food cost is somewhere around 25% to 35%. That's a rule of thumb, not a rule. Pick a number that leaves enough money for everything else you pay.

Example at a 30% target:

  • $2.91 ÷ 0.30 = $9.70
  • Round to a menu price: $9.75
  • Check it: $2.91 ÷ $9.75 = 29.8% food cost
  • Gross margin per order / Margen bruto: $9.75 − $2.91 = $6.84

That $6.84 is not profit. It still has to cover labor, rent or commissary fees, propane, permits, card fees and your own pay.

For a single taco: $0.88 × 1.05 = $0.92, and $0.92 ÷ 0.30 = $3.08. Price it around $3.00 to $3.25 and check the % again.

Spreadsheet formulas you can copy / Fórmulas para Excel o Google Sheets

Suppose column B holds your numbers in this order:

  • B2: Raw weight in oz / Peso crudo (oz)
  • B3: Cooked weight in oz / Peso cocido (oz)
  • B4: Batch cost / Costo de la tanda
  • B5: Portion oz / Porción (oz)
  • B6: Other ingredients per plate / Otros ingredientes por plato
  • B7: Buffer % / Merma % (enter 5% as 0.05)
  • B8: Target food cost % / % meta (enter 30% as 0.30)

Formulas:

  • Yield % / Rendimiento: `=B3/B2`
  • Cost per cooked oz / Costo por onza: `=B4/B3`
  • Plate cost / Costo del plato: `=(B5*(B4/B3)+B6)*(1+B7)`
  • Suggested price / Precio sugerido: `=((B5*(B4/B3)+B6)*(1+B7))/B8`
  • Rounded up to the next quarter / Redondeado: `=CEILING(((B5*(B4/B3)+B6)*(1+B7))/B8,0.25)`

If your copy of Sheets is set to Spanish, function names and separators can change. For example, CEILING becomes MULTIPLO.SUPERIOR, and you may need ";" between arguments instead of ",".

Same method, other dishes / El mismo método para otros platillos

  • Pupusas: cost a batch of masa and each filling (revuelta, queso, frijol con queso). Count how many pupusas one batch actually makes, then divide. Add curtido and salsa roja per order.
  • Carne asada plate / Plato de carne asada: weigh the yield after grilling and slicing. Skirt and flap can lose a lot. Then add rice, beans, tortillas and grilled onion or jalapeño per plate.
  • Horchata / aguas frescas: cost the batch (rice, sugar, cinnamon, milk if you use it). Divide by the number of cups you actually pour, then add the cup, lid and straw.
  • Combos: add up the plate costs of each item, apply your target %, and decide how much discount the combo can carry.

Checklist: the mistakes that hide your real cost / Errores comunes

  • [ ] Dividing by the raw weight instead of the cooked weight
  • [ ] Portions taken from the recipe card instead of weighed on the line
  • [ ] Forgetting tortillas are doubled
  • [ ] Not counting free chips, salsa bar or limes
  • [ ] Prices that are months old. Update them whenever an invoice changes, especially for meat, avocado, limes and cooking oil.
  • [ ] Packaging mixed into food cost, or left out completely
  • [ ] Treating gross margin as profit
  • [ ] Only the owner understands the sheet. Label it in both languages so whoever orders the food can update prices.

How often to recheck / Cada cuándo revisar

Recheck your plate costs whenever a main supplier changes a price, whenever you change a recipe or portion, and on a regular schedule even if nothing seems different. Prices can creep up slowly between invoices. If you keep one master ingredient price list and link every recipe to it, one update recalculates every plate. That way repricing takes minutes instead of an afternoon.
