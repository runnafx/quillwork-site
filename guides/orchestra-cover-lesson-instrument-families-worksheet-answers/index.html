Orchestra Cover Lesson: Instrument Families Worksheet + Answers

A no-tech, one-period orchestra or band cover lesson on instrument families and how instruments make sound. It includes a student reading, 12 questions and an answer key that a cover teacher who doesn't read music can mark.

If you run an orchestra or band and you're going to be out, you need cover work that keeps instruments in their cases and still has some real music content. The cover teacher also needs to be able to mark it without help. This page has a full lesson that does that. Copy the student reading and questions onto one double-sided sheet. Give the answer key to the cover teacher only.

The lesson is aimed at KS3 (roughly ages 11 to 14). It needs no audio, no screens, no instruments and no music reading. It takes one period of 50 to 60 minutes.

Before you leave: checklist

  • Print one sheet per student plus five spares. Leave pencils, because ensemble rooms often have none.
  • Lock the instrument store or put a sign on it. A note on the door saying "No instruments today" means the cover teacher doesn't have to make that decision.
  • Leave a register or seating plan. Mark any students who need to sit apart.
  • Put the answer key in a separate envelope labelled "Cover teacher only".
  • Name two reliable students who can hand out sheets. They should not run the lesson.
  • Say where to leave completed work, for example on your desk or in a named tray.

Plan for the cover teacher (read aloud where shown)

  • 0–5 min: Take the register. Say: "Today is a written lesson. Instruments stay in the store. Your teacher will read this work."
  • 5–25 min: Students read the information sheet and answer Questions 1–8 on their own and in silence.
  • 25–40 min: Students answer Questions 9–12. They may whisper with the person next to them.
  • 40–50 min: Read out the answers from the key. Students mark their own work in a different colour.
  • Last 5 min: Collect every sheet, including blank ones.

If students finish early: they do the extension task at the end of the worksheet.

If a student says "we always play at the start": "Not today. Your teacher has set written work."

Student information sheet

Instrument families

An orchestra groups its instruments into four families, according to how each instrument makes its sound. Every sound is a vibration. The family tells you what is vibrating.

1. Strings: violin, viola, cello, double bass and harp. A stretched string vibrates. Players either draw a bow across the string or pluck it. Plucking a string that is normally bowed is called pizzicato. The harp is always plucked.

2. Woodwind: flute, piccolo, oboe, clarinet and bassoon. Here a column of air inside a tube vibrates. The way the air gets started depends on the instrument:

  • Flute and piccolo: the player blows across an edge, like blowing over the top of a bottle.
  • Clarinet (single reed): one thin reed vibrates against the mouthpiece.
  • Oboe and bassoon (double reed): two reeds are tied together and vibrate against each other.

The family name comes from how the sound is made, not from the material. Modern flutes are usually metal and are still woodwind. Saxophones are made of brass, but they use a single reed, so they are also woodwind. You'll find saxophones in concert bands and jazz bands far more often than in orchestras.

3. Brass: trumpet, French horn, trombone and tuba. The player buzzes their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece. Valves (trumpet, horn, tuba) or a slide (trombone) change the length of the tube.

4. Percussion: timpani, snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, xylophone and glockenspiel. These instruments are hit, shaken or scraped. Some can play definite notes. These are tuned percussion, for example timpani and xylophone. Others make a sound without a set note. These are untuned percussion, for example snare drum and cymbals.

Pitch: high and low

Within each family, the bigger or longer the instrument, the lower the notes it plays. The violin is the smallest string instrument and the highest. The double bass is the largest and the lowest. In the same way, the piccolo plays higher than the flute, and the tuba plays lower than the trumpet.

Where everyone sits

Orchestras usually sit in a fan shape facing the conductor. The strings are at the front because there are the most of them. The woodwind sit behind the strings in the middle. The brass sit behind the woodwind. Percussion is at the back. The principal first violinist sits next to the conductor. This player is called the leader in the UK and the concertmaster in the US. Before the concert begins, the orchestra usually tunes to a note A played by the oboe.

Questions

Section A: Recall (1 mark each)

  1. Name the four instrument families.
  2. What is the biggest and lowest string instrument?
  3. What is it called when a string player plucks a string instead of bowing it?
  4. Name one instrument that uses a double reed.
  5. How do brass players make a sound?
  6. Which instrument does the orchestra usually tune to?
  7. Where do the percussion usually sit?
  8. What is the leader of the orchestra called in the US?

Section B: Explain (2 marks each)

  1. A flute is made of metal. Explain why it is still in the woodwind family.
  2. Explain the difference between tuned and untuned percussion. Give one example of each.
  3. Put these in order from highest to lowest: cello, violin, double bass, viola. Explain the rule you used.

Section C: Apply (4 marks)

  1. A new student has joined the orchestra and plays the trombone. Write them a short note (4 to 6 sentences) explaining which family they belong to, how their instrument makes a sound, where they will probably sit and one other fact from the sheet.

Extension: Draw a seating plan of the orchestra from above, with the conductor at the front. Label each family. Then add any instruments in our own ensemble that aren't on the sheet and say which family each one belongs to.

Answer key (cover teacher only)

You don't need any music knowledge to use this key. Accept spelling mistakes if the meaning is clear.

Section A

  1. Strings, woodwind, brass, percussion. Give 1 mark only if all four are given, in any order.
  2. Double bass. Also accept "bass".
  3. Pizzicato. Accept close spellings.
  4. Oboe or bassoon. Either one is enough.
  5. They buzz their lips into a mouthpiece. Accept "buzzing lips" or "vibrating lips".
  6. Oboe.
  7. At the back.
  8. Concertmaster.

Section B (2 marks each)

  1. 1 mark for saying that families are based on how the sound is made, not on what the instrument is made of. 1 mark for saying that the flute player blows across an edge, or that the air inside the tube vibrates.
  2. 1 mark for the explanation: tuned percussion plays definite notes and untuned percussion does not. 1 mark for a correct example of each. Tuned: timpani, xylophone or glockenspiel. Untuned: snare drum, bass drum or cymbals.
  3. Correct order: violin, viola, cello, double bass. 1 mark for the correct order. 1 mark for the rule: the bigger or longer the instrument, the lower the notes.

Section C (4 marks, 1 mark for each point included)

  • Brass family
  • Buzzing lips into a mouthpiece. Also accept a mention of the slide.
  • Sits behind the woodwind, near the back
  • Any other correct fact from the sheet

Total: 18 marks. The extension is not marked.

Adapting this for band or choir

Concert band: A concert band usually has no orchestral strings. Sometimes there is a double bass. Change the seating paragraph so it describes your own band's layout. Add saxophones and euphonium to the lists (euphonium is a brass instrument). Change Question 7 or Question 8 to ask about your band, and change the key to match.

Choir: Replace the families with voice types. From highest to lowest, these are soprano, alto, tenor and bass. Explain that singers make sound with their vocal folds vibrating. Ask students to put the voice types in order and to explain where each section stands in your choir. Write the answers into the key before you leave, because the cover teacher can't work them out.

Your own ensemble: For a higher-level question, ask students to count how many players your ensemble has in each family and say which family is the largest. Only you know these numbers, so write them in the key.

Why this format works for cover

  • Everything students need is on the sheet, so nobody has to ask the cover teacher to explain music.
  • Recall answers are short and exact, so anyone can mark them.
  • Explain answers are marked against listed points, so marking stays fair without specialist knowledge.
  • No sound is involved, so there's nothing to set up, nothing to go wrong and no noise for the classrooms next door.

Before you print, check the facts and the seating description against your own ensemble. Orchestras and bands arrange their players in different ways, and your students will notice if the sheet doesn't match their room.
