December Piano Lessons: 4-Week Winter Theory Plan (Primer–Level 2)

A week-by-week plan for the theory part of piano lessons between November and the winter break, with short secular winter activities for Primer, Level 1 and Level 2 students, plus examples and answers.

The last few weeks before winter break are hard to plan. Recital pieces need polishing, attendance gets patchy, and students are excited about the holidays. Theory is usually the first thing to get dropped. Then in January you find that half your Level 1 students have forgotten their bass clef notes.

This plan fixes that. Each lesson gets one short theory block of 5 to 10 minutes. It runs for four weeks, and each level has its own track. Every activity needs only a pencil, paper and the piano. The themes are secular winter ones (snowmen, mittens, gingerbread, gifts), so you can use them with every family in your studio.

How to use this plan

  • Keep theory short. In recital season, give it 5 to 10 minutes at the start or end of the lesson. Don't let it eat into repertoire time.
  • One concept per week. Each week has one focus for each level. If a student misses a week, start them where they left off. Don't skip ahead.
  • Week 3 is the light week. Most studios have their recital or dress rehearsal around week 3. Theory that week is a review game, not new material.
  • Send one page home each week. A half-page of practice is enough. Students who travel over the break can do it anywhere.
  • UK and Australian studios: wherever this page says quarter note, half note, whole note or eighth note, say crotchet, minim, semibreve or quaver.

Week 1: Warm-up and check where everyone is

The aim is to find gaps before the break, not to teach anything new.

Primer: Snowman landmark notes

  • Draw three snowmen. Write a landmark note on a staff on each one's tummy: treble G, middle C and bass F.
  • The student names each note, then plays it on the piano.
  • Look for: does the student find middle C straight away, or count up from the bottom of the keyboard?

Level 1: Mitten note-naming check

  • Write 10 notes on the grand staff: 5 in the treble clef and 5 in the bass clef. Include lines and spaces.
  • Time the student, but only to compare with their own time later. Don't compare students with each other.
  • Write down every note they hesitate on. Those notes are their focus for the rest of the month.

Level 2: Interval snowball check

  • Write 6 melodic intervals (2nds through 5ths) on the treble staff.
  • The student names the interval number, then plays it.
  • Look for: do they count the line and space they start on? (A common mistake is calling C to E a "2nd" instead of a 3rd.)

Week 2: Main new skill

Primer: Steps and skips down the snowy hill

  • Draw a "hill" of 5 notes going down: G, F, E, D, C.
  • Then draw a second hill that skips: G, E, C.
  • The student labels each move "step" or "skip," then plays both hills with fingers 5 to 1.
  • Rule to teach: a step goes line to space or space to line. A skip goes line to line or space to space.

Level 1: Gingerbread word decoding Write words on the staff using only the music alphabet letters A to G. The student names each note to spell the word. Winter-friendly words that work:

  • CAFE
  • BAGGED
  • BADGE
  • FACE
  • CAGE
  • BEAD
  • CABBAGE (a good challenge word: 7 notes)

Tip: put at least one word in the bass clef. Most students are slower there, and this is the gap you're trying to close before January.

Level 2: Key signature gift tags

  • Draw gift tags, each with a key signature: no sharps or flats, one sharp, one flat, two sharps.
  • The student writes the major key on each tag.
  • Answers: no sharps or flats = C major, one sharp (F♯) = G major, one flat (B♭) = F major, two sharps (F♯, C♯) = D major.
  • Extension: the student finds a piece in their current book that uses one of these keys.

Week 3: Recital week (review only)

Don't teach anything new this week. Students are nervous and short on time. Use a quick game that reviews weeks 1 and 2.

All levels: Snowflake rhythm math Write simple sums with note symbols. The student writes the total number of beats (in 4/4 or 3/4 time).

Note values to use:

  • Quarter note (crotchet) = 1 beat
  • Half note (minim) = 2 beats
  • Dotted half note (dotted minim) = 3 beats
  • Whole note (semibreve) = 4 beats
  • Eighth note (quaver) = ½ beat (Level 1 and up)

Worked examples:

  1. Half + quarter = 3 beats
  2. Quarter + quarter + half = 4 beats
  3. Dotted half + quarter = 4 beats
  4. Whole − half = 2 beats (Level 1 and up)
  5. Eighth + eighth + quarter = 2 beats (Level 1 and up)
  6. Half + dotted half + eighth + eighth = 6 beats (Level 2)

Primer adjustment: use only quarter and half notes, with at most 3 notes per sum.

Level 2 extension: ask "Is this a full measure in 3/4?" for each answer. Only the answers equal to 3 are full measures.

Week 4: Last lesson before the break

This lesson does two jobs: it finishes the month on a fun note, and it sends home work that keeps skills fresh over the break.

Primer: Black key snowflakes

  • On a keyboard diagram, the student circles every group of 2 black keys in blue and every group of 3 black keys in another colour (or uses circles and squares if you print in black and white).
  • Then the student finds every D (between the 2 black keys) and every F (just left of the 3 black keys).

Level 1: Whole and half step sledding

  • List pairs of notes. The student writes W (whole step) or H (half step).
  • Example answers: E to F = H, B to C = H, C to D = W, F to G = W, A to B = W.
  • Rule to teach: a half step has no key in between, black or white. E–F and B–C are the two white-key half steps.

Level 2: Interval and triad mittens

  • Draw pairs of mittens. On the left mitten, write a root position triad (C, F or G major). On the right mitten, the student writes the note names.
  • Answers: C major = C E G, F major = F A C, G major = G B D.
  • Extension: the student plays each triad hands separately, then blocked.

Send-home page for the break

Give every student a one-page "winter break challenge" with three short tasks at their level. A simple template:

Winter Break Challenge — Level: ________

  1. Name these notes: ________ (5 notes at their level, built from their Week 1 trouble notes)
  2. Rhythm sums: ________ (3 sums from the Week 3 game)
  3. Play it: Play your recital piece once for someone at home. They sign here: ________

Students bring it to their first January lesson. Give a sticker or small reward for any attempt, not just for perfect answers. You want them to bring it back.

Quick checklist for the month

  • [ ] Week 1: write down each student's trouble notes or intervals
  • [ ] Week 2: one new concept per level, plus a take-home page
  • [ ] Week 3: only the review game, no new material on recital week
  • [ ] Week 4: the last skill, plus the winter break challenge page
  • [ ] Mark who got the break page so you can follow up in January
  • [ ] Keep a copy of Week 1 results to compare with January

Common questions

Is secular winter theme really better than Christmas-specific? That depends on your studio. Snowmen, mittens, gingerbread and gifts work for families who celebrate different holidays or none. They also stay usable through January and February, so nothing you print goes to waste.

What if a student is between levels? Use the lower level's activity for speed and the higher level's activity as an extension. The decoding and rhythm activities scale well: just change the clef or the note values.

What about group classes? The rhythm math and word decoding activities work as team races. Have teams write answers on mini whiteboards or scrap paper, and give a point for each correct answer.

How long should each worksheet take? In the lesson, aim for 5 minutes for Primer and up to 10 minutes for Level 2. If it takes longer, the page is too long for recital season. Cut it in half and send the rest home.

The short version

Do 5 to 10 minutes of theory per lesson for four weeks. Find the gaps in week 1, teach one new skill in week 2, play a review game in recital week, and finish with a send-home challenge. Students go into the break with their reading skills intact, and your January lessons start where you left off.
