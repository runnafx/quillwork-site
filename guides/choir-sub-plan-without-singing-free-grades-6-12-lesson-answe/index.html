Choir Sub Plan Without Singing: Free Grades 6-12 Lesson + Answers

A print-and-go choir lesson that a non-musician substitute can run with no singing, no piano and no audio. It includes a minute-by-minute run sheet, a student reading, questions and a full answer key.

If you direct a middle or high school choir, you know the problem. Your substitute probably can't play piano, lead warm-ups or run sectionals, and you shouldn't ask them to. Letting students "just sing through the concert music" without a director usually leads to off-task talking or bad vocal habits.

This page is a complete lesson you can print today. Students read about how the singing voice works, answer questions and do a short writing task. The sub only needs to hand out papers, keep time and check answers against the key below. It works for grades 6-12, and there are notes on adjusting it for younger or older groups.

What to Print

  • One copy of this page for the sub (the run sheet and answer key)
  • One copy per student of the Student Reading and Questions sections
  • A seating chart or attendance list
  • Optional: lined paper for the writing task

Run Sheet for the Substitute (50-Minute Period)

  1. 0-5 min: Take attendance. Students sit in their normal sections (sopranos, altos, tenors, basses). No singing or warm-ups today. If students ask to sing, say the director has asked for a written lesson.
  2. 5-20 min: Hand out the reading. Students read silently. You can also read it aloud or ask volunteers to read one paragraph each.
  3. 20-35 min: Students answer Questions 1-10 on their own.
  4. 35-45 min: Go over the answers using the key. Students mark their own work in a different color.
  5. 45-50 min: Students start the writing task and finish it as homework if needed. Collect all papers at the bell.

Shorter period (40 min): Skip the writing task. Block period (80-90 min): Add the vocabulary match and let the writing task run the full time.

Student Reading: How Your Singing Voice Works

Your voice is an instrument, but unlike a violin or a trumpet, you can't take it out of a case and look at it. Every sound you sing depends on three things working together: breath, vibration and resonance.

Breath is the power source. When you breathe in, a dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, the diaphragm, contracts and moves downward, and your lungs fill with air. When you sing, you control how that air leaves your body. Singers practice breath control so they can hold long phrases and sing softly or loudly without strain.

Vibration makes the sound. Air from your lungs travels up through your windpipe to the larynx, sometimes called the voice box. Inside the larynx are two small folds of tissue called the vocal folds (often called vocal cords). When you sing, they come together and vibrate very quickly as air passes between them. Higher pitches come from faster vibration and lower pitches from slower vibration. Your body changes the pitch by changing the length and tension of the vocal folds.

Resonance shapes the sound. On its own, the buzz from the vocal folds would sound thin. That sound is amplified and colored as it passes through the spaces in your throat, mouth and nose. These spaces are called resonators. This is a big part of why two people singing the same note can sound completely different. The quality or "color" of a voice is called its timbre.

Articulation turns sound into words. Your tongue, lips, teeth and jaw shape vowels and consonants. Choir directors care a great deal about matching vowels across the choir so the group sounds unified, and about clear consonants so the audience can understand the words.

Voice parts. Most choirs divide singers by range, meaning how high or low they can comfortably sing. The four standard parts, from highest to lowest, are soprano, alto, tenor and bass, usually shortened to SATB. Many middle school choirs also use parts such as baritone or "cambiata" while voices are changing. During puberty the larynx grows and the vocal folds get longer and thicker. This lowers the voice, most noticeably in boys, and is a normal part of growing up. Some girls' voices also change during these years, though less dramatically.

Keeping your voice healthy. Vocal folds are delicate. Singers protect them by drinking water, getting rest, warming up before singing hard and avoiding yelling or shouting for long periods. If singing ever hurts, stop and tell your director.

Questions (Answer in Complete Sentences)

  1. What are the three things that work together to make a sung sound?
  2. What does the diaphragm do when you breathe in?
  3. Where are the vocal folds located?
  4. What is the difference between how the vocal folds vibrate on a high pitch and on a low pitch?
  5. Name two of the spaces that act as resonators.
  6. What is timbre?
  7. Name three body parts used for articulation.
  8. Why do choir directors want singers to match their vowels?
  9. List the four standard voice parts from highest to lowest. What does SATB stand for?
  10. Give two ways a singer can keep their voice healthy, and say what a singer should do if singing hurts.

Writing Task

Choose one prompt and write at least one full paragraph. Older or advanced students write two.

  • Option A: Pick a song our choir is working on. Describe one place in the music where breath control matters most (a long phrase, a held note, a very soft section) and explain what a singer has to do there.
  • Option B: Your younger sibling or friend wants to join choir next year. Write them a short guide to looking after their voice, using at least three vocabulary words from today.
  • Option C: Think of two singers you know whose voices sound very different. Using the word *timbre*, describe the difference.

Tips for the Director Before You Leave

  • Say "no singing" in writing. A clear line on the sub note heads off most "but we always sing" arguments.
  • Leave the seating chart by section. Students in their usual seats settle faster, and the sub can use the chart for names.
  • Name two reliable students who can help with handouts and show where materials are kept, but not run rehearsal.
  • Lock or cover the piano if students tend to drift toward it.
  • Give the work a grade value, even if it's just completion. Students take it more seriously when they know it counts.
  • Keep one extra copy in your emergency sub folder so the lesson is ready on a day you can't plan ahead.

Adapting for Different Grades

  • Grades 6-7: Read the passage aloud as a class, do the questions in pairs and use the vocabulary match as a game.
  • Grades 8-10: Run it as written.
  • Grades 11-12: Ask for two-paragraph writing answers. Add a question: "How might what you learned today change the way you rehearse a difficult passage?"
