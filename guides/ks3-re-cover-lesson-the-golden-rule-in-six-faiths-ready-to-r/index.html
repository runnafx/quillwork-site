KS3 RE Cover Lesson: The Golden Rule in Six Faiths (Ready to Run)

A complete one-period KS3 RE cover lesson on the Golden Rule that a non-specialist can run with no preparation. It includes a timed script, six faith quotations, student tasks, an answer guide and tips for sensitive moments.

The Golden Rule makes a good topic for RE cover. Most students already know the idea ("treat people the way you want to be treated"), so a non-specialist doesn't need to teach new content. It doesn't ask anyone to say what they personally believe. It also works for Years 7, 8 and 9.

Below is the whole lesson. You can copy it into a document, print it, and leave it for your cover supervisor.

Length: about 50–60 minutes Year groups: 7–9 (the extension task stretches older students) Resources: this page printed, lined paper or exercise books, pens. No screens needed. Learning aim: Students can explain what the Golden Rule is, find it in at least three religions, and give their own reasoned view on whether it is a good rule to live by.

Note for the cover supervisor (read this first)

You don't need any RE knowledge to run this lesson. Read the boxed instructions aloud, keep to the timings, and collect the work at the end.

  • The aim is to compare, not to decide who is right. Students don't have to agree with any religion. They only have to describe what each one says.
  • If a student asks what you believe, you can say: "Today we're looking at what different traditions teach, so I'll keep my own views out of it."
  • If a student makes a dismissive comment about a faith, keep it calm: "In RE we can disagree, but we describe other people's beliefs respectfully." Then move on.
  • If a question comes up that you can't answer, write it on the board under "Questions for Mr/Ms [teacher]" and keep going. That's a normal thing to do in RE.

Lesson timings at a glance

  1. Starter: 5 minutes. A quick-write question.
  2. Introduction: 5 minutes. Read the explanation aloud.
  3. Main task 1: 15 minutes. The quotation table.
  4. Main task 2: 10 minutes. Similarities and differences.
  5. Main task 3: 10 minutes. Scenarios.
  6. Plenary: 5–10 minutes. Exit ticket.

1. Starter (5 minutes)

Write on the board:

"Think of a time someone treated you the way you would want to be treated. What did they do?"

Students write 2–3 sentences in silence. Ask two or three volunteers to share. Nobody has to share anything personal.

2. Introduction (5 minutes)

Read aloud:

> "Today's lesson is about an idea called the Golden Rule. It's usually put as: treat other people the way you would want to be treated. People sometimes call it an 'ethic of reciprocity', which means what you give out should match what you would want back. A version of the idea shows up in many world religions and in non-religious thinking too. Today you'll compare how six religions put it into words, then decide whether you think it is a good rule to live by."

Write Golden Rule and reciprocity on the board.

3. Main task 1: The quotation table (15 minutes)

Students copy the table headings into their books: Religion | What the text says | Put it in your own words

Write the quotations on the board or hand out printed copies. These are common English translations, and the wording varies between translations.

  • Christianity: "Do to others as you would have them do to you." (The Bible, Luke 6:31. Jesus says something similar in Matthew 7:12.)
  • Judaism: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary." (Rabbi Hillel, in the Talmud, Shabbat 31a.) The Torah also says: "Love your neighbour as yourself." (Leviticus 19:18)
  • Islam: "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." (A hadith, a saying of the Prophet Muhammad, found in the collection known as An-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, number 13.)
  • Hinduism: "One should never do to another what one would regard as harmful to oneself." (A common translation of a teaching in the Mahabharata, a Hindu sacred text.)
  • Buddhism: "All tremble at violence; all hold their lives dear. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should not harm or cause others to harm." (The Dhammapada, verse 130, a common translation.)
  • Sikhism: "No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone." (Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh holy book, page 1299, a common translation.)

Support: Students who are struggling can do just three religions. Challenge: Students write which quotation they think is closest to the simple version "treat others how you want to be treated", and why.

4. Main task 2: Same or different? (10 minutes)

Students answer in full sentences:

  1. What do all of these quotations have in common?
  2. Some of the quotations say what you should do (positive). Others say what you should not do (negative). Find one of each.
  3. The Sikh quotation doesn't say "treat others as you want to be treated". How is it still connected to the same idea?

Answer guide for the supervisor (and for marking):

  1. They all ask people to think about how their actions affect others and to treat others as they would want to be treated themselves.
  2. Positive: Christianity, Islam. Negative: Judaism (Hillel), Hinduism, Buddhism. Students might argue about some of them, which is fine if they give a reason.
  3. If nobody is a stranger or an enemy, you should treat everyone with the same care you would want for yourself. It's about equality and seeing everyone as connected.

5. Main task 3: Scenarios (10 minutes)

Read each scenario aloud. Students write what someone following the Golden Rule would do, and whether it would be easy or hard.

  • A. A new student is sitting alone at lunch.
  • B. You find a phone on the bus with no lock screen.
  • C. Someone who was unkind to you last week drops their books in the corridor.
  • D. Your friend asks you to copy your homework.

Discussion prompt (if time allows): Scenario C is harder than A. Why? Does the Golden Rule still apply to people who haven't treated us well?

Scenario D has no single right answer. A student might argue that helping a friend copy isn't what you would really want for yourself in the long run. Accept any well-reasoned answer.

6. Plenary: Exit ticket (5–10 minutes)

Students write their answer at the bottom of their work:

"Is the Golden Rule a good rule to live by? Give one reason for and one reason against."

Prompts for students who are stuck:

  • *For:* it is simple, it works in every culture, it helps people get along.
  • *Against:* people want different things (I might like loud surprises, you might not), and it doesn't say what to do when two people need different things.

Extension for Years 8–9: Some people suggest a "Platinum Rule": treat others the way *they* want to be treated. Is that better or worse than the Golden Rule? Explain.

Why this lesson works for cover

  • No specialist knowledge needed. All the answers are on the page.
  • Low risk of controversy. Students compare teachings and are never asked to judge whose belief is true.
  • Easy to mark. The table and the exit ticket show quickly who understood the lesson.
  • Flexible. If the lesson starts late, skip Main task 3. If it finishes early, use the Platinum Rule extension as a whole-class discussion.

Common questions

Can I use this lesson with a class that has many students of one faith? Yes. Students may want to add what they know from their own tradition. Invite this as "In my family/community we…", which keeps it personal and respectful.

What if a student says they aren't religious? That's fine. The Golden Rule is also part of many non-religious ethical views, including humanism. The final question asks for the student's own view, so a non-religious student can answer it just as well.

Are the quotations exact? They are common English translations. Different translations use slightly different words, and some faith communities prefer certain versions. If your school's scheme of work uses a particular translation, swap it in.
