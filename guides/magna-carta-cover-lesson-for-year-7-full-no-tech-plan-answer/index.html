Magna Carta Cover Lesson for Year 7: Full No-Tech Plan + Answers

A complete one-hour Magna Carta lesson that any cover supervisor can run from paper. It includes a timed plan, a reading, a primary source, questions, an answer key and a writing frame.

You're off tomorrow, Year 7 are on the medieval unit, and the cover supervisor isn't a historian. This page has a whole lesson you can print as it is. It needs no projector or laptop, and the person running it doesn't need any history knowledge. Copy the sections below into a document, print one per student (or one per pair), and leave the answer key on the desk.

What the cover supervisor needs to know

  • Lesson length: 60 minutes. Students work on their own, on paper or in exercise books.
  • Equipment: this sheet and a pen. Nothing else.
  • What students are learning: why King John agreed to Magna Carta in 1215, and whether it really mattered.
  • Script to start the lesson (read aloud): "Today you're finding out why a king was forced to agree to a set of rules in 1215. Read the text carefully, answer the questions in full sentences, then write one longer paragraph. Your teacher will mark this."

Lesson timings

  1. 0-5 min: Settle the class. Students write the title "Magna Carta 1215" and the date.
  2. 5-15 min: Students read the text in silence, or the supervisor reads it aloud.
  3. 15-30 min: Retrieval questions 1-8, in full sentences.
  4. 30-40 min: Source questions (content, origin, purpose).
  5. 40-55 min: Extended paragraph using the writing frame.
  6. 55-60 min: Students swap books, check questions 1-8 against the answers read out by the supervisor, then tidy away.

Student sheet

Reading: Why did King John agree to Magna Carta?

In 1199 John became King of England. He also ruled large lands in France, including Normandy. By 1204 he had lost Normandy to the French king, Philip II. To win it back, John needed money for soldiers, so he raised taxes again and again. One of these was scutage, a payment barons made instead of fighting for the king. John asked for it far more often than earlier kings had.

John also argued with the Church. He refused to accept Stephen Langton, the Pope's choice as Archbishop of Canterbury. Pope Innocent III punished England with an interdict, which meant most church services stopped. In 1209 he excommunicated John, throwing him out of the Church. John gave in to the Pope in 1213.

The barons were the most powerful landowners in England. They were angry that John took their money, fined them heavily and punished people without fair trials. In 1214 John's army was beaten at the Battle of Bouvines in France, and his plan to take back Normandy failed. All that tax money had been wasted. In 1215 a group of rebel barons turned against him and captured London.

John had to bargain. On 15 June 1215, at Runnymede, a meadow beside the River Thames, he agreed to a document the barons had drawn up. It later became known as Magna Carta, Latin for "Great Charter". It had 63 clauses. Some protected the Church, some limited taxes and fines, and some promised fair justice. One clause set up a council of 25 barons to check that the king kept his word.

The peace didn't last. Within weeks John asked the Pope to cancel the charter, and the Pope declared it invalid. War between the king and the barons began again. John died in October 1216. His young son, Henry III, reissued Magna Carta in 1216, 1217 and 1225, and over time it became an important part of English law.

Most of Magna Carta dealt with problems of 1215. A few clauses are still law in England and Wales today, though. People later used it to argue that even the king must obey the law.

Retrieval questions (answer in full sentences)

  1. Who became King of England in 1199?
  2. Which area of France did John lose by 1204?
  3. What was scutage?
  4. Which Pope argued with John?
  5. What happened at the Battle of Bouvines in 1214?
  6. Where was Magna Carta agreed, and on what date?
  7. What does "Magna Carta" mean?
  8. What happened to Magna Carta within weeks of it being agreed?

Source: Clause 39 of Magna Carta, 1215

The original was written in Latin. This is a simplified translation:

"No free man shall be arrested or put in prison, or have his land or rights taken away, or be outlawed or sent out of the country, or harmed in any other way, except by the lawful judgement of his equals or by the law of the land."

Source questions: use the three steps

  • Content: What does this clause promise? Write it in your own words.
  • Origin: Who produced this source and when? Was it written by the king's side, the barons' side, or both?
  • Purpose: Why do you think the barons wanted this clause included? Think about how John had treated people.

Extended task: "How far do you agree?"

Statement: "Money was the main reason King John was forced to agree to Magna Carta."

Write one long paragraph (at least 8 sentences). Use this writing frame:

  • I agree / partly agree / disagree with this statement because...
  • One reason money mattered was... For example...
  • This led to... because...
  • However, another reason was... For example...
  • This is important because...
  • Overall, I think the most important reason was... because...

Useful words: taxes, scutage, barons, Normandy, Bouvines, the Pope, excommunicated, unfair justice, rebellion.

Finished early?

  • Write three clauses you would include in a "Great Charter" for your school today. Explain why each one is fair.
  • Draw a timeline from 1199 to 1225 with at least six dates from the reading.

Answer key (for the cover supervisor or teacher)

  1. John became King of England in 1199.
  2. He lost Normandy.
  3. Scutage was a payment barons made to the king instead of fighting for him.
  4. Pope Innocent III.
  5. John's army was beaten, so his plan to win back Normandy failed and the tax money had been wasted.
  6. At Runnymede, by the River Thames, on 15 June 1215.
  7. "Great Charter."
  8. John asked the Pope to cancel it, the Pope declared it invalid, and war broke out again.

Source answers (look for these points):

  • Content: A free man can't be arrested, imprisoned or have his property taken without a fair trial or proper legal process.
  • Origin: It comes from Magna Carta, 1215. The barons drew it up, and King John agreed to it under pressure. Strong answers note that it was a bargain between the two sides and that John didn't agree willingly.
  • Purpose: The barons wanted protection from John punishing, fining or seizing land from people unfairly. Strong answers link this to John's record of heavy fines and punishments without trial.

Extended task: what a good answer includes:

  • A clear judgement in the first and last sentence.
  • At least one money reason backed by evidence (repeated scutage, high taxes, money wasted at Bouvines).
  • At least one other reason backed by evidence (the quarrel with the Pope and excommunication, unfair justice, losing Normandy, the barons capturing London).
  • Linking words such as "because", "this led to" and "however".
  • A final judgement that weighs the reasons rather than just listing them. For example: "Money was the main cause, but John's military failure made it far worse, because the barons had paid for a war he lost."

Tips for the teacher setting it

  • Print the answer key separately so it doesn't go out to students by accident.
  • If you have an SEND or EAL student, highlight questions 1, 2, 6 and 7 as the minimum. Let them use the writing frame sentence starters word for word.
  • Leave a seating plan and a named contact (head of department or a nearby history teacher) on top of the pile.
  • Ask the supervisor to collect books open at today's page. That makes marking quick and shows who finished.
  • Follow-up next lesson: start with a five-question recap quiz using questions 1, 3, 5, 6 and 8. It shows you whether the cover lesson stuck.

Why this format works for cover

Every task can be done from the sheet alone, and the reading gives students all the facts they need. The order of read, recall, analyse a source, then write a paragraph matches the routine most KS3 history lessons already follow. The supervisor never has to explain history content. They only keep time and read the answers aloud at the end.
