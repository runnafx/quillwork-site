A Christmas Carol Context for AQA: How to Use It Without Bolting On

A checklist and set of sentence stems for teachers to help GCSE students link A Christmas Carol context to the extract and the question. It covers which facts to teach, how AO3 is credited in Section B, and before-and-after examples.

Most students revising A Christmas Carol for AQA GCSE English Literature Paper 1, Section B, know a few context facts. The common problem is how they use them. A paragraph of history about workhouses tacked onto the end of an essay does little. One clause that ties Dickens's purpose to a quotation does a lot more.

This page is for teachers planning mock revision. It gives you a short list of context worth teaching, a test for whether a context point earns its place, sentence stems students can learn, and a before-and-after example you can project in class.

How context fits into Section B

A few points to check against your current AQA specification and mark scheme before you brief students:

  • Section B (the 19th-century novel) is worth 30 marks. It has no separate SPaG marks. Those sit with the Shakespeare question in Section A.
  • Students answer one question. It starts from a printed extract and asks them to write about the extract and the novel as a whole.
  • Context is assessed as AO3: the relationship between texts and the contexts in which they were written. It carries fewer marks than AO1 (response and references) and AO2 (language, form and structure). Students should not write it as a separate section.
  • The higher levels of the mark scheme reward a response that is convincing and critical, or thoughtful and developed. Context counts most when it shows why Dickens made a choice, not when it simply describes Victorian life.

A useful line for students: context explains why Dickens wrote it, so it belongs next to the evidence.

The context worth teaching (and what each one unlocks)

You don't need twenty facts. Teach these seven well and show students which questions each one helps with.

  1. The 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act and the workhouses. Poor relief was deliberately made harsh so that people would avoid it. *Unlocks:* Scrooge's "Are there no prisons?... And the Union workhouses?" in Stave One, and the later moment when the Ghost of Christmas Present throws those words back at him. *Good for:* poverty, social responsibility, Scrooge's attitude to the poor.
  1. Thomas Malthus and "surplus population". Malthus argued that population growth would outstrip resources. Some people used his ideas to justify doing nothing for the poor. *Unlocks:* Scrooge's "decrease the surplus population" and the Ghost's rebuke over Tiny Tim. *Good for:* greed, redemption, the Cratchits.
  1. Dickens's own childhood. His father was imprisoned for debt in the Marshalsea, and Dickens was sent at about twelve to work in a blacking factory. *Unlocks:* the lonely schoolboy in Stave Two and the sympathetic presentation of poor children. Use with care: it supports a reading but doesn't prove one.
  1. The 1843 reports on child labour. That year an official commission reported on children working in mines and manufacturing. Dickens first considered writing a political pamphlet about the poor and instead wrote a story. *Unlocks:* Ignorance and Want, and the idea of the novella as a deliberate moral message. *Good for:* any question on Dickens's purpose.
  1. Ragged Schools and education. Dickens visited a Ragged School, a free school for destitute children, in 1843. *Unlocks:* the Ghost's warning to beware Ignorance "most of all". *Good for:* Ignorance and Want, social responsibility.
  1. Christian morality and charity. Dickens draws on Christian ideas of charity, repentance and redemption. Fred describes Christmas as a time when people "open their shut-up hearts freely". *Unlocks:* Fred, Marley's chains, Scrooge's transformation. *Good for:* redemption, family, Christmas spirit.
  1. The novella as a form. It was published in December 1843 as a short book in five "staves", a word borrowed from music, and it was meant to be read at Christmas. *Unlocks:* structural points about the carol-like shape, the ghost visits, and the circular ending. *Good for:* AO2 and AO3 together.

The two-question test for any context point

Before a context sentence goes into an essay, students should be able to answer yes to both of these:

  • Does it connect to a specific quotation or moment I've just analysed?
  • Does it explain what Dickens wanted his readers to think or feel?

If either answer is no, the sentence is probably bolted on. Cut it or rework it.

Sentence stems for embedded context

Have students learn three or four of these so that context comes out as part of the analysis.

  • "Dickens's Victorian readers, many of whom accepted that the poor should go to the workhouses, would have been uncomfortable hearing ______ because ______."
  • "By giving Scrooge the words ______, Dickens puts the arguments of his time in the mouth of his least likeable character, which suggests ______."
  • "Writing in 1843, the same year as ______, Dickens uses ______ to ______."
  • "The phrase ______ echoes Christian ideas of ______, so Scrooge's change is presented as ______."
  • "Dickens chose a Christmas story rather than a political pamphlet, so moments like ______ are designed to ______ the reader instead of lecturing them."
  • "Because Dickens knew poverty from his own childhood, the presentation of ______ may be meant to ______."

Before and after: a worked example

Question focus: How does Dickens present Scrooge's attitude to the poor? (Extract: the charity collectors in Stave One.)

Before (bolted on): "Scrooge says 'Are there no prisons?' which shows he is cold and doesn't care about the poor. The question shows he is sarcastic. In Victorian times there were workhouses, which were very bad places where poor people had to go. Dickens didn't like them."

What's wrong: the analysis is thin. The context takes up two sentences that never return to the quotation, and "Dickens didn't like them" says nothing about the purpose of the text.

After (embedded): "Scrooge's question 'Are there no prisons?' isn't really a question. It's a dismissal, and the rhetorical form shows he thinks the matter is settled. Dickens gives Scrooge the language of the 1834 Poor Law, which treated the workhouse as the answer to poverty, so readers who held similar views would hear their own opinions coming from the novella's least likeable character. Dickens returns to these exact words in Stave Three, when the Ghost of Christmas Present throws them back at Scrooge. This structural echo turns Scrooge's dismissal into a judgement on him and on the society that shared his attitude."

What's better: context sits inside the analysis and explains Dickens's choice. It also leads into a whole-text reference.

Common context mistakes to correct before mocks

  • Long history openings. An introduction that spends four sentences on the Industrial Revolution before reaching the question.
  • Treating context as proof. "Dickens was poor as a child, so Scrooge represents his father." Students should say "may" and "suggests", not "proves".
  • Getting the dates wrong. Students sometimes attach later events or traditions to 1843. If they're unsure of a date, they should leave it out.
  • Treating everything Victorian as one thing. Not all Victorians agreed with Scrooge. Dickens was writing into a live argument, and saying so is more convincing.
  • Forgetting the reader. Strong answers often mention how Dickens's original audience might have responded. That links AO3 to effect.
  • Ignoring Fred and the Cratchits. Context isn't only about poverty. Family, Christmas and Christian ideas of charity count too.

A five-minute starter you can use tomorrow

  1. Project one quotation, such as "decrease the surplus population".
  2. Ask students to write one sentence of analysis on the language.
  3. Give them one context card (Malthus, from the list above).
  4. Ask them to rewrite the sentence so that the context sits inside it, using a stem from this page.
  5. Share two or three rewrites. As a class, apply the two-question test.

Repeat with a new quotation and context card each lesson in the run-up to mocks. Students build a bank of ready-linked quotations that they can adapt to whatever extract comes up.

Quick student checklist

Students can tick this off when they check a practice answer:

  • [ ] I have used context at least twice, both times next to a quotation
  • [ ] Every context point says something about Dickens's purpose or his readers
  • [ ] I have no paragraph that is only history
  • [ ] I have used tentative language ("suggests", "may") where I'm interpreting
  • [ ] My context links the extract to at least one moment elsewhere in the novella
  • [ ] I have not written a date or fact I'm unsure of
