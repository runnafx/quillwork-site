How to Spot a Fake GBA Cartridge Before You Resell It (Checklist)

A step-by-step checklist for checking whether a loose Game Boy Advance cartridge is genuine before you bid, buy or list it. It covers photo checks, shell and label checks, a look at the board, play and save tests, and how to word the listing based on what you find.

Loose Game Boy Advance cartridges show up in auction lots, thrift bins and garage sales. Many of them are reproductions: copies built to look like the original. If you sell one as authentic, you can expect a "not as described" return, an angry buyer and possibly trouble with eBay.

No single check proves a cartridge is real. Experienced sellers look for a pattern of correct details, and you should do the same. A fake can boot and play perfectly, so "it works" tells you nothing about whether it's genuine.

Work through the four stages below in order: before you buy, outside the cartridge, inside it, then on a console.

What you need

  • A real GBA, GBA SP, Game Boy Micro, DS or DS Lite with a GBA slot. You need real hardware, not an emulator.
  • A small tri-wing screwdriver, because GBA cartridges are closed with a tri-wing screw.
  • A phone flashlight or desk lamp.
  • Clear reference photos of a known genuine copy of the same title and same region. US, European and Japanese copies differ, so compare like with like.

Stage 1: Checks from the listing photos (before you bid)

You often only have two or three photos. Use them to decide whether the risk is worth it.

  • Is the title named? A lot listed as "GBA games" with no titles gives you nothing to check. Price it as a gamble or skip it.
  • Label print quality. Genuine labels are sharp. Blurry artwork, washed-out or oversaturated colours, or text that looks pixelated all point to a reproduction.
  • Spelling and wording. Look closely at "Nintendo", "Game Boy Advance" and any small print. Typos are a classic sign of a fake.
  • Label fit. A genuine label sits neatly in its recess. Labels that are crooked, overlap the edges, are glossy where the original is matte, or seem the wrong size are warning signs.
  • Shell colour and shape. Compare the shell colour with your reference photos. An unusual colour for that title is a red flag. So is a seam or moulding that looks soft or uneven.
  • Is a back photo shown? If the back is never shown, assume you can't check it. Bid as if the cartridge might be fake.
  • Seller wording. Words like "untested", "as is" or "no warranty" mean you carry the whole risk. Many auction houses sell everything this way, so set your maximum bid assuming the worst outcome.

Rule of thumb: if a fake would mean a loss you can't accept, drop your maximum bid until you can accept it, or pass.

Stage 2: Outside checks (cartridge in hand)

  1. Label number stamp. Many genuine GBA labels have a small two-digit number pressed into the front label. It can be very faint. Shine a light across the label at a low angle and tilt the cartridge slowly. If you find one, that's a good sign. If you don't, keep checking, because it is easy to miss.
  2. Screw type. Genuine GBA cartridges use a tri-wing screw. A Phillips or flat-head screw doesn't prove the cartridge is fake, since people sometimes swap screws, but write it down as a warning sign.
  3. Back of the shell. Compare the moulded text, logo and any back sticker with your reference photos of the same region. Missing branding, wrong text or odd phrases are warning signs.
  4. Shell fit. The two halves should close evenly with no gaps, and the plastic should feel firm, not thin or rough.
  5. Contacts. Look at the gold contacts at the edge. Heavy dirt or corrosion doesn't mean the cartridge is fake, but it will affect testing and the condition you describe.

Stage 3: Board check (open the cartridge)

This is the most reliable check you can do at home. Remove the tri-wing screw, lift the back half off gently and compare the board with photos of a verified board for the same game.

  • Black epoxy blobs. A black blob of epoxy covering the main chip, instead of a normal printed chip, is a common sign of a reproduction. Very few official GBA games used this.
  • Nintendo markings. Genuine boards usually carry Nintendo copyright text and a board code. If those are missing, or the text looks wrong, that's a strong warning sign.
  • Stickers or ink on the board. Guides on spotting fakes mention numbered stickers or hand-inked codes on the back of the board as signs of a fake.
  • Board size and finish. A board that looks cheap or tiny, sits loosely in the shell, has messy soldering, or has a colour that doesn't match the reference photos points to a fake.
  • Save battery. Some GBA games save using a small coin battery on the board. If you see one, the save depends on that battery, and on a cartridge this old it may be weak or dead. That isn't a sign of a fake, but it changes what you can promise a buyer.

Put the board back carefully, line up the shell halves and refit the screw. Don't overtighten it.

Stage 4: Play and save test

Run these tests even if the cartridge looks genuine. Buyers care about whether it works as much as whether it's real.

  1. Clean the contacts if they're dirty. Isopropyl alcohol on a cotton swab works; let it dry fully.
  2. Boot test. Insert the cartridge with the console off, then switch on. Check that the startup logo looks right and the title screen loads.
  3. Play for a few minutes. Look for freezes, graphics glitches or sound problems.
  4. Save test. Create a save, switch the console off, wait a minute, switch it on and confirm the save is still there. Do this twice. A save that survives a normal power-off is the claim buyers care about most.
  5. Write down the console model you used, so your listing can say exactly how it was tested.

Decide based on what you found

  • Genuine, plays, holds a save after power-off: list it as authentic and tested, and say how you tested it.
  • Genuine, plays, won't hold a save: list it as "plays, does not save" (or "needs battery" if it has one) and price it lower. Say so in the title, not only in the description.
  • Genuine, won't boot after cleaning: list it as "for parts or not working". Don't describe it as "untested".
  • Several warning signs, or a reproduction board: don't list it as authentic. Check eBay's current rules on counterfeit and replica items before listing it at all. Selling it outside eBay doesn't make it acceptable to describe it as genuine. Count it as a loss in your records.
  • Mixed signals: if a few details look right and one looks wrong, compare again with a second set of verified photos. If you still aren't confident, don't call it authentic.

Fill-in listing wording (only for what you actually checked)

Use only the lines that are true for your cartridge.

Title pattern: [Game title] Game Boy Advance GBA Authentic Cartridge Only Tested [Holds Save / Does Not Save]

Description:

> [Game title] for Game Boy Advance. Cartridge only: no box or manual. > > Authenticity: I opened the shell and checked the board against a verified genuine copy. > > Tested on [your exact console model]: the game starts and plays. I created a save, powered off, and the save was still there on reload. *(Or: the game plays but does not keep a save after power-off.)* > > Condition: [describe the label and shell honestly: scuffs, tears, writing, stickers, fading]. > > [If it has a save battery:] This game saves using an internal battery. I have not replaced it, so I can't say how long it will keep holding saves. > > Photos show the actual cartridge you'll receive, front and back.

Photos to take: the front label straight on, the back of the shell, the label at an angle showing the number stamp if you found one, and the contacts. Use your own photos only, never stock images.

Quick checklist (print or save this)

  • [ ] Title named in the listing, with front and back photos
  • [ ] Maximum bid allows for the chance that it's fake
  • [ ] Label sharp, correctly spelled and seated neatly
  • [ ] Number stamp checked under angled light
  • [ ] Tri-wing screw present
  • [ ] Back of shell matches a reference copy from the same region
  • [ ] Board: no epoxy blob, Nintendo markings present, matches the reference
  • [ ] Save battery noted (yes or no)
  • [ ] Boots on real hardware
  • [ ] Save survives power-off, tested twice
  • [ ] Listing title and description match the test results exactly

If every box is ticked, you can describe the cartridge confidently and back up what you say. If any box fails, describe the cartridge as it is, or don't sell it as genuine.
