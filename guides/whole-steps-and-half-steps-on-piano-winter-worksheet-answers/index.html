Whole Steps and Half Steps on Piano: Winter Worksheet + Answers

A simple way to teach whole steps and half steps to Level 1 piano students, plus a free winter worksheet you can copy out and a full answer key. You only need a pencil and a keyboard.

Whole and half steps are one of the first places where piano students stop reading key names and start hearing distance. Once a student gets them, five-finger patterns, major scales and sharps and flats all make sense. Until then, students mostly guess.

This page gives you a 3-minute way to explain the idea, the mistakes students make most often, and a winter-themed worksheet in four parts with an answer key. It suits Level 1 students and works as a review for Level 2. The themes are snowballs, mittens and gingerbread, so it fits any family in your studio.

UK and Australian teachers: many UK books say semitone and tone instead of half step and whole step. The worksheet works the same way. Swap the words when you copy it out.

The 3-minute explanation

  1. A half step is the very next key, up or down, with no key in between. Black keys count. From C, the next key up is C sharp (the black key), so C to C sharp is a half step.
  2. A whole step is two half steps. You skip exactly one key. From C, skip C sharp and land on D. C to D is a whole step.
  3. Find the two "no black key" spots. Put the student's finger on E and F, then on B and C. There's no black key between them, so these white keys are a half step apart. This is the main thing to remember.

A quick check: ask the student to play a half step starting on any key you name, then a whole step. If they can do it without stopping to count, they're ready for the worksheet.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Counting the starting key. A student says "C, C sharp, D, that's three keys, so it's a whole step... or is it?" Teach them to count the *moves*, not the keys. A half step is 1 move and a whole step is 2.
  • Thinking white-to-white is always a whole step. E to F and B to C are both white keys and both half steps.
  • Thinking black-to-white is always a half step. F sharp to G is a half step, but B flat to C is a whole step.
  • Trusting how it looks on the staff. On the staff, C to D and E to F look the same: a line note next to a space note, or a space next to a line. Both are seconds. Only the keyboard (or knowing where E–F and B–C are) tells you which one is the half step. Accidentals can change it too.
  • Getting lost going down. Many students can do steps going up but freeze going down. Give at least a third of your practice to downward steps.

The worksheet: "Snow Day Steps"

Copy each part onto paper or a whiteboard. Students should have a keyboard nearby, or a paper keyboard if they're working at home. Allow 10 to 15 minutes for all four parts, or do one part per lesson.

Part A: Snowball sort (H or W)

Write H for a half step or W for a whole step under each snowball.

  1. E – F
  2. C – D
  3. B – C
  4. F – G
  5. F – F sharp
  6. A – B
  7. E – F sharp
  8. B flat – C
  9. D – E flat
  10. G sharp – A

Part B: Mitten moves

Start on the note in the mitten. Move the way the arrow tells you and write the note you land on.

  1. Start on G. Up a whole step.
  2. Start on D. Up a whole step.
  3. Start on E. Up a whole step.
  4. Start on B. Up a whole step.
  5. Start on C. Down a half step.
  6. Start on F. Down a half step.
  7. Start on A. Down a half step.
  8. Start on D. Down a whole step.

Tip for students: if you land on a black key, you can name it as a sharp or a flat. Both are right if you name the correct key.

Part C: Gingerbread path

Start on the first gingerbread house and follow the directions. Write the note name at each stop, then play the whole path on the piano.

Path 1: Start on C. Up W, up W, up H. Path 2: Start on G. Up W, up W, up H, down W. Path 3: Start on F. Up H, up W, down H, down H.

Part D: Build a five-finger snowman (Level 1 extension / Level 2 review)

A major five-finger pattern always follows the same steps: W – W – H – W. Build the pattern from each starting note. Write all five notes, then play them with your right hand.

  1. Start on C.
  2. Start on G.
  3. Start on D.
  4. Start on F.

Bonus question: Which pattern needed a black key? Why did it need one?

Answer key

Part A

  1. E – F: H (no black key between them)
  2. C – D: W
  3. B – C: H (no black key between them)
  4. F – G: W
  5. F – F sharp: H
  6. A – B: W
  7. E – F sharp: W (E to F is a half step, F to F sharp is a half step, so two half steps)
  8. B flat – C: W (B flat to B, then B to C)
  9. D – E flat: H
  10. G sharp – A: H

Part B

  1. G up a whole step: A
  2. D up a whole step: E
  3. E up a whole step: F sharp (also correct: G flat)
  4. B up a whole step: C sharp (also correct: D flat)
  5. C down a half step: B
  6. F down a half step: E
  7. A down a half step: G sharp or A flat
  8. D down a whole step: C

Items 3 and 4 catch students who forget that E–F and B–C are half steps. If a student writes F for item 3 or C for item 4, go back to the "no black key" spots.

Part C

  • Path 1: C → D → E → F
  • Path 2: G → A → B → C → B flat (also correct: A sharp)
  • Path 3: F → F sharp (or G flat) → G sharp (or A flat) → G → F sharp (or G flat)

Path 3 is the hardest one on the page. Let students use the keyboard, and check that they move on the keys rather than working it out in their heads.

Part D

  1. C: C D E F G
  2. G: G A B C D
  3. D: D E F sharp G A
  4. F: F G A B flat C

Bonus answer: D needed F sharp and F needed B flat. In D, the step from E to the third note has to be a whole step, and E to F is only a half step, so we raise F to F sharp. In F, the step from A to the fourth note has to be a half step, and A to B is a whole step, so we lower B to B flat. C and G work on white keys alone.

How to fit this into December lessons

  • Lesson 1: Do the 3-minute explanation and Part A. Students circle anything they got wrong and play it on the piano.
  • Lesson 2: Do Part B. Spend extra time on the downward questions.
  • Lesson 3: Use Part C as a game. You call out the directions and the student plays the path without writing anything. This works well in a busy recital week.
  • Lesson 4: Do Part D. Then ask the student to find the five-finger pattern in a piece they're already playing.

For group lessons, read out a starting note and a direction ("Start on B, up a whole step!"). The first student to play the right key earns a snowflake sticker. You can also let students take turns calling out the questions.

For students away over the break, send Part D home with a paper keyboard. Ask them to build a pattern from A (A B C sharp D E) and from E (E F sharp G sharp A B) on their own. You can check those against the W – W – H – W rule when they come back.

Quick reference card

  • Half step (semitone): the next key, no key in between. One move.
  • Whole step (tone): skip one key. Two moves.
  • White-key half steps: E–F and B–C only.
  • Major five-finger pattern: W – W – H – W.
  • On the staff: whole steps and half steps both look like a line-to-space or space-to-line move. Check the keyboard and the accidentals.
