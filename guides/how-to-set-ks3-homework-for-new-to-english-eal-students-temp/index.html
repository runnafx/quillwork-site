How to Set KS3 Homework for New-to-English EAL Students (Templates)

A practical way to turn one whole-class KS3 homework into versions a new-to-English or developing-English student can really do. Includes fill-in task templates, a home note, a marking guide and a worked Water Cycle example.

The class homework is "Write 150 words explaining the water cycle." You have a student who arrived six weeks ago and can say a few sentences in English. Excusing them sends the message that they are not part of the lesson. Setting the task unchanged is likely to produce a blank page or a pasted translation.

The fix is to keep the subject goal and change the language demand. Everyone learns the same science, geography or history. Only the amount of English they must read and produce changes.

The Bell Foundation's classroom guidance makes similar points for new arrivals: be flexible, do not penalise a student who did not understand the task, and offer an alternative that can be done in English or the first language.

Step 1: Write the subject goal in one sentence

Before adapting anything, finish this sentence:

By doing this homework, the student will show they know that ______.

Example: "...water moves from the sea to the sky and back, in stages."

If the goal is really about the content, the English can be reduced. If the goal is a language skill, such as writing a formal paragraph, then the task for a beginner needs a smaller language goal instead.

Step 2: Choose the right level of task

Go by what the student can do this week, not by how long they have been in the country. The levels below are rough guides, not formal assessments.

  • Level 1: Just arrived. Knows few English words, or is still learning the Roman alphabet. Task type: match, label, copy, draw, and work in the home language.
  • Level 2: Around A2. Can read short, simple sentences on a familiar topic. Task type: put in order, complete sentences from a word bank, write 3 to 5 short sentences with a frame.
  • Level 3: Around B1. Follows everyday classroom English but struggles with subject texts. Task type: write a short paragraph using a frame, key words and linking words.

If you are unsure, start one level lower and move up after a success. A finished task tells you more than an abandoned one.

Step 3: Fill in the template for that level

Level 1 template

  • Topic: ______
  • Key words to learn (5 maximum): ______
  • 1. Write each word in your language next to the English word.
  • 2. Label this picture or diagram with the words: [insert diagram]
  • 3. Copy this sentence and draw a picture of it: ______
  • 4. Tell someone at home about ______ in your language.

Level 2 template

  • Topic: ______
  • Word bank (8 to 10 words): ______
  • 1. Put these sentences in the correct order: ______
  • 2. Complete the sentences using the word bank: ______
  • 3. Write 3 sentences. Start with: First, ______ Then, ______ Finally, ______

Level 3 template

  • Topic: ______
  • Key words (use at least 5): ______
  • Linking words: because, so, however, after that
  • Question: ______
  • Plan in any language: ______
  • Write 80 to 100 words in English using this frame:
  • "______ happens when ______. First, ______. This is because ______. After that, ______. As a result, ______."

Worked example: the Water Cycle

Class task: Write 150 words explaining how water moves around the Earth.

Subject goal: Water moves from the sea to the sky and back, in stages.

Level 1 version

  • Key words: sun, sea, cloud, rain, river.
  • Write each word in your language.
  • Label the diagram with the five words.
  • Copy and draw: "The rain falls from the cloud."
  • Tell someone at home in your language: where does rain come from?

Level 2 version

Word bank: sun, heats, sea, water vapour, cold, cloud, rain, river.

Put in order:

  • A. The drops fall as rain.
  • B. The sun heats the water in the sea.
  • C. The rivers carry the water back to the sea.
  • D. The water vapour goes up and gets cold. It makes clouds.

Correct order: B, D, A, C.

Then write three sentences: First, ______ Then, ______ Finally, ______

Level 3 version

Use at least five of: evaporation, water vapour, condensation, droplets, precipitation, collection.

Frame: "The water cycle is the movement of water between ______. First, the sun ______, which causes ______. When the water vapour rises, it ______ because ______. After that, ______. Finally, ______."

Model answer: "The water cycle is the movement of water between the sea, the air and the land. First, the sun heats the sea, which causes evaporation. When the water vapour rises, it cools and condensation happens because the air is colder high up. After that, the droplets form clouds and fall as precipitation. Finally, rivers carry the water back to the sea. This is called collection, and then the cycle starts again."

All three students have now worked on the same idea, so all three can join the next lesson's discussion.

Step 4: Make sure the homework reaches home

A well-adapted task can still fail at this point.

  • Give the task on paper, or stick it in the planner for the student. Copying from the board at the end of a lesson is hard for a new arrival.
  • Show one finished example of the first question.
  • Check understanding by asking the student to point to or show what they will do. "Do you understand?" often gets a polite yes.
  • Check that the student has what the task needs. If it requires a device, internet access or a quiet place to work, offer a paper version or a homework club.
  • Do not assume someone at home can read English, or can read the home language. Some students have also had interrupted schooling and may not read their home language confidently.

Fill-in note for home (ask your EAL lead or the school's usual interpreting route to translate it):

"Dear family, this is ______'s homework for ______ (subject). It is due on ______. It should take about ______ minutes. Your child can talk about it with you and can use your home language. You do not need to speak English to help. If there is a problem, please contact ______."

Step 5: Be clear about translation tools

Bilingual dictionaries and translation apps are useful for single words, so give students clear rules:

  • Allowed: looking up key words, checking a meaning, and planning in the home language.
  • Not useful: pasting a whole English answer from a translator, because it does not show what the student can do.
  • Teach students to check a word against the example sentence. Subject words can go wrong. "Cell", "table" and "force" all have everyday meanings as well as subject ones.
  • Follow your school's rules on which apps and online tools students may use.

If a piece of work looks far above the student's level, ask them to explain one sentence aloud. Treat this as a check on learning, not an accusation.

Step 6: Mark the subject and one language point

Do not correct every error. Use this short guide:

  • Subject: Did they show the goal from Step 1? Say what they got right.
  • Language: Pick one thing to practise next, such as the past tense or using "because".
  • Credit home-language work. Notes in another language are evidence of thinking. If you cannot read them, ask the student to talk you through them, or ask a colleague who shares the language.

Move a student up when they complete a level well on two tasks in a row. Move back a level for one task if they are stuck, then try again.

Quick checklist before you set it

  • [ ] The subject goal is written in one sentence
  • [ ] The student's version has the same goal as the class version
  • [ ] Instructions use short sentences and one action per line
  • [ ] Key words are given, not left for the student to find
  • [ ] There is a picture, frame or example to copy from
  • [ ] The student has shown you what to do
  • [ ] The task can be done without help in English at home
  • [ ] The home language is allowed for planning
  • [ ] You know the one thing you will mark for language
