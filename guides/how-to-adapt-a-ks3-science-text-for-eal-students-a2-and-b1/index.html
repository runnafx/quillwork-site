How to Adapt a KS3 Science Text for EAL Students (A2 and B1)

A step-by-step method for rewriting one KS3 text at two CEFR levels, so new-to-English and developing-English students learn the same content as their class. Includes a full worked example on the water cycle, a glossary template and a checklist.

You have a Year 8 class reading about the water cycle. Two students arrived this term with very little English. A third can hold a conversation but gets lost in textbook paragraphs. You don't want to give them a different, easier topic. You want them learning the same science, in English they can actually read.

This guide shows one way to do that. You rewrite a single source text at two levels: roughly CEFR A2 (elementary) and CEFR B1 (intermediate). Everyone works on the same content, takes part in the same discussion and builds the same subject vocabulary.

CEFR levels describe broad ability bands. They are not exact word counts. Treat the targets below as practical guidance, and adjust them for the students in front of you.

Step 1: Decide what must survive

Before you change a single word, write down the 3-5 key ideas every student must leave with. Everything else can be cut from the A2 version.

For the water cycle:

  • Water moves around the Earth in a cycle.
  • The sun heats water and it evaporates (it becomes water vapour).
  • Water vapour cools and condenses into clouds.
  • Water falls as precipitation (rain, snow, hail).
  • Water collects in seas, rivers and underground, and the cycle starts again.

If a sentence in the original doesn't support one of these, it is optional.

Step 2: Pick the subject words to keep

Don't simplify the subject vocabulary. *Evaporation* is the word in the test, on the board and in the next lesson. Keep it, and support it instead.

Sort the words in the original text into two groups:

  1. Keep and teach: subject terms the student needs (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, water vapour, collection, cycle).
  2. Replace: hard general words that are not the point of the lesson (e.g. "subsequently" becomes "then", "accumulate" becomes "collect", "atmospheric" becomes "in the air").

Aim for about 12-15 "keep and teach" words per topic. Those go in your glossary (Step 5).

Step 3: Write the A2 version

Targets for an A2 text:

  • About 200-250 words.
  • One idea per sentence. Most sentences under 12 words.
  • Mostly present simple ("The sun heats the water"), with past simple only where it's needed.
  • No passive voice if you can avoid it. "The sun heats the water", not "The water is heated by the sun".
  • Repeat the key word instead of using a pronoun or a synonym. Write "the water vapour", not "it" or "this gas".
  • One short heading or label for each stage.
  • Bold each subject word the first time it appears.

Step 4: Write the B1 version

Targets for a B1 text:

  • About 350-450 words.
  • Same key ideas, plus one or two extra details or reasons.
  • Join ideas with simple connectives: *because, so, when, after, however*.
  • Some passive voice is fine ("is stored", "is called"), because students will meet it in subject textbooks.
  • Paragraphs instead of single-line points.
  • Same bolded subject words as the A2 version, so both groups can share a glossary and a word wall.

Worked example: the water cycle

Typical textbook-style original

"Water is continuously recycled through the Earth's systems. Solar energy causes water from oceans, lakes and rivers to evaporate, forming water vapour, which rises into the atmosphere. As it ascends, the vapour cools and condenses into tiny droplets, forming clouds. When these droplets combine and become sufficiently heavy, they fall as precipitation. This water subsequently accumulates in bodies of water or infiltrates the ground, and the cycle recommences."

A2 version

The Water Cycle

Water moves around the Earth. It goes round and round. We call this the water cycle.

1. Evaporation The sun is hot. The sun heats the water in the sea. The water changes into a gas. This gas is water vapour. We call this change evaporation. The water vapour goes up into the sky.

2. Condensation It is cold high in the sky. The water vapour gets cold. It changes into very small drops of water. The small drops make clouds. We call this change condensation.

3. Precipitation The drops in the clouds join together. The drops get big and heavy. Then they fall down. Water falls as rain, snow or hail. We call this precipitation.

4. Collection The water goes into rivers, lakes and the sea. Some water goes into the ground. We call this collection.

Then the sun heats the water again. The water cycle starts again. It never stops.

(About 200 words.)

B1 version

The Water Cycle

The water on Earth is always moving. It travels from the sea to the sky and back again in a continuous loop. This is called the water cycle, and it has four main stages.

Evaporation The sun heats water in oceans, lakes and rivers. When water gets warm enough, it changes from a liquid into a gas called water vapour. This process is called evaporation. Water vapour is invisible and lighter than air, so it rises into the sky.

Condensation The air high above the ground is much colder. When the water vapour rises, it cools down and changes back into tiny drops of liquid water. This is called condensation. Millions of these tiny drops together form clouds. You can see condensation at home too, when steam from a shower turns into drops of water on a cold mirror.

Precipitation Inside a cloud, the tiny drops bump into each other and join together. They get bigger and heavier. When they are too heavy to stay in the air, they fall to the ground. Water that falls from clouds is called precipitation. It can be rain, snow, sleet or hail, depending on how cold the air is.

Collection After it falls, the water collects in oceans, rivers and lakes. Some of it soaks into the ground and is stored there as groundwater. Plants also take up water through their roots. This stage is called collection.

The sun then heats the collected water again, so the cycle continues. Because of the water cycle, the Earth has roughly the same amount of water today as it had long ago. The water just keeps moving between the land, the sea and the sky.

(About 300 words. For a full B1 text, add one more paragraph, for example on why the water cycle matters for farming or drinking water.)

Step 5: Build a glossary with a home-language column

A glossary works best when it does more than define the word. Use four columns:

| Word | Simple meaning | Example sentence | My language | |---|---|---|---| | evaporation | water changes into a gas | Evaporation happens when the sun heats the sea. | | | water vapour | water as a gas, in the air | Water vapour goes up into the sky. | | | condensation | gas changes back into water drops | Condensation makes clouds. | | | precipitation | water that falls from clouds | Rain and snow are precipitation. | | | collection | water goes into seas, rivers and the ground | After rain, collection happens in rivers. | | | cycle | something that goes round and starts again | The water cycle never stops. | |

Leave the last column blank. Students fill it in themselves, using a bilingual dictionary, a family member or a classmate who shares their language. This uses what they already know, gives them a personal reference and works for any language, including ones you don't speak.

Keep the definitions in very simple English, at A2 level, for both groups.

Step 6: Write questions at three levels

Write questions that both groups can attempt, then add extras for B1:

  1. Find it (A2 and B1): "What does the sun heat?" The answer is copied straight from the text.
  2. True or false (A2 and B1): "Water vapour is a liquid. True or false?"
  3. Order it (A2 and B1): Put the four stages in the right order.
  4. Explain it (B1): "Why does water vapour turn into drops when it rises?"
  5. Apply it (B1, stretch): "Where else have you seen condensation?"

Sentence starters help both levels: *"Evaporation is when..."*, *"First... then... after that..."*, *"This happens because..."*

Checklist before you hand it out

  • [ ] Both versions cover the same 3-5 key ideas.
  • [ ] Subject words are kept, bolded and the same in both versions.
  • [ ] A2: short sentences, one idea each, mostly present simple, few pronouns.
  • [ ] B1: connectives (because, so, when), short paragraphs, one or two extra details.
  • [ ] Glossary has 12-15 words, simple definitions, example sentences and a blank home-language column.
  • [ ] Questions run from "find it" to "explain it".
  • [ ] A labelled diagram sits next to the text (very useful for A2 readers).
  • [ ] Both versions look alike: same headings, same layout, so no student looks like they're "on the easy sheet".

Tips for the lesson

  • Pre-teach 4-5 key words before anyone reads. Point to the diagram as you say each one.
  • Pair students across levels for the ordering task, so B1 readers can explain to A2 readers.
  • Let students use their home language to talk about the content first, then report back in English.
  • Re-use the glossary on the next lesson's starter. Seeing a word again is what makes it stick.

Once you've done this for one topic, the same six steps work for volcanoes, the Romans, digestion or any other KS3 text. It's slow the first time and much quicker after a couple of goes.
