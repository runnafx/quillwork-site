How to Test a Used Leatherman Before Reselling It (Checklist)

A 15-minute bench test for used Leatherman multi-tools from auctions, estate sales or thrift stores, covering what to check, what to fix, what to leave alone and how to describe faults honestly in your eBay listing.

Used Leatherman multi-tools often turn up in auction lots, estate boxes and thrift bins. Many work fine. Some have a weak lock, a chipped blade or pliers that don't line up, or turn out to be a different model than the lot said. Buyers who paid for a "working" tool notice these faults within a minute of opening the box.

This checklist takes about 15 minutes per tool. It covers what to look at and in what order, and what to write in your listing. It does not promise your tool will sell or what it will sell for. Price it from your own eBay Sold search.

What you need: good light, a paper towel, a piece of paper, a scrap of insulated copper wire, a few screws (Phillips and flat), a phone camera and a notepad.

Step 1: Confirm it's a genuine Leatherman, and which model

Copies and look-alike tools exist, and auction descriptions get model names wrong.

  • Look for the Leatherman logo stamped into the metal, not printed on. Genuine tools usually also carry a country-of-origin stamp. Check the handles and the pliers' pivot area.
  • Find the model name. Some tools have it stamped on a handle. If yours doesn't, compare the layout with the manufacturer's published photos and tool list for that model: the number and type of blades, outside-accessible blades or not, and the cutter style.
  • Check for retired models. Older and discontinued models are listed under their original names. Search the name with "retired" or "discontinued" to confirm what you have.
  • Note anything that doesn't match. Mismatched screws, a replacement part or a non-Leatherman pouch all need to go in your listing.

If you can't confirm the model, don't put a model name in the title. "Leatherman multi-tool" plus clear photos is more honest than guessing.

Step 2: Do a first look before cleaning

Photograph the tool as received, both sides, open and closed. If a buyer later says you sent the wrong tool, these photos show what you had.

Then check:

  • Rust: light surface spots, or pitting you can feel with a fingernail? Pitting is permanent, so disclose it.
  • Bent or cracked handles: look down the length of each handle. Squeeze the closed tool and listen for creaks or clicks.
  • Engravings: names, initials or company logos are common. They can't be removed, so photograph them.
  • Missing parts: compare against the model's tool list. Count everything, including bit holders and removable bits if the model takes them.

Step 3: Test the pliers and wire cutters

The pliers are the main tool, and they are what buyers test first.

  1. Open and close the pliers 10 times. The motion should be smooth, with no grinding or catching. Some stiffness is normal on a dirty tool.
  2. Check the jaw alignment. Close the jaws and hold them up to light. The tips should meet, and the gripping surfaces shouldn't overlap side to side. Misaligned jaws usually mean the tool was used as a pry bar.
  3. Grip test: clamp a screw head in the needlenose tips and twist. The jaws shouldn't slip or spread.
  4. Wire cutter test: cut the insulated copper wire. It should cut cleanly with a firm squeeze. If it crushes or leaves a strand, look for a chip or a gap in the cutter edges.
  5. Check the pivot: hold one handle and wiggle the other. A little play is normal. A loose, clunky pivot should be noted.

Do not cut hardened steel, piano wire or springs to "test" the cutters. That can chip a good edge and leave you with a fault you have to disclose.

Step 4: Test every blade and implement

Work through each tool one at a time, and tick it off on your notepad.

  • Opening: does it open with normal effort? Does it lie flat against the tool, or is it bent?
  • Lock: many Leatherman implements lock open. Open each locking tool and press gently on the back of the blade. It should not fold. Then release the lock and check it closes normally. A lock that fails or slips is the most important fault to find. Disclose it clearly, or don't sell the tool as working.
  • Blade edge: slice the sheet of paper. Note chips, rolled spots and a "recurve" left by uneven sharpening. Photograph the edge at an angle.
  • Blade tip: broken or reground tips are common. Note them.
  • Serrations: look for flattened or missing teeth.
  • Saw: count the teeth and check none are missing or bent.
  • Files: if the files are dulled or rusted, they won't bite. Run one along a fingernail.
  • Screwdrivers and bits: fit each driver into a matching screw. Rounded or twisted tips should be noted. On bit-driver models, check the bits stay in the holder and come out with normal effort.
  • Can opener, bottle opener, ruler and similar: these rarely fail, but still open each one and check it.

Step 5: Clean it (lightly) and test again

Light cleaning is fine, and it improves photos. Don't make permanent changes.

  • Do: wipe it down, blow grit out of the joints, and use a small amount of light oil on the pivots. Wipe off the extra oil.
  • Do: remove loose surface rust gently with oil and a soft cloth.
  • Avoid: heavy sharpening, grinding, power buffing or taking the tool apart if you don't know how to reassemble and set it. These can create new faults or hide old ones, and some buyers prefer an "unsharpened, original" tool.
  • Avoid: saying it was "professionally sharpened" or "restored" unless that's true.

After cleaning, run Steps 3 and 4 again quickly. Dirt sometimes hides faults, and cleaning sometimes frees up a stiff tool.

Step 6: Grade it and decide how to list

Use plain categories you can defend:

  • Working, light wear: everything opens, locks and cuts. Only cosmetic marks.
  • Working, heavy wear: everything works, but there are scratches, worn finish, resharpened blades or light pitting.
  • Working with faults: one or more items are weak or damaged, such as a chipped blade, a soft lock or a worn bit. List each fault.
  • For parts or not working: a failed lock, broken pliers, a cracked handle or missing major parts. List it under eBay's parts/not-working condition rather than Used.

Don't mention or imply manufacturer warranty coverage in your listing. Coverage depends on the manufacturer's current terms and its own inspection, and you can't promise how they'll handle a claim.

Listing description template

Keep it short and factual. Replace every bracket and delete any line that isn't true.

> Used Leatherman [model, or "multi-tool" if unconfirmed]. [Retired/discontinued model, only if confirmed.] > > Tested by hand: pliers [result], wire cutters [result], blades [opening and locking results], saw/files/drivers [results]. > > Condition: [wear, scratches, rust, engraving, resharpened edges]. Faults: [list each one, or "none found in my testing"]. > > Included: [tool only / sheath]. Not included: box, paperwork, or anything not pictured.

Photos to take: both sides closed, pliers open, each blade open, close-ups of the blade edges, the logo stamp, any engraving, any rust or damage, and every included item.

Common mistakes

  • Writing "tested" when you only opened it. "Tested" should mean you did the steps above.
  • Leaving out a weak lock. Buyers find it quickly, and it can lead to a return.
  • Trusting the lot description for the model name. Confirm it yourself.
  • Over-sharpening before you sell. Removing metal can't be undone, and it can make photos look worse.
  • Pricing from active listings. Use eBay's Sold filter, match the condition and what's included (sheath or no sheath), and leave out parts and bundle listings.

Once this checklist is routine, a box of mixed multi-tools takes an afternoon to sort into tools to list, parts lots and tools not worth the shipping cost.
