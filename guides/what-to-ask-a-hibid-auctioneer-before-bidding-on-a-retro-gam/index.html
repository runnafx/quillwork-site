What to Ask a HiBid Auctioneer Before Bidding on a Retro Game

Five things to confirm with the auction house before you bid on a single game cartridge, with message templates you can copy and a worked max-bid example for a Pokemon GBA cart.

A lot page can show "$0, 0 bids" and still cost you a lot more than the hammer price. Many small auction houses on HiBid don't list their buyer's premium, sales tax or shipping cost on the lot page. Often the photos are also too small to tell an authentic cartridge from a reproduction.

If you plan to resell the game on eBay, each unknown cuts into your margin. This page gives you the questions to ask, copy-and-paste messages for asking them, and a worked example showing how the answers change your maximum bid.

Why one cartridge needs more checks than a big lot

With a bulk lot, a few surprises get spread across many items. With a single cartridge, a 15% premium or a $15 shipping quote can wipe out the whole profit. Popular titles like Pokemon FireRed, Emerald or Ruby also get reproduced a lot, so a fake turns a small profit into a loss you can't easily recover. Most auction houses sell as-is with no returns.

So treat each question below as a gate. If you can't get an answer before the lot closes, don't bid.

The 5 gates to clear before you bid

  1. Buyer's premium. What percentage gets added to the hammer price? Does it change by payment method (some houses add a card fee or offer a cash discount)?
  2. Sales tax. Will tax be charged, and will they accept a resale certificate for your state? The rules depend on where the auction house is and how you collect the item.
  3. Shipping. Do they ship at all, or is it pickup only? If they ship, is it in-house or through a third-party shipper, and what's the quote to your ZIP code? Is there a minimum shipping or handling fee?
  4. Authenticity photos. Can they send clear photos of the label front, the back of the cartridge and, if possible, the circuit board?
  5. Timing and terms. What time zone is the closing time in? Is it a soft close (bidding extends when late bids come in)? When is the checkout or payment deadline, and are any returns accepted?

Message templates (copy, fill in, send)

Use the auction house's contact form or the phone number on the lot page. Keep it short, and ask for everything in one message so you get one reply.

Template 1: Terms and shipping

Hello, I'm interested in lot [LOT NUMBER], "[LOT TITLE]", in your auction closing [DATE]. Before I bid, could you confirm:

  • Your buyer's premium (and whether it differs for card vs. other payment)
  • Whether sales tax applies, and whether you accept a resale certificate from [YOUR STATE]
  • Whether you ship this item, and the shipping cost to ZIP [YOUR ZIP]
  • The time zone of the closing time shown on the lot page

Thank you, [NAME]

Template 2: Photos for authenticity

Hello, thanks for the details on lot [LOT NUMBER]. Could you send close, well-lit photos of:

  • The front label, straight on, with no glare
  • The back of the cartridge, including the screw and any stamped or printed codes
  • The circuit board, if the cartridge can be opened

I understand if you can't open it; the label and back photos are the most helpful. Thank you, [NAME]

Template 3: Short phone script

"Hi, I'm calling about lot [NUMBER] in your online auction closing [DATE]. I have three quick questions: what's your buyer's premium, can you ship to [ZIP] and roughly what would that cost, and is the closing time Eastern, Central, Mountain or Pacific?"

Write the answers down with the date and the name of the person you spoke to. If something goes wrong at checkout, you'll want a record of what you were told.

What to check in the photos

You don't need to be an expert to spot the most common problems on a Pokemon GBA cart. Look for:

  • Label quality. On authentic labels the print is sharp, with even colours and clean edges. Blurry print, pixelation, a glossy sticker look or a label that's misaligned are warning signs.
  • Embossed code on the front. Authentic GBA carts have a small code pressed into the plastic on the front. If it's missing, ask more questions.
  • Back details. Authentic carts have a moulded "Nintendo" mark and printed or stamped codes on the back. A completely plain back, or text that looks wrong, is a red flag.
  • Shell colour and fit. Pokemon carts often come in a coloured shell (FireRed is translucent red). Look for even colour and halves that sit flush.
  • Board, if shown. Authentic boards have a Nintendo marking and properly packaged chips. A board with a black "blob" chip is a strong sign of a reproduction.

If the photos are too poor to judge and the auctioneer can't send better ones, pass. "Authentic" in the lot title is a claim, not proof.

Also note that GBA Pokemon carts from this era save to flash memory, not a battery, though the internal clock battery in Ruby, Sapphire and Emerald can run dry. When the cart arrives, test that it boots, starts a new game and keeps a save after you power off, before you list it.

How the answers change your max bid (worked example)

Here's the formula. Plug in your own numbers, because fees and prices change.

  1. Expected sale price. Pull recent sold prices for loose, cartridge-only copies, and leave out bundles, boxed copies and "untested" listings. Use a conservative figure such as the lower quartile, not the best sale you can find.
  2. Net receipts = sale price − eBay fees − outbound postage and packaging. eBay's final value fee is charged on the total the buyer pays, sales tax included, plus a per-order fee. Check eBay's current fee table for your category.
  3. Max hammer bid = (net receipts − inbound shipping − your profit target) ÷ (1 + buyer's premium).
  4. If tax applies to your purchase, also divide by (1 + tax rate).

Example numbers (illustrative, not a price promise)

These assumptions are only there to show the maths:

  • Fee of 13.6% of (sale + 7% sales tax), plus $0.40 per order
  • $5.75 outbound postage and packaging
  • $10 inbound shipping from the auction house
  • $10 profit target
  • No sales tax on the purchase

If the cart sells for $95:

  • Fees: 0.136 × ($95 × 1.07) + $0.40 = $14.22
  • Net receipts: $95 − $14.22 − $5.75 = $75.03
  • Left for the hammer and premium: $75.03 − $10 − $10 = $55.03

If the cart sells for $110:

  • Net receipts: $87.84
  • Left for the hammer and premium: $67.84

Now divide by the premium:

  • 0% premium: max bid $55.03 at a $95 sale / $67.84 at $110
  • 5% premium: $52.41 / $64.61
  • 10% premium: $50.02 / $61.68
  • 15% premium: $47.85 / $58.99
  • 18% premium: $46.63 / $57.49

The lesson: say a lot opens at $60. That works only if the cart sells near the higher price *and* the premium is low. At a conservative $95 sale it doesn't work at any premium. That's why you ask before you bid instead of finding out at checkout.

Bidding rules worth writing down

  • Set one number and stick to it. On a soft-close auction, late bids extend the clock, so a bidding war can keep going. Decide your max in advance.
  • Watch the time zone. "Monday 08:02 AM" means different things in New Hampshire and California. Confirm it.
  • Plan around checkout. If payment and shipping happen after the auction ends, your resale may land weeks later. Count that time when you decide whether it's worth it.
  • Passing costs nothing. If any gate fails (premium unknown, no shipping quote, unclear photos), skipping the lot is a good result.

Quick checklist

  • [ ] Buyer's premium confirmed in writing
  • [ ] Tax status and resale certificate confirmed
  • [ ] Shipping quote to my ZIP received (or pickup arranged)
  • [ ] Closing time zone and soft-close rules confirmed
  • [ ] Clear label, back and (if possible) board photos received
  • [ ] Sold comps checked: loose cart only, conservative price
  • [ ] Max bid worked out with the formula and written down
  • [ ] Arrival test planned: boot, new game, save, power off, reload
