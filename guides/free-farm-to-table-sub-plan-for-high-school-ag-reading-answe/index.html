Free Farm-to-Table Sub Plan for High School Ag: Reading + Answers

A complete one-period agriculture lesson for grades 9-12 that a non-ag substitute can run with only paper and pencils. Students follow a loaf of bread from wheat field to grocery shelf, and the page includes a timed plan, a reading, questions and an answer key.

Most of your students eat bread every week. Few could tell you how many people and businesses handled it before it reached the store. This lesson walks through that chain one stage at a time. A substitute with no agriculture background can run it from start to finish.

It takes one 45-50 minute period and stays in the classroom: no greenhouse, shop, animals, tools, food samples, video or devices. It fits Intro to Ag, Ag Business, Food Science or any general agriculture course in grades 9-12, and students need no background knowledge. It was written with AI assistance and is not aligned to a specific state standard, so skim it first and adjust it to your course.

Before You Leave

  • Print one copy of the reading and questions per student.
  • Print one answer key for the sub, or keep it for your own grading.
  • Leave your seating chart and attendance list.
  • Write on the sub note: no greenhouse, shop, barn, lab or outdoor work today. Lock those areas or tell the office they are closed.
  • Arrange plant and animal care with a qualified adult. Don't leave it to the substitute unless your school allows it.
  • Tell the sub where finished work goes (a tray, a folder or your desk).

Timed Plan for the Substitute

The substitute can read this section aloud or follow it as written.

  1. 0-5 min: Attendance and warm-up. Write on the board: "Name every person or business that touched your last sandwich before you ate it." Students write their answers on the back of the handout.
  2. 5-10 min: Share. Ask 3-4 students to read their lists. Say: "Today we follow one food, bread, from the field to the store."
  3. 10-25 min: Reading. Students read the reading below on their own or in pairs. Partners may take turns reading paragraphs aloud quietly.
  4. 25-40 min: Questions. Students answer Questions 1-10 in full sentences. Students who finish early do the Challenge question.
  5. 40-45 min: Exit ticket. Students complete the exit ticket at the bottom of the handout. Collect all papers.

If time is left over, students can draw the five stages as a flow chart with an arrow between each one.

Student Reading: From Wheat Field to Bread Shelf

Stage 1: Production (the farm). Bread starts as wheat, a cereal grain. A farmer prepares the soil, plants seed and manages the crop through the growing season, which includes watching for weeds, insects and disease. Some wheat is planted in fall and grows through winter (winter wheat). Other wheat is planted in spring (spring wheat). When the grain is dry and ripe, the farmer harvests it with a combine. This machine cuts the plants and separates the grain kernels from the straw and chaff.

Stage 2: Storage and marketing. After harvest, the grain is usually stored in bins on the farm or hauled to a grain elevator. An elevator is a business that buys, stores and ships grain. There the wheat is weighed and checked for quality, such as moisture and protein content. The farmer can sell it right away or store it and sell later, hoping for a better price. Prices change with supply, demand and weather in other growing regions.

Stage 3: Processing. From the elevator, wheat travels by truck, rail or barge to a flour mill. The mill cleans the grain and grinds it into flour. A wheat kernel has three main parts: the bran (outer layer), the germ (the part that could sprout into a new plant) and the endosperm (the starchy center). White flour is made mostly from the endosperm. Whole wheat flour uses all three parts. The flour then goes to a bakery, which mixes it with water, yeast, salt and other ingredients, then bakes, slices and packages the bread. Processing turns a raw commodity into a finished product, and each step adds value.

Stage 4: Distribution. Bread doesn't keep long, so it has to move quickly. Trucks carry it from the bakery to warehouses or straight to stores. Distribution includes drivers, warehouse workers, schedulers and the people who stock shelves. Fuel, packaging and labor all add to the cost of each loaf.

Stage 5: Retail and the consumer. A grocery store, convenience store or restaurant sells the bread to the consumer. The store sets a price that covers what it paid for the bread plus its own costs, such as rent, staff and electricity, and a profit. The consumer is the last link in the chain. Choices about what to buy send signals back up the chain. If more shoppers buy whole wheat bread, bakeries order more whole wheat flour, and mills need more of the wheat varieties suited to it.

Why it matters. Only part of what you pay for a loaf goes to the farmer who grew the wheat. The rest pays for storage, transport, milling, baking, packaging, distribution and retail. A problem at any stage, such as a drought, a rail delay or a shortage of truck drivers, can affect the price or supply of food far away. Agriculture includes all of these jobs, not only farming.

Questions

  1. List the five stages of the supply chain in order.
  2. What does a combine do?
  3. What is a grain elevator, and what happens to wheat there?
  4. Name the three main parts of a wheat kernel.
  5. How is white flour different from whole wheat flour?
  6. What does "adding value" mean? Give one example from the reading.
  7. Why does bread have to move quickly through distribution?
  8. Name three costs, besides the wheat itself, that are included in the price of a loaf.
  9. How can consumer choices affect a wheat farmer?
  10. Pick one problem that could happen at one stage. Explain how it could affect the price or supply of bread.

Challenge: Pick another food you ate this week, such as milk, a hamburger or an apple. List the five supply chain stages for that food, with one job at each stage.

Exit ticket: Name one agricultural career from today's reading that is *not* a farmer, and describe what that person does in one sentence.

Answer Key

Accept reasonable answers in students' own words.

  1. Production (farm), storage and marketing, processing, distribution, retail and consumer.
  2. It harvests the crop by cutting the plants and separating the grain kernels from the straw and chaff.
  3. A business that buys, stores and ships grain. The wheat is weighed and checked for quality, such as moisture and protein.
  4. Bran, germ and endosperm.
  5. White flour is made mostly from the endosperm. Whole wheat flour uses the bran, germ and endosperm.
  6. Turning a raw product into something worth more by processing it. Examples: milling wheat into flour, or baking flour into packaged bread.
  7. Bread doesn't keep long, so it must reach stores and consumers before it goes stale.
  8. Any three: storage, transport or fuel, milling, baking labor, packaging, warehouse work, store rent, staff, electricity, store profit.
  9. When consumers buy more of one product, demand travels back up the chain through stores, bakeries and mills. That changes what wheat buyers want and can affect what farmers plant and the price they get.
  10. Sample: A drought cuts the wheat harvest, so there is less grain. Mills pay more for it, and bread prices may rise. Also accept rail or truck delays, driver shortages, fuel price increases or mill problems, as long as the effect is explained.

Challenge (sample, milk): Production: dairy farmer milks cows. Storage/marketing: milk hauler collects milk in a refrigerated tank truck. Processing: dairy plant pasteurizes and bottles it. Distribution: refrigerated truck driver delivers it to stores. Retail: grocery clerk stocks the dairy case.

Exit ticket: Any non-farmer role described correctly, such as grain elevator operator, grain inspector, miller, baker, truck driver, warehouse worker or grocery manager.

Grading Suggestion

  • Questions 1-10: 2 points each (20 points). Give 1 point for partly correct answers.
  • Exit ticket: 2 points.
  • Challenge: extra credit, up to 3 points.

Students who are absent, at a CDE or at a show can do the same handout as make-up work. It needs nothing that isn't on the page.

Tips for Making It Work

  • Tape the timed plan to the top of the stack. Substitutes follow what they see first.
  • Name two reliable students on the sub note who can point out where materials and the work tray are.
  • Leave a back-up. If a class finishes early, the flow chart drawing or the Challenge question will fill the rest of the period.
  • Change the food to fit your program. If your area grows corn, soybeans, rice or cotton, change the Challenge question so students trace a local product.
