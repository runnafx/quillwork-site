Free KS3 Networks Cover Lesson: Packet Passing, No Tech + Answers

A complete one-hour KS3 lesson on how data travels across the internet in packets. It needs no computers, and a non-specialist cover supervisor can run it from the script and answer key.

Networks are hard to set as cover work. Most resources rely on slides, videos or online simulators, and those tend to fail when the computing teacher is away. This lesson needs only paper, pens and the slips you print or copy out below. It suits Years 7 to 9. Where a Year 7 extension differs, it is noted.

Learning objective: Explain how a message is split into packets, sent across a network by routers and put back together in the right order.

You will need:

  • One set of 5 packet slips for every group of 4 to 6 students. Copy them out from the "Packet slips" section.
  • Lined paper for the written tasks.
  • This page, so the supervisor has the script and answers.

Lesson at a glance (60 minutes)

  1. Starter: what happens when you send a message? (8 min)
  2. Key ideas read-aloud (7 min)
  3. Packet-passing activity (20 min)
  4. Network diagram task (10 min)
  5. Written questions (12 min)
  6. Plenary: exit question (3 min)

Supervisor script

You do not need any computing knowledge. Read the bold lines aloud. Everything students need to know is in the "Key ideas" section.

Starter (8 min). Write on the board: "When you send a photo to a friend, does it travel as one piece?" Say: "Write one sentence with your guess. There is no wrong answer yet. We'll find out today." Take two or three answers. Do not correct them yet.

Key ideas (7 min). Read the "Key ideas" section aloud, or ask a confident reader to do it. Then copy the four bold keywords onto the board: packet, header, router, reassemble.

Packet-passing activity (20 min). Give each group one set of 5 shuffled slips. Say: "Your group is the internet. One person is the Sender, one is the Receiver and everyone else is a Router. The Sender hands packets to Routers one at a time. Routers pass them along, and the packets can go by different people. The Receiver has to rebuild the message using the packet numbers."

If students cannot move around, run it at the desks. Packets are passed along the row and can go left or right.

After round 1, run round 2 with a twist. Say: "Router, secretly keep one packet in your pocket." The Receiver has to say which packet number is missing and ask the Sender to send it again. This is how real networks deal with lost data.

Diagram task (10 min). Students copy the instructions from the "Diagram task" section and draw the diagram in their books.

Written questions (12 min). Students answer questions 1 to 8 in full sentences. Anyone who finishes early does the extension.

Plenary (3 min). Say: "On a scrap of paper, finish this sentence: A packet needs a number because..." Collect the slips as students leave and pass them to the class teacher.

Key ideas (read aloud)

When you send a message, photo or video over the internet, it is not sent as one big piece. It is split into small chunks called packets.

Each packet carries part of the data plus a header. The header is like the label on a parcel. It holds:

  • the address the packet is going to (the destination)
  • the address it came from (the source)
  • a packet number, such as "packet 3 of 5"

Packets travel through devices called routers. A router reads the destination address and sends the packet on to the next step towards it. Packets from the same message can take different routes, so they may arrive in the wrong order.

The receiving computer uses the packet numbers to reassemble the message in the right order. If a packet goes missing, the receiver can tell from the numbers and asks for that packet to be sent again.

Splitting data into packets means one lost piece does not ruin the whole message. It also means lots of people can share the same network at once.

Packet slips (copy or print one set per group)

Every slip has the same header details:

  • To: Receiver
  • From: Sender

Only the packet number and the data change from slip to slip:

  • Packet 1 of 5 | Data: CODE
  • Packet 2 of 5 | Data: CLUB
  • Packet 3 of 5 | Data: MEETS
  • Packet 4 of 5 | Data: ON
  • Packet 5 of 5 | Data: FRIDAY

Shuffle the slips before handing them out. The Receiver should end up with the message "CODE CLUB MEETS ON FRIDAY".

Optional second message for groups who finish quickly. Make 4 new slips:

  • 1 of 4: BRING
  • 2 of 4: A
  • 3 of 4: PACKED
  • 4 of 4: LUNCH

Diagram task

Copy these instructions onto the board:

  1. Draw a computer on the left and label it Sender. Draw a computer on the right and label it Receiver.
  2. Draw four boxes in between, labelled Router A, Router B, Router C and Router D.
  3. Join them with lines so that there are at least two different routes from Sender to Receiver.
  4. Use two different colours to show packet 1 and packet 2 taking different routes.
  5. Write one sentence under the diagram explaining why the packets might arrive in a different order.

Written questions

  1. What is a packet?
  2. Name three things found in a packet's header.
  3. What job does a router do?
  4. Why do packets from the same message sometimes arrive in the wrong order?
  5. How does the receiving computer put the message back in the right order?
  6. Packets arrive in this order: 4, 1, 5, 2, 3. The data is ON, CODE, FRIDAY, CLUB, MEETS. Write out the original message.
  7. In round 2 of the activity, a packet went missing. How did the Receiver know, and what did they do?
  8. Give two reasons why sending data in packets is better than sending it as one big piece.

Extension (Years 8 and 9): A video call is made of thousands of packets every second. Suggest why a video call sometimes freezes or goes blocky for a moment, even though the call does not end.

Extension (Year 7): Write your own 4-packet message. Draw the 4 slips with full headers.

Answer key

  1. A packet is a small chunk of data sent across a network. It includes a header with information about where it is going.
  2. Any three of: destination address, source address, packet number (sequence number). Some students may also say "total number of packets", which is fine for KS3.
  3. A router reads the packet's destination address and forwards the packet towards it, choosing the next step on the route.
  4. Packets can take different routes through different routers. Some routes are slower or busier than others, so the packets do not all arrive at the same time.
  5. It uses the packet numbers in the headers to put the packets in order: 1, 2, 3 and so on.
  6. CODE CLUB MEETS ON FRIDAY. (Packet 1 = CODE, 2 = CLUB, 3 = MEETS, 4 = ON, 5 = FRIDAY.)
  7. A number was missing from the sequence. For example, they had 1, 2, 4 and 5 but no 3. The Receiver asked the Sender to send that packet again.
  8. Any two of:
  • If one packet is lost, only that packet has to be resent, not the whole file.
  • Packets can take different routes, so traffic can go around busy or broken parts of the network.
  • Many users can share the network at the same time because no single message blocks the line.
  • Small packets are easier for routers to handle.

Extension (Years 8 and 9), suggested answer: Some packets arrive late or are lost. A live call cannot wait long for them to be resent, so for a moment parts of the picture are missing or out of date. The call continues because most packets still arrive.

Plenary, model answer: "A packet needs a number because packets can arrive in the wrong order, and the number lets the receiver put them back together and spot any that are missing."

Tips for the cover supervisor

  • If the room is noisy, run the activity at the desks. Packets pass along rows and only the Receiver may speak.
  • If a group finishes in 5 minutes, give them the second message, or let one Router "lose" two packets.
  • If students ask something you can't answer, write the question down for the class teacher. That is a perfectly good response.
  • Evidence of learning: the diagram, written answers 1 to 8 and the plenary slips. Leave them in a pile marked with the class name.

For heads of department: making this work as emergency cover

  • Print the slips and this page, then store them in a labelled wallet in the cover folder. The lesson then needs no preparation on the day.
  • The lesson is self-contained, so it can go anywhere in a networks unit. It works as an introduction or as consolidation.
  • If a later lesson covers protocols, use question 7 to introduce the idea that agreed rules (protocols) control how lost packets get resent.
