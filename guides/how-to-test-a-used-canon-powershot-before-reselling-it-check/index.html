How to Test a Used Canon PowerShot Before Reselling It (Checklist)

A 15-minute test routine for used Canon PowerShot and ELPH digicams (SD790 IS, SD890 IS, A520, A60, A95 and similar) to run before you list one as "tested working" on eBay. It covers what to have ready, every check in order, how to grade each camera and what to put in the listing.

Older Canon PowerShot and Digital ELPH cameras are popular again as "Y2K digicams." Buyers search for terms like "tested working," and they pay more when you prove a camera works than when you write "untested." To make that claim honestly you need a routine that catches the common faults before a buyer finds them.

This checklist is meant for resellers who bought one camera or a mixed lot. It works for most Canon compacts from the early 2000s through the early 2010s. The examples use models that often turn up together in lots: SD790 IS, SD890 IS, A520, A60, A95, S45 and S500.

Before you start: build a test kit

You will test faster, and get fewer false failures, if these are on the bench first.

  • The right batteries. Look up each model before you assume. For example, the SD790 IS takes an NB-4L and the SD890 IS takes an NB-5L. The S45 uses an NB-2L, the S500 an NB-1LH, and the A-series (A520, A60, A95) run on AA cells. Keep one known-good battery and charger for each type you test regularly.
  • Good AA cells. Use charged NiMH or brand-new alkaline. Weak AAs cause false "change the batteries" shutdowns and slow, struggling lens movement, and that can look like a dead camera.
  • Small, old-format memory cards. Many older Canons do not read SDHC. Keep a 1–2 GB standard SD card. Some older models (for example the A60, A95 and S45) take CompactFlash, so keep a small CF card too.
  • A card reader and a computer. You will check the photos at full size. The camera's LCD is too small to show some faults.
  • A blower, a microfiber cloth and a cotton swab. These are for cleaning lenses and battery contacts. Do not spray liquid into the camera.
  • A printed test card for each camera (template below).

Step 1: Look it over before powering on (2 minutes)

  1. Check the lens barrel. Look for dents, grit or a barrel that sits crooked or partly extended while the camera is off. Any of these suggests the camera has been dropped or the lens is jammed.
  2. Open the battery door and card door. Make sure each latch clicks shut and stays shut. A broken battery door is a common flaw on AA-powered A-series bodies, and buyers will ask about it.
  3. Look in the battery compartment for green or white corrosion. Clean light corrosion off the contacts with a dry swab or a pencil eraser. Heavy corrosion is a red flag.
  4. Look at the LCD with the camera off for cracks, pressure marks and deep scratches.
  5. Check the strap lug, tripod socket and USB/AV port covers. Note anything missing.

Step 2: Power-on and lens test (2 minutes)

  1. Insert the battery and card, then power on. The lens should extend smoothly within a few seconds.
  2. If you see "E18," "Lens error" or a lens that starts out and then retracts with an error, stop. That is a lens or zoom failure. Some people try power-cycling a few times with fresh batteries, but grade the camera as not working unless it then extends and works every time.
  3. Zoom fully in and fully out three times. Listen for grinding and watch for hesitation.
  4. Power off. The lens should retract completely and the lens cover (on ELPH models with a built-in cover) should close fully.

Step 3: Shooting test (5 minutes)

Take each of these shots on the test card, then check them on the computer.

  1. Outdoor or bright-window shot at wide angle. This checks basic exposure and color.
  2. The same scene at full zoom. This checks focus at the long end.
  3. A close-up in macro mode (the flower icon) of printed text about 10 cm away. This checks close focus.
  4. An indoor shot with the flash forced on. Confirm the flash fires and the exposure looks normal.
  5. A dim indoor shot without flash. On IS models such as the SD790 IS and SD890 IS, this also gives a rough check that image stabilization is doing its job. The shot should be reasonably sharp, not badly smeared.
  6. A short video clip with sound. Speak during it, then play it back to confirm the microphone and speaker work.
  7. A photo of a plain white wall or sheet of paper, slightly out of focus. At full size this shows dust spots, haze and dead or stuck pixels.

Step 4: Check the files at full size (3 minutes)

Open each image on the computer at 100% and look for:

  • A purple tint, purple bands, garbled color blocks or all-black frames. These are symptoms of the CCD sensor failure that Canon put out a service advisory for in the mid-2000s. Models on that advisory include the A60, A95 and S500, among others. The free-repair program is long over, so a camera showing these symptoms is a parts camera. Check every camera from that era, even if its LCD preview looked fine.
  • Soft corners or one blurry side. This suggests a knocked or decentered lens.
  • Haze or low contrast in every shot. This can mean fungus or film inside the lens, which you cannot fix with cleaning.
  • Dark spots in the same place on every frame. This is dust inside the lens or on the sensor. Disclose it.
  • Single bright or colored pixels. A few stuck pixels are common on old sensors. Mention them if you can see them at normal viewing size.

Step 5: Controls, screen and playback (2 minutes)

  1. Press every button and turn every dial or switch. Check that each one does what its label says.
  2. In playback, scroll through the images, zoom in on one, delete one and check the camera asks you to confirm.
  3. On a solid-color menu screen, look for lines, dead areas or flickering on the LCD. If the camera has an optical viewfinder, look through it.
  4. Format the card in the camera. A camera that cannot format a known-good card has a card-slot fault.
  5. Leave the camera powered on for about five minutes. Some faults, including some sensor problems, only show up once the camera warms up.

Step 6: Grade each camera honestly

Put every camera in one of these four grades and use the same words in your listing.

  • Tested working: passed every step above. List the cosmetic wear separately.
  • Working with flaws: takes good photos but has a stated problem, such as a broken battery-door latch, a scratched LCD, a few stuck pixels or a dead flash. Name the flaw in the title or the first line of the description.
  • Partially working: powers on but has a problem that affects image quality or use, such as an intermittent lens error or visible haze. Price it much lower and describe it plainly.
  • For parts / not working: E18 that won't clear, CCD purple/black images, no power with a known-good battery, or heavy corrosion. List it as For parts or not working. A lot of parts cameras can still sell, but never sell one as working.

Your listing should only claim what you actually tested. "Tested: power, zoom, autofocus, flash, photos, video and playback all work" is clearer and safer than "works perfectly."

What to put in the listing

  • Title: model name, "tested working" (only if it passed) and what's included, for example "Canon PowerShot SD890 IS Digital Camera, Tested Working, Battery + Charger."
  • Photos: a few full-size sample images from your test (the white-wall and outdoor shots are good choices), plus clear photos of the lens, screen, battery door and any wear.
  • Description: the test steps you ran, the grade, every known flaw and exactly what's in the box. If you don't include a charger or card, say so plainly. Buyers of these cameras often assume one is included.

Quick troubleshooting before you give up on a camera

  • Won't power on: try a second known-good battery, clean the contacts, and check the battery door is fully latched. Many cameras won't power on if the door switch isn't pressed.
  • "Memory card error": try a smaller standard SD card instead of SDHC, or reseat the CF card. Then format the card in the camera.
  • "Change the batteries" on an A-series camera: swap to freshly charged NiMH before you decide it's a fault.
  • Lens stuck partly out: power-cycle with fresh batteries. If it still shows a lens error, grade it as not working. Don't force the barrel by hand.

Running the same routine on every camera means every "tested working" label is backed by a filled-in test card, which leads to fewer returns.
