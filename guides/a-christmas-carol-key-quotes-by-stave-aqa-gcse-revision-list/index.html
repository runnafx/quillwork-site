A Christmas Carol Key Quotes by Stave: AQA GCSE Revision List

40 key quotations from A Christmas Carol, sorted stave by stave and tagged by theme, each with a short analysis prompt. A free list for teachers and Year 10/11 students revising for AQA GCSE English Literature Paper 1, Section B.

In the A Christmas Carol question, students get a printed extract and must also write about the novella as a whole. The extract half is the easier half, because the quotations are on the page in front of them. The whole-text half needs quotations they have learned. Students who only learn "Bah! Humbug!" end up with a thin second half.

This page sorts key quotations stave by stave, with theme tags and a one-line analysis prompt for each. You can project it, print it, or cut it into retrieval cards. At the bottom are three short drills that turn the list into usable whole-text evidence for mocks.

A note on wording: these come from the 1843 text, which is in the public domain. Punctuation and capital letters vary a little between editions, so check them against the edition your class uses before students learn them word for word.

Theme tags used below: greed, poverty and social responsibility, redemption, family, Christmas spirit, isolation.

Stave One: Marley's Ghost

  1. "a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!" *Greed.* A list of seven -ing verbs and adjectives. Ask what the build-up of harsh, grabbing words makes the reader feel before Scrooge has said a word.
  2. "Hard and sharp as flint... solitary as an oyster." *Isolation, greed.* Two similes. Ask: is anything hidden inside the oyster? It hints that Scrooge can change.
  3. "The cold within him froze his old features" *Isolation.* The weather comes from inside him. Link this to how the weather changes in Stave Five.
  4. "darkness is cheap, and Scrooge liked it." *Greed.* A short, dry sentence. Scrooge sees everything in terms of cost, even light.
  5. "Bah!" said Scrooge, "Humbug!" *Christmas spirit.* His catchphrase dismisses joy as a fraud. Track when he stops saying it: he breaks off at "Hum" in Stave One and doesn't finish it.
  6. "open their shut-up hearts freely" (Fred) *Christmas spirit, family.* Fred's speech gives the novella's moral early. Look at "shut-up hearts" next to Scrooge's oyster.
  7. "fellow-passengers to the grave" (Fred) *Social responsibility.* Everyone shares the same ending, so nobody is "another race of creatures".
  8. "Are there no prisons?... And the Union workhouses?" *Poverty.* Rhetorical questions that repeat the harsh views of his time. Remember them, because they come back in Stave Three.
  9. "decrease the surplus population." *Poverty, greed.* Malthusian language. The cold, statistical noun phrase turns people into a number.
  10. "I wear the chain I forged in life" (Marley) *Redemption, greed.* Metaphor: you make your own punishment. "Forged" suggests labour and choice.
  11. "Mankind was my business." (Marley) *Social responsibility.* Dickens takes the word "business" back from money and gives it a moral meaning.

Stave Two: The First of the Three Spirits

  1. "like a child: yet not so like a child as like an old man" *Redemption.* The Ghost of Christmas Past is a paradox. Memory belongs to both youth and age.
  2. "a bright clear jet of light" *Redemption.* Light imagery (truth, revelation) against Scrooge's love of darkness.
  3. "A solitary child, neglected by his friends, is left there still." *Isolation, family.* The word "solitary" echoes Stave One. Ask: what does this suggest about where Scrooge's coldness came from?
  4. "He has the power to render us happy or unhappy" (Scrooge, about Fezziwig) *Social responsibility.* Scrooge praises a good employer without meaning to. Set this beside how he treats Bob Cratchit.
  5. "Another idol has displaced me... a golden one." (Belle) *Greed, family.* Religious language: money has become a false god that replaced love.
  6. "Haunt me no longer!" *Redemption.* Scrooge can't bear the truth yet. Change is painful.

Stave Three: The Second of the Three Spirits

  1. "a jolly Giant, glorious to see" *Christmas spirit.* Alliteration and size. Generosity is presented as abundant and larger than life.
  2. "Its capacious breast was bare" *Christmas spirit.* Openness, nothing hidden. The opposite of the "secret, and self-contained" Scrooge.
  3. "God bless us every one!" (Tiny Tim) *Family, Christmas spirit.* An inclusive blessing from the most vulnerable character. Note "every one".
  4. "If these shadows remain unaltered by the Future, the child will die." *Poverty, redemption.* A conditional sentence. Tim's fate depends on whether Scrooge changes.
  5. "Will you decide what men shall live, what men shall die?" *Poverty, redemption.* The Ghost turns Scrooge's "surplus population" back on him.
  6. "more worthless and less fit to live than millions like this poor man's child." *Social responsibility.* Dickens reverses the idea that the poor are the worthless ones.
  7. "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want." *Poverty, social responsibility.* Allegory: the children stand for society's neglect.
  8. "most of all beware this boy" *Social responsibility.* Dickens singles out lack of education as the greater danger.
  9. "Spirit, are they yours?" *Social responsibility.* Scrooge asks the right question. The answer implies they belong to all of mankind.
  10. "Are there no prisons?" said the Spirit, turning on him for the last time with his own words. *Poverty, redemption.* Cyclical structure: Stave One's cruelty comes back as a punishment.

Stave Four: The Last of the Spirits

  1. "The Phantom slowly, gravely, silently, approached." *Redemption.* Three adverbs slow the pace and build dread. This ghost never speaks.
  2. "left nothing of it visible save one outstretched hand" *Redemption.* The future is unknown, but it points somewhere. Ask what the hand directs Scrooge towards.
  3. "I fear you more than any spectre I have seen." *Redemption.* Fear of death becomes the push to change.
  4. "He frightened every one away from him when he was alive, to profit us when he was dead!" *Greed, isolation.* The people who steal from the dead man's room describe the result of his greed.
  5. "I am not the man I was." *Redemption.* A short declarative sentence that marks the turning point.
  6. "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year." *Christmas spirit, redemption.* Christmas becomes a year-round moral commitment, not one day.

Stave Five: The End of It

  1. "I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel, I am as merry as a school-boy." *Redemption.* A cluster of similes. The repeated "I am" mirrors the list in Stave One, but now it is joyful.
  2. "I'll raise your salary" *Social responsibility.* A concrete action, not just a feeling. Dickens shows change in practical terms.
  3. "to Tiny Tim, who did NOT die, he was a second father" *Family, redemption.* The capitals (in many editions) stress the "shadow" from Stave Three being undone.
  4. "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man" *Redemption.* A tricolon showing him change in his friendships, as an employer and as a person.
  5. "his own heart laughed: and that was quite enough for him." *Redemption, isolation.* He no longer needs others' approval. His heart is open now.
  6. "he knew how to keep Christmas well" *Christmas spirit.* The final verdict, which links back to the promise in Stave Four.
  7. "God bless Us, Every One!" *Family.* The closing line returns to Tim's blessing and completes the circular structure.

Three drills that make the list useful

Drill 1: The "elsewhere pair"

For each theme, students choose one early quotation and one late quotation, for example 1 and 34 for greed, or 8 and 35 for social responsibility. In the whole-text half of the answer, an early/late pair lets them show change across the novella. That is a structural point, not just a list of examples.

Prompt: "In Stave One Dickens shows ______ through ______, but by Stave ______ this has changed to ______, which suggests ______."

Drill 2: Ten-word retrieval

Give students only the stave and the theme, for example "Stave Three, poverty". They have 60 seconds to write a quotation of ten words or fewer from memory, plus one technique. Short quotations are easier to remember under exam pressure, and easier to embed in a sentence.

Drill 3: Echo hunt

Many quotations deliberately echo each other. Ask students to find the pair and explain why Dickens repeats the idea:

  • Scrooge's prison/workhouse questions (8) and the Spirit's return of them (27)
  • "surplus population" (9) and "what men shall live" (22)
  • the Stave One list (1) and the Stave Five similes (34)
  • the "solitary" oyster (2) and the "solitary child" (14)
  • "Bah! Humbug!" (5) and "keep Christmas well" (39)

Echoes like these give the strongest whole-text material, because they show how the novella is built and not only what happens in it.

Using this before mocks

  • Ask students to learn 10 quotations well rather than recognise 40 vaguely. Two per theme is a good target.
  • Check that each learned quotation has a technique and a "why" attached. A quotation with no analysis earns little.
  • Remind students that the extract will supply quotations for one half of the answer. This list is for the "novel as a whole" half.
  • Before your mock, check the current AQA specification and mark scheme for the question format, marks and assessment objectives.
