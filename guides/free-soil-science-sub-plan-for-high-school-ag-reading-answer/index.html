Free Soil Science Sub Plan for High School Ag: Reading + Answers

This is a one-period soil science lesson for grades 9-12 agriculture classes that any substitute can run. It needs no lab, no tools and no prep, and it includes a reading passage, questions, a timed plan and a full answer key.

If you are the only ag teacher in your building, you know what happens when you are out. The substitute can't run the greenhouse, the shop or the animal lab, and they probably don't know much about soil. This page gives you a whole class period on soil basics that works with nothing but paper and pencils. Print it, leave it on your desk, and the period is covered.

It works for Intro to Ag, Agriscience, Plant Science or Natural Resources. It takes 45-55 minutes and fits grades 9-12.

What to Leave for the Substitute

  • One copy of the reading passage and questions per student. Students can share in pairs if you are short on copies.
  • One copy of the answer key, for the sub or for you to grade later.
  • Your seating chart and attendance list.
  • A note that says: "Today's class is a written lesson only. Students do not go to the greenhouse, shop, barn or outside, and they do not use any tools, equipment or chemicals."

Timed Plan the Sub Can Read Aloud

  1. 0-5 minutes: Attendance and start-up. Take attendance. Hand out the reading. Say: "Today you are learning what soil is made of and how soil layers form. You will read a short passage and answer questions on your own. This is graded."
  2. 5-10 minutes: Warm-up. Write this on the board: "Write down three things you think soil is made of." Give students 3 minutes, then ask two or three of them to share.
  3. 10-25 minutes: Reading. Students read the passage silently, or the sub picks volunteers to read paragraphs aloud.
  4. 25-45 minutes: Questions. Students answer Questions 1-10 on their own, in complete sentences where it asks for them.
  5. 45-50 minutes: Exit ticket and collect. Students answer the exit ticket at the bottom of their paper. The sub collects every paper and leaves them in a stack on the teacher's desk.

If students finish early: give them the Early Finisher task at the end of this page.

Student Reading: What Is Soil?

Soil is more than dirt. Farmers, ranchers and gardeners all depend on soil. It holds plant roots in place and stores water and nutrients. It is also home to billions of living organisms. A healthy soil is a mix of four parts: mineral particles, organic matter, water and air.

Mineral particles come from rock that has broken down over a very long time. This breakdown is called weathering. Freezing and thawing, rain, wind, plant roots and chemical reactions all slowly break rock into smaller and smaller pieces.

Mineral particles come in three sizes. Sand particles are the largest. They feel gritty, and water drains through them quickly. Silt particles are medium-sized and feel smooth, like flour. Clay particles are the smallest. They feel sticky when wet and hold water and nutrients very well, but they can drain slowly and pack down hard. The mix of sand, silt and clay in a soil is called its soil texture. A soil with a good balance of all three is called a loam. Many growers like loam because it holds enough water for plants and still drains well.

Organic matter is material that was once alive, such as dead leaves, roots, manure and decomposed organisms. When it is fully broken down it is called humus, which is dark and crumbly. Organic matter feeds soil organisms, helps soil hold water and supplies nutrients to plants. Earthworms, insects, fungi and bacteria help break it down.

Water and air fill the spaces between soil particles, called pore spaces. Roots need both. If a soil is waterlogged, the pores fill with water and roots cannot get the oxygen they need. If a soil is packed down, or compacted, by heavy equipment or foot traffic, there are fewer pore spaces for water and air.

Soil forms in layers called horizons. If you dug a deep hole and looked at the side wall, you would see a soil profile, which is all the layers stacked on top of each other. The main horizons, from top to bottom, are:

  • O horizon: the surface layer of leaves and other organic material.
  • A horizon (topsoil): dark, rich in humus, and where most plant roots grow.
  • E horizon: a lighter layer that is not in every soil. Water has washed (leached) minerals out of it.
  • B horizon (subsoil): where clay and minerals washed down from above build up.
  • C horizon: partly weathered rock.
  • R horizon: solid bedrock.

Topsoil is very valuable because it forms extremely slowly. Wind and water can carry it away, which is called erosion. That is why farmers use practices like cover crops, no-till planting and contour farming to protect it.

Student Questions

  1. Name the four main parts of soil.
  2. What is weathering? Give two things that cause it.
  3. List the three mineral particle sizes from largest to smallest.
  4. Which particle feels sticky when wet? Which feels gritty?
  5. What is a loam, and why do many growers like it?
  6. What is humus?
  7. Explain why roots in a waterlogged soil can struggle. Use complete sentences.
  8. Which horizon is called topsoil, and why is it important for crops?
  9. What is erosion? Name one farming practice from the reading that helps prevent it.
  10. A farmer's field has a hard, packed area where tractors always turn. Explain what has happened to the soil and how it could affect plants. Use complete sentences.

Exit ticket: In one sentence, explain why soil is "more than dirt."

Answer Key

  1. Mineral particles, organic matter, water and air.
  2. Weathering is rock slowly breaking down into smaller pieces. Any two of: freezing and thawing, rain, wind, plant roots, chemical reactions.
  3. Sand, silt, clay.
  4. Clay feels sticky when wet. Sand feels gritty.
  5. A loam is a soil with a good balance of sand, silt and clay. Growers like it because it holds enough water for plants and still drains well.
  6. Humus is fully decomposed organic matter. It is dark and crumbly.
  7. In a waterlogged soil, water fills the pore spaces, so there is no room for air. Roots need oxygen, so they struggle to get what they need.
  8. The A horizon. It is rich in humus and is where most plant roots grow, so it supplies the water and nutrients crops depend on.
  9. Erosion is wind or water carrying soil away. Any one of: cover crops, no-till planting, contour farming.
  10. The soil has become compacted. Its particles were pressed together, so there are fewer pore spaces. Water and air can't move through the soil as easily, so roots may struggle to grow and plants may get less water and oxygen.

Exit ticket (sample answer): Soil is a living mix of minerals, organic matter, water and air that holds plants up and feeds them.

Suggested grading: 1 point each for Questions 1-6, 8 and 9, and 2 points each for Questions 7 and 10. That makes 12 points, plus the exit ticket as completion credit.

Early Finisher Task

On the back of your paper, draw and label a soil profile showing the O, A, B, C and R horizons, in order from top to bottom. Next to each layer, write one short phrase describing it. If you want a challenge, add an E horizon in the right place and explain in one sentence why it is lighter in color.

Tips for Making This Plan Sub-Proof

  • Put the safety note at the top of the first page. A sub who doesn't know your room may assume students can go to the greenhouse or shop like usual.
  • Collect work in a single stack. Ask the sub to write absent students' names on a sticky note so you can follow up.
  • Keep one copy in a labeled folder in the main office. If you are out with no warning, someone can still find it.
  • Grade it when you're back. Students take sub work more seriously when they know it counts.
  • Use it as a pre-lesson. If you teach soil texture with a hands-on lab later, this reading gives students the vocabulary first.
