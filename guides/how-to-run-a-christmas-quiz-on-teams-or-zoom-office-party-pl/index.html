How to Run a Christmas Quiz on Teams or Zoom (Office Party Plan)

A step-by-step plan for hosting a remote or hybrid office Christmas quiz on Microsoft Teams or Zoom. It covers timings, team set-up, honest marking, a host checklist and a fill-in answer form.

An online Christmas quiz has different problems from one in a room. People talk over each other, someone's microphone stays on, answers get typed into the main chat where everyone can see them, and marking takes longer than the round did. This plan deals with each of those, so you can run a calm 45 to 60 minute quiz for a remote or hybrid team.

It works on Microsoft Teams, Zoom, Google Meet or anything else with video, a chat and screen sharing. Menus differ between apps and versions, so test the features you plan to use (especially breakout rooms) in a short practice call first.

Step 1: Pick a format that suits your group

There are three ways to run it. Choose one before you write anything else.

  • Solo players, answers by private message. Best for up to about 15 people. Each person sends their answers to the host in a private chat at the end of each round. It's simple and needs no breakout rooms, but you'll have a lot of marking to do.
  • Teams in breakout rooms. Best for 15 to 50 people. Teams of 3 to 6 confer in breakout rooms, and one captain per team sends the answers. It's the most sociable option, but you need breakout rooms set up and tested.
  • Teams in separate group chats. Use this if breakout rooms aren't available or allowed. Each team sets up its own group chat (or a side call) to confer and listens to the host on the main call.

For hybrid parties, where some people are in the office and some at home, put the office people in one or two teams around a single laptop and keep remote people in their own teams. Mixed teams split across locations struggle to confer.

Step 2: Plan the timings

Online rounds take longer than in-person ones because of joining, muting and moving between rooms. These planning figures are a sensible starting point:

  • Welcome and rules: 5 minutes
  • Each 10-question round: about 8 minutes to read, plus 3 minutes to confer and submit
  • Marking and scores: about 3 minutes per round, done while people chat
  • Results and tiebreaker: 5 minutes

That gives these layouts:

  • 30 minutes: welcome, 2 rounds, results
  • 45 minutes: welcome, 3 rounds, results
  • 60 minutes: welcome, 4 rounds, results and tiebreaker

Online attention drops faster than it does in a room. Four rounds is usually the most a remote group will enjoy.

Step 3: Choose rounds that work on a screen

Some round types are much easier online than others.

Rounds that work well:

  • General Christmas knowledge (traditions, history, food)
  • Wordplay and anagrams, shown on a shared slide
  • "Finish the line" from well-known carols, typed on a slide
  • Christmas films and TV, using text questions only
  • "Odd one out" lists shown on a slide

Rounds to avoid or adapt:

  • Music clips. Audio shared over a call is often out of sync, quiet or blocked. If you want a music round, type the lyric instead of playing it.
  • Picture rounds made from images found online. These can raise copyright questions in a work setting. Use text puzzles, or photos you took yourself, instead.
  • Physical scavenger hunts ("Find something red in your house"). They're fun, but they take ages and are hard to score fairly.

Show each question on a slide as well as reading it aloud. Some people will have poor audio, and some may be joining with captions.

Step 4: Make answers secret and marking quick

This is where most online quizzes fall apart. Agree one submission method and stick to it.

Option A: Private chat message. Captains send one message per round in this format:

Team name / Round number

  1. ______
  2. ______
  3. ______

(and so on to 10)

Tell people to write the numbers even if they leave an answer blank, so you can mark line by line.

Option B: A form. Make one short form per round, using whatever form tool your workplace already uses, with a field for team name and ten answer boxes. Some form tools can auto-mark exact answers, but check any auto-marking by hand. A form will reject "Rudolf" when you meant to accept it.

Option C: Swap and mark. At the end of each round, each team sends its answers to the next team on the list. You read out the answers and they mark each other's. This saves the host the most time, but it only works with good-humoured groups.

Whichever you choose, write down the accepted alternatives for each answer before the quiz, such as spelling variants, surname only or a well-known nickname. Decide them in advance so you don't have to rule on them live.

Step 5: Set the call up before anyone joins

Use this checklist 15 minutes before the start:

  • Slides open and tested in screen share, with the answers on separate slides at the end
  • Answer key printed or open on a second device, never on the screen you're sharing
  • Breakout rooms created and named after the teams, if you're using them
  • Team list ready to paste into the chat
  • Notifications turned off on your computer, so a pop-up doesn't reveal the answers
  • Scoreboard ready (a simple spreadsheet works)
  • A co-host chosen to admit latecomers, sort out audio problems and watch the chat
  • Timer ready
  • Prizes decided, such as a voucher, a box of chocolates posted out or bragging rights

A co-host matters more online than in a room. You can't read questions and fix someone's breakout room at the same time.

Step 6: Say the rules out loud

Read something like this at the start (adapt it to your format):

"Welcome to the Christmas quiz! There are [number] rounds of 10 questions. I'll read each question twice and show it on screen. Stay muted while I'm reading, please. At the end of each round you'll have three minutes to agree your answers, and then your captain sends them to me in a private message. Please don't post answers in the main chat. No searching online, phones or smart speakers: it's just more fun without them. My answer is final, but if you think I've got one wrong, message me and I'll check it at the break. The winning team gets [prize]. Any questions? Then let's start."

Asking people to message you with disputes, rather than arguing in front of everyone, keeps the call friendly.

Step 7: Keep it moving during the quiz

  • Mute all while reading, if your app and your role allow it. Otherwise just ask.
  • Read each question twice, then move on. Don't wait for confirmation that everyone has it.
  • Give a "one minute left" warning before the submission deadline.
  • While you mark, let your co-host run a quick bonus question or open the floor for a chat.
  • Share the running scores after every round. Remote players lose interest if they don't know where they stand.
  • Read the answers out round by round and give one sentence of explanation for the surprising ones. That's often the best bit.

Step 8: Settle ties and finish on time

Have two or three "closest number wins" tiebreakers ready, for example the year something happened or the height of a famous tree. Captains type their number into the chat at the same moment when you say "go", which stops anyone copying. Check every tiebreaker answer in a reliable source beforehand.

Finish with the scores, the prize and a thank-you. Ending exactly on time is the best way to get people to come back next year.

Inclusion checks for an office quiz

  • Not everyone celebrates Christmas. Keep the tone light, base questions on general knowledge, and avoid making anyone feel singled out.
  • Check that questions don't rely only on UK or US childhoods if your team is international.
  • Don't write questions about colleagues unless they've agreed to it.
  • Make taking part optional, and give people who'd rather listen an easy role, such as scorekeeper.
  • Provide questions on slides for anyone who's hard of hearing or has bad audio.

Quick printable checklist

  1. Format chosen (solo, breakout rooms or group chats)
  2. Rounds and timings set
  3. Questions checked for a single clear answer, with alternatives written down
  4. Slides made, with answers kept separately
  5. Submission method chosen and tested
  6. Co-host briefed
  7. Practice call done
  8. Prizes sorted
  9. Rules script ready
  10. Tiebreakers checked
