Christmas Truce Cover Lesson for Year 9: No-Tech Plan + Answers

A complete, print-and-go 60-minute KS3 History cover lesson on the 1914 Christmas Truce. It includes a supervisor script, a reading, evidence cards, questions and a full answer key.

You need Year 9 History cover in the last weeks of term, and whoever runs it may not be a historian. This lesson turns a festive topic into proper source work. Copy it into a document, print it and leave it on the desk. It needs no slides, no laptop and no subject knowledge.

Lesson question: Did British and German soldiers really play a football match on Christmas Day 1914?

Fits: Year 9 units on the First World War, life in the trenches, or evidence and interpretations.

You need: One copy of the reading and evidence cards per pair, exercise books or lined paper, and pens.

A note on the cards: They are plain-English summaries written for this lesson, not original quotations. If your department has real letters or diary extracts, swap them in.

Lesson at a Glance (60 Minutes)

  1. 0-5 min: Register. Write the lesson question on the board.
  2. 5-10 min: Starter: what do you already think happened?
  3. 10-20 min: Reading and comprehension.
  4. 20-40 min: Evidence cards and grid.
  5. 40-55 min: Written conclusion.
  6. 55-60 min: Show of hands and collect work.

For a 50-minute lesson, use only cards B, C, D and F, and ask for one paragraph at the end.

Cover Supervisor Script

You can read these lines out word for word.

Start: "Today you are history detectives. Many people have heard that enemy soldiers stopped fighting at Christmas 1914 and played football. Your job is to decide how much of that story we can prove."

Starter: "In your book, write the lesson question. Then write yes, no or not sure, with one sentence explaining why. You have three minutes. Nobody will be marked down for changing their mind later."

Before the cards: "Each card is one piece of evidence. Do not just ask whether it sounds true. Ask who made it, when they made it, and whether it actually proves a match took place."

If asked something you can't answer: "Good question. Write it at the bottom of your page for your teacher."

Ending: "Hands up if you now think a proper match took place. Now hands up if you think soldiers kicked a ball around. Why might those two answers be different?"

Student Reading

The First World War began in the summer of 1914. Many people expected a short war, but by December the armies on the Western Front had dug long lines of trenches facing each other across Belgium and France. The strip of ground between the enemy trenches was called No Man's Land.

Around Christmas Eve and Christmas Day 1914, something unusual happened in parts of the front line. In some places the shooting stopped. Soldiers sang carols, called across to each other and then climbed out of their trenches. They swapped small gifts such as food and tobacco, and some used the pause to bury their dead.

The truce was unofficial. It was not agreed by the governments, and senior commanders did not want friendly contact with the enemy. It also did not happen everywhere: in other parts of the line, fighting went on and men were killed on Christmas Day.

Historians agree that truces took place. The argument is about football. The popular version says Britain played Germany in a proper match. How strong is the evidence?

Comprehension questions

  1. What was No Man's Land?
  2. Give two things soldiers did during the truce.
  3. Give two pieces of evidence from the reading that the truce was not official or universal.
  4. Exactly what are historians still arguing about?

Evidence Cards

Card A: Letters home. Soldiers wrote to their families in the days after Christmas 1914, describing meeting Germans in No Man's Land. Some letters were printed in British local newspapers in early 1915.

Card B: Mentions of a ball. Letters and diaries from late December 1914 and early January 1915 mention men kicking a ball around. They do not give reliable details of an organised Britain versus Germany match.

Card C: The score. A German officer's account says the Germans won a match 3-2. Historians have not found official records that back this up.

Card D: The famous photograph. A photograph of soldiers playing football is often printed alongside the truce story. It really shows soldiers playing, but it was taken in Greece, about 1,600 miles from Flanders, a year later.

Card E: The general's warning. Before Christmas 1914, General Sir Horace Smith-Dorrien, a senior British commander, warned his troops against friendly contact with the enemy.

Card F: A modern advert. In 2014, 100 years later, a British supermarket's Christmas advert showed British and German soldiers meeting and playing football in No Man's Land.

Task 1: Evidence Grid

Draw a table with four columns, and complete one row per card:

  • Card
  • Made at the time, or later?
  • What does it suggest?
  • How useful is it for proving a proper match took place? (High, Medium or Low, with a reason)

Task 2: Written Conclusion

Answer the lesson question in one or two paragraphs. Use this structure:

  • "The evidence shows that..."
  • "For example, Card __ tells us..."
  • "However, we cannot be sure because Card __..."
  • "Overall, I think..."

Support: Complete the frame using only cards B, C and D.

Challenge: Explain why people today might prefer the story of a proper match, even though the evidence for it is weak.

Finish-Early Tasks

  1. Write three questions you would ask a soldier who claimed to have played in the match.
  2. Design a museum label for the Card D photograph that tells visitors the truth about it.
  3. Decide whether "The Christmas Truce Football Match" is a fair title for a display. Suggest a better one and justify it.

Exit Question

On a slip or at the bottom of the page: "What is the difference between evidence that a truce happened and evidence that a match happened?"

Answer Key for Non-Specialists

Comprehension

  1. The ground between the opposing trenches.
  2. Any two: sang carols, called to each other, met in No Man's Land, swapped gifts, buried the dead.
  3. Not official: governments did not agree it, or commanders opposed it. Not universal: it happened only in parts of the line, or fighting and deaths continued elsewhere.
  4. Whether football was played, and especially whether there was a proper Britain versus Germany match.

Evidence grid (reasonable answers; accept others with a sensible reason)

  • Card A: Made at the time. Suggests that meetings really happened. Strong for the truce, but Low to Medium for a match unless a letter describes one.
  • Card B: Made at the time. Suggests informal kickabouts probably took place. Medium for football in general, Low for a proper match.
  • Card C: A personal account. Suggests a match with a score. It looks like the best evidence for a match, but one account without backing is limited, so Medium or Low.
  • Card D: Made at the time, but of a different place and year. Low: it tells us nothing about Christmas 1914. It does show how evidence can be misused.
  • Card E: Made at the time. Suggests that friendly contact was a known possibility and was officially discouraged. Low for a match, but useful for explaining why truces were not official.
  • Card F: Made 100 years later. Low as evidence of what happened, but useful for showing how the story is remembered.

Model conclusion

"The evidence shows that truces really happened at Christmas 1914 and that some soldiers probably kicked a ball around. For example, Card B tells us that letters and diaries from the time mention this. However, we cannot be sure a proper match took place, because Card C is a single account without official backing and the photograph in Card D is from another place and year. Overall, I think informal football is likely, but a proper Britain versus Germany match cannot be proved."

Challenge pointers: It is a hopeful, simple story. It suits Christmas and football culture, and adverts and memorials have kept it in public memory.

Quick marking guide

  • Secure: Separates the truce from the match and uses at least two cards.
  • Developing: Uses one card or simply retells the story.
  • Strong: Comments on when or why evidence was made, and reaches a judgement limited by that evidence.

What the Class Should Take Away

The strongest answer is neither "yes, it happened" nor "no, it's a myth". Historians are confident about the truces and think informal football was likely. A proper international match has not been reliably shown to have happened, and it is reasonable to tell students that evidence could still change the picture.
