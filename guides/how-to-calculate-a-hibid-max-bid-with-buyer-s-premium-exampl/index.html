How to Calculate a HiBid Max Bid With Buyer's Premium (+ Example)

A step-by-step formula for turning an eBay Sold price into a hard maximum bid on a HiBid lot after buyer's premium, sales tax, inbound shipping and eBay fees. Includes a fill-in worksheet and a worked example using a used multi-tool.

On a HiBid lot, the current bid is not your price. Your real cost includes the hammer price, the buyer's premium, any sales tax, and shipping that is often billed after the auction ends. If you bid as high as "a bit under what it sells for on eBay," you can win the lot and still lose money.

This page shows how to work backward from a realistic resale price to a maximum hammer price: the highest bid you can enter and still make the profit you want. Set that number before the lot closes and don't go above it.

What you need before you start

  • A Sold median. Use eBay's Sold filter for the same model in similar condition, and leave out parts-only listings, bundles and new-in-box. Active listings show asking prices, not sale prices. If you can't find at least a handful of recent sales, treat the price as a guess.
  • The lot's buyer's premium. It's usually in the auction terms, shown as a percentage of the hammer price.
  • Whether sales tax applies, and whether it is charged on the hammer price alone or on hammer plus premium.
  • A written shipping figure from the auctioneer, or a confirmed pickup plan. Many HiBid sellers quote shipping only after the auction, so ask before you bid.
  • Your eBay fee rate. Final value fees depend on the category and change over time, so check eBay's current fee page for your category. The example below uses an assumed rate.
  • Your outbound cost: postage plus the mailer, if you offer free shipping.
  • Your minimum profit. Pick a dollar figure that makes the time spent testing, photographing, listing and shipping worth it.

The formula

Max hammer = (Net from eBay sale − minimum profit − inbound shipping) ÷ ((1 + buyer's premium) × (1 + tax rate))

Where:

Net from eBay sale = Sold median − eBay fees − outbound shipping

If the lot isn't taxed, leave out the tax term. If tax is charged on the hammer only and not on the premium, the divisor is slightly different. For small lots the difference is pennies, and using the stricter version above keeps you on the safe side.

Then round down to the auction's bid increment. If the formula gives $8.33 and bids go up in $1 steps, your cap is $8.

Step by step

  1. Find the Sold median. List recent Sold prices for the exact model in comparable condition and take the middle value, not the highest. If condition in the lot photos is unclear, use the lower end.
  2. Work out eBay fees on that price. On many categories eBay charges its percentage on the total the buyer pays, sales tax included, plus a fixed per-order fee. Calculate fees on that total, not just your item price.
  3. Subtract outbound shipping. Include the mailer or box, not only postage.
  4. Subtract your minimum profit.
  5. Subtract inbound shipping. Use the auctioneer's written figure. If you only have your own estimate, label it as an estimate and treat the result as provisional.
  6. Divide by (1 + premium) and (1 + tax). This converts what you can afford to pay all-in into the highest bid you can make.
  7. Round down to the bid increment and write the number down before the lot goes into its closing period.
  8. Check backward. Multiply your cap by the premium and tax, add shipping, and confirm the total still leaves your profit.

Worked example: a used multi-tool lot

These numbers are illustrative assumptions to show the math. They are not a pricing claim for any item. Replace each one with your own figures.

  • Sold median: $45
  • eBay fee: assumed 13.6% + $0.40, charged on the sale plus an assumed 8% buyer sales tax
  • Outbound shipping including mailer: $6
  • Minimum profit: $10
  • Inbound shipping: $12
  • Buyer's premium: 20%
  • Sales tax on the lot: none listed

Step 1-3: Net from the sale

  • Buyer pays $45 × 1.08 = $48.60
  • eBay fee = $48.60 × 0.136 + $0.40 = $7.01
  • Net = $45 − $7.01 − $6 = $31.99

Step 4-5: Money left for the bid itself

  • $31.99 − $10 profit − $12 shipping = $9.99

Step 6: Divide by the premium

  • $9.99 ÷ 1.20 = $8.33

Step 7: Round down

  • Max hammer = $8

Step 8: Check backward

  • $8 hammer + 20% premium = $9.60
  • Plus $12 shipping = $21.60 all-in
  • $31.99 net − $21.60 = $10.39 profit. The cap holds.

If the lot were taxed at 8%, the divisor would be 1.20 × 1.08 = 1.296, and the cap would drop to $9.99 ÷ 1.296 = $7.71, which rounds to $7.

How sensitive the cap is

Using the example's fee and outbound assumptions with a $10 profit target:

| Sold median | Inbound shipping | Premium | Max hammer (before rounding) | |---|---|---|---| | $35 | $12 | 20% | $1.22 (effectively PASS) | | $40 | $12 | 20% | $4.77 | | $45 | $12 | 20% | $8.33 | | $45 | $8 | 20% | $11.66 | | $45 | $12 | 10% | $9.08 | | $49 | $12 | 20% | $11.17 |

Three points stand out:

  • Shipping hits small lots hardest. At a 20% premium, each extra $1 of shipping lowers the cap by about $0.83. On a cheap item, an unknown shipping fee can wipe out the deal.
  • A small drop in the Sold median can end the deal. Using $45 when worn examples actually sell for $35 turns a buy into a pass.
  • The premium matters less than people expect on low-dollar lots. The shipping figure and the Sold median matter more.

Common mistakes

  • Using asking prices instead of Sold prices. Unsold listings tell you what sellers hope for, not what buyers pay.
  • Forgetting the premium is added on top. A $10 hammer at 20% costs $12 before shipping and tax.
  • Assuming shipping will be cheap. In-house auction shipping can include handling or per-box fees. Get it in writing, or use pickup.
  • Ignoring soft close. Many HiBid lots extend when a bid comes in near the end. Your cap is the most you will pay, not a starting point you raise as the clock resets.
  • Bidding on unclear photos. If you can't confirm condition, completeness or authenticity, price it at the worn-condition comps or pass.
  • Leaving out returns. One return on a low-margin flip can cancel the profit, so a higher minimum profit target is a reasonable cushion.

When to pass

Pass, with no cost to you, if any of these apply:

  • You can't find enough recent Sold comps to trust the median.
  • The auctioneer won't give a shipping figure and pickup isn't practical.
  • The current bid is already above your cap.
  • The photos don't show condition well enough to match your comps.

A pass costs nothing. A lot you won above your cap starts at a loss before you've listed it.
