Year 11 Geography Mock Revision Cover Lesson: AQA Paper 1, No Tech

A free 60-minute AQA GCSE Geography Paper 1 revision lesson that a non-specialist can run from one printed page. It includes a script, 20 read-aloud questions with answers, and a 9-mark exam question with a marking guide.

If you are off during mock season, the most useful cover is usually retrieval plus one timed extended answer. This lesson does that for AQA GCSE Geography (8035) Paper 1, Living with the physical environment. It needs no slides, no computers and no Geography knowledge from the person running it.

What to print: this page, once, for the supervisor. Students only need lined paper and a pen, plus a different-coloured pen if they have one.

Two minutes of teacher prep:

  • Cross out any question on a topic your class has not covered.
  • In Section C, students answer two of the three options (coasts, rivers, glacial landscapes). The questions below cover coasts and rivers, so swap in your own if you teach glacial.
  • Write the names of the two tectonic case studies your class learned here: ____________ and ____________.

These are original practice questions, not AQA past paper questions. Please check the answers against the textbook your class uses, because wording varies.

Why this format works for Paper 1

Paper 1 lasts 1 hour 30 minutes and has 88 marks, including 3 for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terminology (SPaG). That gives students about one minute per mark. Section A (natural hazards) is 33 marks, Section B (the living world) is 25, and Section C (physical landscapes) is 30. The questions below follow that spread, and the timed answer gives practice at the pace students will need in the exam.

Supervisor script (60 minutes)

Before students arrive: write this on the board: "Number your page 1 to 20. Pens only. No talking."

  1. 0–5 min: Settle. Say: "Your Geography teacher has set mock revision. You'll answer 20 questions from memory, mark them, then write one exam answer. Close your books."
  2. 5–18 min: Quiz. Read each question below twice, slowly. Leave about 30 seconds before the next one. Say: "If you don't know, leave a gap and move on."
  3. 18–28 min: Mark. Say: "Pick up a different pen." Read out the answers. Students tick what they got right and write in the correct answer for anything they missed. Accept answers that mean the same thing.
  4. 28–32 min: Find the gaps. Say: "Add up your score out of 20. At the bottom, write the three topics you most need to revise."
  5. 32–35 min: Plan. Write the 9-mark question (below) and the names of the two case studies on the board. Say: "Spend three minutes planning. Write a point for each side and a final judgement."
  6. 35–47 min: Timed answer. Say: "Twelve minutes, exam conditions, silence." At 45 minutes say: "Two minutes left. Finish with your overall judgement."
  7. 47–56 min: Self-check. Read out the checklist below. Students tick each thing they did in the different pen, then write one sentence starting "To improve, I will…".
  8. 56–60 min: Collect. Collect every page, with names on them.

If a student says "we haven't done this": say "Write what you remember, then leave a gap. Your teacher wants to see what you still need." Do not help with the answers during the quiz or timed answer.

Leave for the teacher: absences, anyone who did little work, and any question most of the class got wrong.

The 20 questions and answers

Section A: Natural hazards

  1. What is a natural hazard? A natural event that threatens people or could cause damage, injury or loss of life.
  2. At which type of plate margin do plates move apart? Constructive.
  3. What is the name for one plate sinking beneath another? Subduction.
  4. Why do conservative plate margins have earthquakes but no volcanoes? Plates slide past each other, so pressure builds up, but no magma rises to the surface.
  5. Give one primary effect and one secondary effect of an earthquake. Primary: buildings collapse or people are killed. Secondary: fires, tsunami, disease or homelessness.
  6. Name two ways of reducing the risk from a tectonic hazard. Any two: monitoring, prediction, protection, planning.
  7. Is air rising or sinking at the equator, and what pressure does this create? Rising, which creates low pressure.
  8. About what sea temperature is needed for a tropical storm to form? Around 27°C. Accept 26–27°C.
  9. What is the difference between mitigation and adaptation to climate change? Mitigation reduces the causes. Adaptation changes how we live to cope with the effects.

Section B: The living world

  1. What is an ecosystem? A community of living things and their non-living environment, and how they interact.
  2. What is a producer? An organism that makes its own food, for example a plant using photosynthesis.
  3. Name three layers of a tropical rainforest. Any three: emergent, canopy, understorey (under-canopy), shrub layer, forest floor.
  4. Give two causes of deforestation. Any two: logging, commercial farming, subsistence farming, mineral extraction, road building, energy development, settlement or population growth.
  5. Give one way of managing a rainforest sustainably. Any one: selective logging, replanting, ecotourism, conservation education, debt reduction, international agreements.
  6. Give one way a plant is adapted to the environment you studied: hot desert or cold environment. Hot desert: long or wide roots, waxy skin, spines. Cold: low-growing shape, shallow roots, small leaves.

Section C: Physical landscapes in the UK

  1. Name the four processes of erosion. Hydraulic action (hydraulic power), abrasion, attrition, solution.
  2. Describe longshore drift. Swash carries sediment up the beach at an angle, backwash pulls it straight back down, so sediment zigzags along the coast.
  3. Put in order: stack, crack, arch, stump, cave. Crack, cave, arch, stack, stump.
  4. How does an ox-bow lake form? Erosion narrows the neck of a meander, and the river cuts through it, often in a flood. Deposition then seals off the old bend.
  5. Give one hard and one soft engineering method for your river or coast. Rivers, hard: dams, embankments, straightening. Soft: floodplain zoning, planting trees. Coasts, hard: sea wall, groynes, rock armour. Soft: beach nourishment, dune regeneration.

The 9-mark question (write this on the board)

Using named examples, assess how far the effects of a tectonic hazard depend on a country's level of wealth. [9 marks]

Self-check list (read aloud)

  • I named at least one specific example, with a place and date.
  • I gave an actual fact or figure from my example.
  • I wrote about effects, not only the cause of the hazard.
  • I explained why wealth made a difference, using words like "because" or "this meant that".
  • I gave at least one other factor apart from wealth.
  • I finished with a judgement that answers "how far".
  • I used key terms such as primary effect, secondary effect, magnitude or development.
  • I read my answer through for spelling and punctuation.

Marking guide for the teacher

AQA 9-mark questions are marked in three levels of 1–3, 4–6 and 7–9, with SPaG marks awarded separately where the paper shows them. The descriptors below are our own simplified version. For moderated mock grades, use the wording from a recent AQA mark scheme.

  • Level 1 (1–3 marks): Simple points about effects with little or no use of examples. Not much link to wealth.
  • Level 2 (4–6 marks): Clear explanation of how wealth affects outcomes, with some real example detail. The judgement may be missing or only asserted.
  • Level 3 (7–9 marks): Detailed, well-linked argument using specific examples. It weighs wealth against other factors and reaches a supported judgement.

Points students may make:

  • Wealth reduces effects: richer countries can afford earthquake-resistant buildings, monitoring, trained emergency services and insurance.
  • Poorer countries often suffer more: weaker buildings, limited healthcare, poor access to remote areas and reliance on international aid can raise deaths and slow recovery.
  • Other factors matter: magnitude, depth, distance from the epicentre, population density, time of day, secondary hazards and how prepared people are.
  • Wealth can mean higher economic losses: richer countries often have more expensive property and infrastructure to damage, so the cost in money may be higher even if fewer people die.

A commonly taught contrast, if your class used it: the 2010 Chile earthquake (magnitude 8.8, around 500 deaths) and the 2015 Nepal earthquake (magnitude 7.8, around 9,000 deaths). A strong answer notes that the stronger earthquake killed far fewer people, then uses Chile's stronger building rules and emergency response to explain why. It also notes that factors such as Nepal's mountainous terrain, landslides and remote villages complicated the picture, so wealth was important but was not the only cause.

After the lesson

The most useful data is on the students' own pages. Note any question that most of the class missed and start your next lesson by re-teaching it. Ask each student to use their three weak topics as their revision list for the week before the mock.
