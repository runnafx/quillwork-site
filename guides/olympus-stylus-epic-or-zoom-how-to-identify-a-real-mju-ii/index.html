Olympus Stylus Epic or Zoom? How to Identify a Real mju-II

The fixed-lens Olympus Stylus Epic (mju-II) shares its name with cheaper zoom and older models. This checklist shows how to confirm which one you have, what to ask a seller, and how to test it.

Short answer: look at the ring around the lens. A real Stylus Epic / mju-II reads 35mm 1:2.8, with one focal length and no range. If you see a range such as 38-80mm, or a maximum aperture other than 2.8, it's a different camera.

Olympus reused the same words across many cameras, so a title or listing can look right and still be the wrong model.

Why the name causes so much confusion

The Stylus Epic came out in 1997. It has several names depending on the market and version:

  • Olympus Stylus Epic or Infinity Stylus Epic: US names
  • mju-II, µ-II or µ[mju:]-II: Europe and Japan
  • DLX or QD on the name: a version with a date function

All of those are the camera most people are looking for. These similar names are different cameras:

  • Stylus Epic Zoom 80, 115 or 170 and mju-II Zoom 80, 115 or 170: they contain the exact words "Epic" or "mju-II", but they have zoom lenses.
  • Infinity Stylus / mju-I: the earlier model, with a slower 35mm f/3.5 lens.
  • Stylus Zoom models (for example, Stylus Zoom 115): film zooms.
  • Digital Stylus models: these have a screen on the back and no film door.

The 60-second identification checklist

Go in this order and stop at the first failure.

  1. Is it film? Look for a hinged back door and no large rear screen. A big rear screen means digital.
  2. Read the lens ring. You want 35mm 1:2.8. This is the single most reliable check.
  3. Look for zoom controls. A zoom rocker or a W/T control on the back means it's a zoom model. The Epic has none.
  4. Read the name on the body. Look for "Stylus Epic" or "mju-II", with no "Zoom" and no number such as 80 or 170 after it.
  5. Check the bottom plate or back for DLX, QD or date markings if the seller's description mentions a date function.

The sliding front cover proves nothing. The original Stylus and the zoom models use one too, so a seller's "clamshell Olympus" could be any of them.

How the models compare

Stylus Epic / mju-II

  • Lens: fixed 35mm f/2.8
  • Close focus: 35 cm
  • Weight: 145 g
  • Shutter: 4 s to 1/1000 s
  • Power: one CR123A

Stylus Epic Zoom / mju-II Zoom

  • Lens: zoom, starting at 38mm
  • Lens ring: shows a range
  • Body: larger, with the lens extending further as it zooms

Original Infinity Stylus / mju-I

  • Lens: fixed 35mm, but f/3.5
  • Looks close to the Epic in a small photo, so zoom in on the ring

Exact words to send a seller

If a listing doesn't show the lens ring, copy and fill in this message:

> Hi, is this still available? Could you send a clear, close photo of the ring around the lens with the cover open? I'm trying to confirm whether it says 35mm 1:2.8. Also, does it power on with a fresh CR123A battery, and did you see any green or white residue in the battery compartment? Has it been dropped, as far as you know? Thanks, [name]

If the seller can't open the cover because the battery is dead, that's normal. Ask for a photo through the gap or ask whether they can install a battery. Treat a camera nobody has powered on as untested, whatever the listing implies.

In-person test: no film needed

Bring a fresh CR123A. Do these in order.

  1. Open the battery compartment first. Corrosion on the contacts is a common fault on old cameras. Clean, bright contacts are what you want.
  2. Install the battery and slide the cover open. The camera should power on and the top LCD should show information. If it doesn't, try the battery the other way round before giving up on it.
  3. Watch the lens. It should extend smoothly when you open the cover and retract when you close it. Grinding, stalling or repeated attempts are warning signs.
  4. Half-press the shutter while pointing at something near, then something far. Listen for focus movement and look at the viewfinder for its focus confirmation. Reviewers report that the focus mechanism can fail outright on some copies, so don't skip this.
  5. Press the flash button on the back. The modes should cycle: Auto, Red-Eye, Flash Off, Fill-In, Night Scene, and Red-Eye Night Scene.
  6. Fire the flash in fill-in mode and check that you see it go off.
  7. Check the self-timer. The spec sheet lists a 12-second delay.
  8. Inspect the body. Look for dents at the corners, a cracked cover, a loose door latch, and scratches or haze on the front lens element. Avoid one that has clearly taken a hard drop.
  9. Open the back door. Look for damaged rollers, a bent pressure plate and crumbling or sticky foam around the door edge. Check that the door closes firmly.

Two things that are not faults:

  • The flash setting resets when you close the cover. The camera is built that way.
  • Spot metering has no separate button. One review says you press the flash and self-timer buttons together. Check the manual before calling that feature broken.

One thing we couldn't confirm: whether the shutter will fire with no film loaded. If it won't fire when empty, load a cheap roll before you decide it's broken.

The test that actually proves it works

A power-on check doesn't prove the camera exposes film correctly. If you're keeping it or selling it as fully working, shoot one roll.

  • Shoot a bright outdoor scene with a distant subject.
  • Shoot something close, at about 35-50 cm, to check close focus.
  • Shoot an indoor flash picture at a few metres.
  • Shoot into shade or low light without flash to check longer exposures.
  • Let the roll finish and confirm the camera rewinds on its own.

On the developed negatives, look for streaks of light from the edges, which point to light leaks. Also look for consistently soft pictures, which point to focus trouble, and blank frames, which point to a shutter or flash problem.

What it's worth, and how to check

Prices on this camera move around, so use these figures as a starting point only.

  • A third-party price tracker summarizing 18 completed sales, checked in October 2026, put a typical Mju II / Stylus Epic at about $445, with most sales between $377 and $572.
  • One Stylus Epic Zoom 170 Deluxe, with film and a case, sold for $250 in October 2026.
  • One working original Infinity Stylus sold for $115 in July.

Those last two are single sales, and they don't set a market price. To check it yourself:

  1. Search eBay for "stylus epic" OR "mju ii" -zoom.
  2. Filter to Sold Items and Used.
  3. Open listings and discard parts-only units, zooms and any bundle whose extras inflate the price.
  4. Take the median of the working units from the last 90 days.

Worked example: how much is too much?

Say you're thinking of paying $200 all-in and plan to resell on eBay. These costs are estimates, so use your own. Check your current eBay fee rate, too.

The price you can pay depends heavily on the condition you can prove. If the working median you find is under $340, a $125 limit is much safer than $200. A camera listed "untested" or "for parts" also carries the risk of being worth little or nothing.

If you're selling one

  • Put "35mm f/2.8" in the title, and "Stylus Epic" and "mju-II" so buyers searching either name find it.
  • Photograph the lens ring, LCD powered on, battery compartment, film chamber and any damage.
  • Say exactly what you tested. "Powers on, flash fires, not film tested" is more credible than "works great", and it protects you against a return claim.
