Golden Rule in World Religions: Free KS3 RE Cover Lesson Plan

A complete one-period KS3 RE lesson on the Golden Rule in six world faiths, ready to leave for a cover supervisor. It includes the script, timings, the six source texts, tasks, model answers and how to handle sensitive questions.

You're off tomorrow, it's late, and your Year 8 RE class needs work that a non-specialist can run without any subject knowledge. This page is that lesson. Copy the parts marked PRINT into a document. Leave the SCRIPT for the cover supervisor and the MODEL ANSWERS for whoever marks the work.

The lesson suits Years 7 to 9 and runs for 50 to 60 minutes. It needs a board, pens and one printed sheet per pupil. No videos, logins or prior knowledge are required.

Why this topic works for cover

  • Low risk. Pupils compare what faiths have in common, so they aren't asked to judge whether any belief is true.
  • Self-explanatory sources. The texts are one sentence long and pupils can read them without any help.
  • Easy to check. Every task has a clear right-or-wrong element plus one open question, so marking is quick.

Learning objective (write on the board)

I can explain what the Golden Rule is, find it in six religions, and decide whether it is a good rule for life.

The SCRIPT for the cover supervisor

0 to 5 minutes: Settle and starter. Write on the board: *"Think of a time someone treated you the way you would want to be treated. What did they do?"* Pupils write two sentences in silence. Take two or three answers. Don't ask for names of other pupils.

5 to 10 minutes: Introduce the idea. Read this aloud:

"Today we're looking at the Golden Rule. It's the idea that you should treat other people the way you would want to be treated. Versions of it appear in many religions and in non-religious ethics too. Your job is to find it in six religions, compare the wording, and decide whether it really works as a rule for life."

10 to 25 minutes: Task 1, Source matching. Hand out the sheet. Pupils read the six sources and complete the table. They can work in pairs, but each pupil writes their own answers.

25 to 40 minutes: Task 2, Spot the difference. Pupils answer the three comparison questions on their own.

40 to 52 minutes: Task 3, Big question. Pupils write a paragraph answering *"Is the Golden Rule a good rule for life?"* using the sentence starters.

52 to 55 minutes: Exit ticket. Each pupil writes one sentence on a sticky note or at the bottom of their sheet: *"One thing the religions agree on is..."* Collect the sheets.

Early finishers: Write your own version of the Golden Rule for your school, in 15 words or fewer.

PRINT: The six sources

Translations vary. The wording below is a common English version of each text.

  1. Christianity: "Do to others what you would have them do to you." (Jesus, in the Gospel of Matthew 7:12)
  2. Judaism: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour." (Rabbi Hillel, in the Talmud, Shabbat 31a)
  3. Islam: "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." (A saying of the Prophet Muhammad, a hadith recorded by Bukhari and Muslim)
  4. Hinduism: "One should never do that to another which one regards as injurious to one's own self." (Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva)
  5. Buddhism: "Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful." (Udanavarga 5:18)
  6. Sikhism: "No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger to me. I get along with everyone." (Guru Arjan, in the Guru Granth Sahib, page 1299)

PRINT: Task 2, Spot the difference

  1. Two of the sources tell you what to do and others tell you what not to do. Which version is harder to follow, and why?
  2. The Sikh source doesn't use the words "do to others". Explain how it still connects to the idea of treating people well.
  3. Pick the version you think is clearest. Explain your choice in two or three sentences.

PRINT: Task 3, Big question

"Is the Golden Rule a good rule for life?" Write one paragraph, using at least two of these starters:

  • "Some people would agree because..."
  • "For example, in [religion] the source says..."
  • "However, some people might disagree because..."
  • "A problem with the Golden Rule is..."
  • "Overall, I think... because..."

Challenge: What if someone wants to be treated in a way that other people don't? Does the rule still work?

MODEL ANSWERS (for marking)

Task 1, sources:

  • Christianity: the Bible (Gospel of Matthew). Phrased as DO.
  • Judaism: the Talmud (teaching of Rabbi Hillel). Phrased as DON'T.
  • Islam: Hadith (sayings of the Prophet Muhammad). Phrased as DO ("loves for his brother").
  • Hinduism: the Mahabharata. Phrased as DON'T.
  • Buddhism: the Udanavarga. Phrased as DON'T.
  • Sikhism: the Guru Granth Sahib. It's a statement of attitude rather than a rule, so accept any sensible answer that shows understanding.

Accept any "own words" rewrite that keeps the meaning, for example: "Don't do things to people that you wouldn't want done to you."

Task 2:

  1. Good answers notice that "do" versions ask you to actively help people, which takes effort. "Don't" versions ask you to avoid causing harm. Either answer can be argued, so reward the reasoning.
  2. If no one is your enemy or a stranger, you treat everyone as a friend. That leads to treating them as you would want to be treated.
  3. Any choice is fine if it's backed by a reason.

Task 3, what a strong paragraph includes:

  • A point in favour (for example, it is simple, it appears in many faiths, and it builds empathy)
  • At least one source quoted or referred to
  • A point against (for example, people want different things, or it doesn't tell you what to do when two people's needs clash)
  • A conclusion with a reason

A simple marking guide:

  • Working towards: gives an opinion with no reason.
  • Secure: gives an opinion with a reason and one religious example.
  • Greater depth: weighs both sides, uses two or more sources, and reaches a justified conclusion.

Handling sensitive moments (leave this with the script)

Cover supervisors say that what worries them most in RE is a pupil question they can't answer. These responses keep things calm without needing subject knowledge:

  • "Which religion is right?" Say: "In RE we learn what people believe and why. You don't have to agree with any of them. Today we're looking at what they have in common."
  • "I don't believe in any of this." Say: "That's fine. Lots of non-religious people agree with the Golden Rule too. You can argue whether it's a good rule without being religious."
  • A pupil makes a negative comment about a faith. Bring it back to the task: "We describe beliefs respectfully in this room. Let's look at what the source actually says." If it continues, follow the school's behaviour policy and note it for the class teacher.
  • A pupil shares something personal, such as being treated badly by someone. Thank them, move the lesson on gently, and follow your school's safeguarding procedure if anything they said raises a concern.
  • A factual question you can't answer. Say: "Great question. Write it at the bottom of your sheet and your teacher will answer it next lesson." Questions like this are useful to the teacher anyway.

Adapting it

  • Year 7: Cut Task 2 down to questions 1 and 3, and give more time to the table.
  • Year 9: Add a seventh source from Humanism. Ask pupils to write their own version of a "no-religion" Golden Rule, then compare it with the faith versions.
  • Lower reading ages: Read the six sources aloud as a class before Task 1, and let pupils draw a symbol next to each one.
  • Short periods (40 minutes): Drop Task 2 and make it homework.

The lesson works because the cover supervisor never has to explain religious content. The sources speak for themselves, and the tasks only ask pupils to compare, rewrite and give their opinion.
