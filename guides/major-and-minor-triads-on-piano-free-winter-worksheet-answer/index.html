Major and Minor Triads on Piano: Free Winter Worksheet + Answers

A no-prep, winter-themed worksheet on major and minor triads for Level 1 to Level 2 piano students, with a teaching script, six activities and a full answer key. Print it and use it in the lesson or send it home.

Triads usually come right after five-finger patterns and whole and half steps, and they tend to land in December. This page gives you a short way to teach them, a worksheet you can copy into a document or write on the board, and a complete answer key. The theme is snowmen and gifts, with nothing religious, so it suits any studio.

Level: late Level 1 to Level 2. Students should already know the white-key names and be able to count half steps on the keyboard. Time: about 10 to 15 minutes in the lesson, or one practice week at home. UK and Australian studios: wherever this page says "half step", use "semitone".

The 2-minute explanation

Say this at the piano, playing along as you go:

  1. "A triad is a three-note chord. The notes are stacked in skips, like a snowman with three snowballs."
  2. "The bottom note is the root, and the chord takes its name from it. The middle note is the third. The top note is the fifth."
  3. "In a major triad, the root and third are 4 half steps apart, and the third and fifth are 3 half steps apart. Think 4 + 3."
  4. "In a minor triad it's the other way round: 3 + 4. The root and fifth stay where they are. Only the middle note moves, down one half step."
  5. "You've already got a shortcut. In any major five-finger pattern, fingers 1, 3 and 5 play the major triad. Lower finger 3 by a half step and you have the minor triad."

Have the student play C major (C E G), then C minor (C E-flat G), and ask which one sounds "sunny" and which sounds "snowy". The words don't matter. What matters is that they hear a difference.

Three mistakes to look out for

  • Counting the starting key. Half steps count *moves*, not keys. From C to C-sharp is 1 half step. If a student counts C itself as "1", every answer comes out one too high.
  • Skipping the black keys. A half step goes to the very next key, black or white. Students who count only white keys will mark C to E as 2.
  • Spelling with the wrong letters. A triad always skips a letter: root, skip one, third, skip one, fifth. So D minor is D F A and never D E-sharp A. On the staff, all three notes sit on lines, or all three sit in spaces.

The worksheet: "Snowman Stacks"

Copy the sections below onto a page. Leave a blank line after each question, or print a keyboard diagram next to it for students who need to see the keys.

Part 1: Build a major snowman (4 + 3)

Write the three notes of each major triad, bottom snowball first.

  1. C major: ___ ___ ___
  2. G major: ___ ___ ___
  3. F major: ___ ___ ___
  4. D major: ___ ___ ___
  5. A major: ___ ___ ___
  6. E major: ___ ___ ___

Part 2: Build a minor snowman (3 + 4)

  1. C minor: ___ ___ ___
  2. G minor: ___ ___ ___
  3. F minor: ___ ___ ___
  4. D minor: ___ ___ ___
  5. A minor: ___ ___ ___
  6. E minor: ___ ___ ___

Part 3: Count the half steps

Count the half steps between the two notes. Use the keyboard and remember not to count the first key.

  1. C up to E: ___
  2. E up to G: ___
  3. D up to F: ___
  4. F up to A: ___
  5. G up to B: ___
  6. A up to C: ___

Part 4: Major or minor mittens?

Each mitten holds a triad. Write M for major or m for minor.

  1. D F A ___
  2. F A C ___
  3. E G B ___
  4. G B D ___
  5. A C-sharp E ___
  6. C E-flat G ___
  7. E G-sharp B ___
  8. A C E ___

Part 5: Swap the gift

Change ONE note to turn each triad into its opposite (major to minor, or minor to major). Write the new triad.

  1. G B D becomes ___ ___ ___
  2. D F A becomes ___ ___ ___
  3. E G B becomes ___ ___ ___
  4. F A C becomes ___ ___ ___

Part 6: The white-key snowman parade (challenge)

Build a triad on every white key using only white keys. Then write major, minor, or "something else" for each one.

  1. C: ___ ___ ___ is ______
  2. D: ___ ___ ___ is ______
  3. E: ___ ___ ___ is ______
  4. F: ___ ___ ___ is ______
  5. G: ___ ___ ___ is ______
  6. A: ___ ___ ___ is ______
  7. B: ___ ___ ___ is ______

Play it: Play all seven triads from Part 6 with your right hand, using fingers 1-3-5. Circle the one that sounds strangest.

Point out that questions 1 and 2 together build C major (4 + 3), and questions 3 and 4 together build D minor (3 + 4).

Part 4

  1. m (D minor)
  2. M (F major)
  3. m (E minor)
  4. M (G major)
  5. M (A major)
  6. m (C minor)
  7. M (E major)
  8. m (A minor)

Part 5

  1. G B-flat D (G minor)
  2. D F-sharp A (D major)
  3. E G-sharp B (E major)
  4. F A-flat C (F minor)

In every case only the middle note moves. If a student moved the root or the fifth, have them play both versions and listen again.

Part 6

  1. C E G is major
  2. D F A is minor
  3. E G B is minor
  4. F A C is major
  5. G B D is major
  6. A C E is minor
  7. B D F is something else. It is a diminished triad, with 3 + 3 half steps.

Most students circle B D F as the strangest. That's a good opening for the word "diminished", but they don't need to learn it yet. It's enough to know that not every triad is major or minor.

Ways to use it in a December lesson

  • As a warm-up: do Part 3 aloud at the piano before the student opens their pieces. It takes about two minutes and checks half-step counting.
  • As homework: send Parts 1, 2 and 4 home. Keep Part 6 for the next lesson so you can hear the triads played.
  • With a group or siblings: call out a triad name. The first student to play it correctly gets a paper snowflake. Whoever has the most snowflakes picks the next holiday piece to sight-read.
  • For a stronger student: have them play each triad from Part 1, then its minor from Part 2, hands together, in both hands.
  • For a student who struggles: do only C, G and F (all white keys in major), and use the five-finger shortcut before you mention counting.

Where triads fit in the winter theory sequence

Triads make the most sense after a student is secure with:

  1. Steps and skips on the staff
  2. Whole steps and half steps
  3. Major five-finger patterns
  4. Intervals of a 3rd and a 5th

If any of these are shaky, spend a week on that first. Students who can find the 1st, 3rd and 5th notes of a five-finger pattern quickly will usually learn triads in a single lesson. Students who can't will find the 4 + 3 counting much harder.

After triads, a natural next step is to name the triad under a simple holiday melody. For example, the first notes of many beginner arrangements of familiar winter songs sit inside a C major or G major triad, and students enjoy spotting them in their own music.
