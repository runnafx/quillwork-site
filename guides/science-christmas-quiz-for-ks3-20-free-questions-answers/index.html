Science Christmas Quiz for KS3: 20 Free Questions + Answers

Two ready-to-use science Christmas quiz rounds for KS3, Christmas chemistry and festive physics, with GCSE stretch questions, answer explanations and a 50-minute plan for running the lesson.

It's the last week of term. Your classes are tired, the room is noisy and you need a lesson that still involves some science. This page gives you two complete quiz rounds you can read out or copy onto slides. Each round has 10 KS3 questions, three GCSE stretch questions for Year 10 and 11 or fast finishers, and a short answer explanation for every question. That way the answer reveal still teaches something.

You can use everything here for free in your own classroom.

How to run it in a 50-minute lesson

  1. Minutes 0–5: Make teams. Use teams of 3 or 4. Each team gets one sheet of paper and one pen, so they have to talk before they write anything down.
  2. Minutes 5–17: Round 1 (Christmas chemistry). Read each question twice and allow about 45 seconds. Don't give answers yet.
  3. Minutes 17–22: Swap and mark Round 1. Teams swap sheets. Read the answers and explanations aloud. This is where the learning happens, so don't rush it.
  4. Minutes 22–34: Round 2 (festive physics). Run it the same way.
  5. Minutes 34–39: Swap and mark Round 2.
  6. Minutes 39–44: GCSE stretch or bonus. Use it with older groups, or as a "double or nothing" for the top two teams.
  7. Minutes 44–50: Tiebreaker, scores and pack-up.

Classroom tips

  • Ban phones before you start, not halfway through.
  • Accept spelling that's close for KS3 (for example "Mallard reaction"). Be stricter with GCSE groups.
  • Give one point for each correct answer. Where an answer has two parts, give half a point for each part.
  • If a team argues about an answer, ask them to explain their reasoning to the class. A good explanation can earn the point.

Round 1: Christmas Chemistry

Questions (KS3)

  1. What is the chemical symbol for gold?
  2. When baking soda is heated in a Christmas cake mix, which gas makes the cake rise?
  3. Firework makers add metal compounds to make colours. Name a metal that gives a red colour.
  4. Name a metal that gives a green or blue-green firework colour.
  5. When a candle burns completely, which two products are made?
  6. Roast potatoes, toast and gingerbread all turn brown when they cook. What is the name of the reaction between sugars and proteins that causes this?
  7. What is the chemical symbol for silver?
  8. Why do councils spread salt on icy roads in winter?
  9. The brandy on a Christmas pudding is set alight. What type of chemical reaction is this?
  10. A candle that burns with a smoky yellow flame leaves black soot on a plate. Which element is soot mostly made of?

Answers and explanations

  1. Au. It comes from the Latin word *aurum*. Many students guess "Go" or "G".
  2. Carbon dioxide. Heating sodium hydrogencarbonate releases carbon dioxide gas. Bubbles of the gas get trapped in the mixture, so the cake rises.
  3. Strontium (also accept lithium). Both give red flames.
  4. Barium (green) or copper (blue-green). Accept either.
  5. Carbon dioxide and water. Candle wax is a hydrocarbon. When a hydrocarbon burns completely, it makes carbon dioxide and water.
  6. The Maillard reaction. For KS3, also accept "a chemical change" for half a point. Caramelisation is a different browning reaction involving sugar only, so it does not get the full point.
  7. Ag. It comes from the Latin word *argentum*.
  8. Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so ice can melt even when the temperature is a little below 0 °C.
  9. Combustion (also accept burning or oxidation). It is also exothermic, so give a bonus point to any team that says that.
  10. Carbon. A smoky yellow flame means incomplete combustion. There isn't enough oxygen, so some carbon is released as soot. Incomplete combustion can also produce carbon monoxide.

GCSE stretch (chemistry)

  • S1. Write a balanced symbol equation for the complete combustion of ethanol (C₂H₅OH).

Answer: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

  • S2. Sodium hydrogencarbonate breaks down when heated. Name this type of reaction and give the three products.

Answer: Thermal decomposition. The products are sodium carbonate, water and carbon dioxide (2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂).

  • S3. Explain why burning a candle with a limited air supply is a safety risk indoors.

Answer: Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide. It is toxic because it reduces how much oxygen the blood can carry, and you can't see or smell it.

Round 2: Festive Physics

Questions (KS3)

  1. Rudolph has a red nose. Which colour of visible light has the longest wavelength?
  2. Some old-style fairy lights are wired in series. What happens to the rest of the string when one bulb breaks?
  3. What type of radiation do you feel as heat when you sit near a fire?
  4. Could Santa hear his sleigh bells ringing in space? Explain why or why not.
  5. What is the unit of frequency?
  6. Two identical snowmen are built. One wears a thick woolly coat. Which one melts more slowly?
  7. Roughly how fast does light travel? (Choose: 300 km/s, 300,000 km/s or 300 million km/s.)
  8. A sledge starts at the top of a hill and speeds up as it slides down. Energy is transferred from which store to which store?
  9. You bite into a hot mince pie. The pastry and the filling are at the same temperature, but the filling burns your mouth more. Why?
  10. What is the name of a material that slows down energy transfer by heating, such as the wool in a Christmas jumper?

Answers and explanations

  1. Red. Violet has the shortest wavelength of visible light.
  2. They all go out. A series circuit has only one path. If a bulb breaks, the circuit is incomplete and no current flows. In a parallel circuit, the other bulbs stay on.
  3. Infrared radiation.
  4. No. Sound is a vibration that needs particles (a medium) to travel through. Space is almost a vacuum.
  5. Hertz (Hz). One hertz means one wave per second.
  6. The snowman in the coat. This surprises most students. The coat is an insulator, so it slows the energy moving from the warmer air into the snow. Coats don't make heat. They slow down energy transfer in either direction.
  7. 300,000 km/s (also written 3 × 10⁸ m/s).
  8. From the gravitational potential store to the kinetic store. Some energy is also transferred to the thermal store of the surroundings by friction.
  9. The filling has a higher specific heat capacity. It contains a lot of water, so each gram stores more energy at the same temperature and transfers more energy to your mouth. "It holds more heat" can get half a point at KS3.
  10. An insulator (thermal insulator).

GCSE stretch (physics)

  • S1. A string of fairy lights has a power of 5 W. It is switched on for 8 hours a day for 20 days. How much energy does it transfer in kilowatt-hours?

Answer: 5 W × 8 h × 20 = 800 Wh = 0.8 kWh.

  • S2. A 230 V plug-in Christmas decoration draws a current of 0.1 A. What is its power?

Answer: P = IV = 0.1 × 230 = 23 W.

  • S3. Explain why LED fairy lights are a better choice than old filament bulbs.

Answer: A filament bulb transfers a lot of its energy to the thermal store of the surroundings, so less is transferred as light. LEDs are more efficient: more of the input energy becomes useful light. For the same brightness they need less power, which means lower energy use and lower running costs.

Tiebreaker

How many elements are on the current periodic table? The answer is 118. The closest team wins. If two teams are equally close, ask them to name as many noble gases as they can in 30 seconds. There are six main noble gases: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon. You can also accept oganesson.

Adapting the quiz for different classes

  • Year 7: Read questions aloud and show them on the board. Give multiple-choice options for questions 3, 4, 6 and 9 in each round.
  • Year 8–9: Use the quiz as written. Offer one stretch question per round as a bonus.
  • Year 10–11: Use all the stretch questions. Give full marks only if the answer includes the explanation, not just the keyword.
  • Mixed or cover classes: Every answer comes with its explanation, so a non-specialist can run the quiz straight from this page.
  • Students with EAL or low reading confidence: Read each question twice and put any key word on the board (for example "freezing point" or "series circuit").

Turning the answers into learning

The answer reveal is the best part of a science Christmas quiz, so give it time. Three questions usually start a useful class discussion:

  • The snowman in a coat (physics Q6). It challenges the common idea that coats "make you warm". Ask the class what the coat does for a person compared with a snowman.
  • Smoky candles (chemistry Q10 and S3). These link to combustion and carbon monoxide safety, which students will meet again at GCSE.
  • Series fairy lights (physics Q2). Draw the circuit diagram on the board, then ask how to rewire it so that one broken bulb doesn't switch off the whole string.

At the end, ask each team to pick the one answer that surprised them most. This takes two minutes and works as an exit ticket.

A quick checklist before the lesson

  • Teams decided, or a method for making them chosen
  • One answer sheet and one pen per team
  • Questions on slides or printed for you to read out
  • Answers kept somewhere students can't see
  • Scoreboard space on the whiteboard
  • Tiebreaker ready
  • Stretch questions ready if you have Year 10–11 or a fast group

Two rounds fill a 50-minute lesson comfortably. For a whole hour, or for a different quiz with each class that week, add rounds on winter biology, periodic table puzzles or famous scientists using the same format: 10 questions, a short explanation for each answer, and swap marking between rounds.
