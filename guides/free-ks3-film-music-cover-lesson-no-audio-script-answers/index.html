Free KS3 Film Music Cover Lesson: No Audio, Script + Answers

A complete one-hour KS3 Music cover lesson on film and game music. A non-specialist can run it with only pens and paper, using a word-for-word script, a student task sheet and a full answer key.

Film music is one of the hardest Music topics to set as cover, because most lessons depend on playing clips. This lesson doesn't. Students act as the composer: they decide what the music should be like and explain why using real musical vocabulary. They never need to hear a note.

It suits Years 7 to 9 and runs for 60 minutes. A cover supervisor who doesn't read music can deliver it. Students need a pen and paper. You need one printed copy of this page.

Before you set it: read it through once and change anything that doesn't match your school's vocabulary. If your students haven't met the elements of music yet, Task A covers what they need.

Lesson at a glance

  • Learning goal: Explain how composers use tempo, dynamics, pitch and instruments to create mood in films and games.
  • Needs: Pens and paper (or exercise books). Nothing to plug in.
  • Noise level: Silent or quiet throughout. There's no clapping or singing.

Supervisor Script

Read the lines marked SAY aloud. You don't need any musical knowledge, because every answer is in the answer key further down.

SAY (start): "Your Music teacher is away, so today there's no playing. Instead, you're going to be film and game composers. Composers decide how music should sound before anyone plays it. That's what you'll do. Write today's title: Film and Game Music."

SAY (starter, 5 minutes): "Think of a film, TV show or video game where the music made you feel something: scared, excited, sad or tense. Write down the name and the feeling. Please don't hum it." Take three or four answers. Point out that the music was *designed* to make them feel that way.

SAY (Task A): "Copy each key term and its meaning. Then answer the three questions underneath."

SAY (Task B): "Diegetic sound is sound the characters in the story can hear. Non-diegetic sound is sound only the audience hears. Sort the eight examples into two lists."

SAY (Task C): "For each scene, choose the tempo, dynamics, pitch and instruments. Then write one sentence explaining your choices."

SAY (Task D): "Game music has to change depending on what the player does. Fill in the cue sheet for your own game."

SAY (plenary): "On a fresh line, write three things: one key term and its meaning, one example of diegetic sound, and one way a composer could make music sound scary."

Collect the sheets for the Music teacher, especially Tasks C and D.

Student Task Sheet

Task A: Key terms (copy and learn)

  • Soundtrack / score: all the music written for a film or game.
  • Leitmotif: a short musical idea linked to a character, place or object. It comes back whenever they appear.
  • Underscore: quiet background music played underneath dialogue.
  • Ostinato: a short pattern that repeats over and over. It's often used to build tension.
  • Stinger: a short, sudden, loud sound or chord used for a shock or jump scare.
  • Mickey-mousing: music that copies the action exactly, such as a falling note when a character falls. It's common in cartoons.
  • Diegetic: sound the characters can hear (it comes from inside the story).
  • Non-diegetic: sound only the audience can hear (it's added for us).

Questions

  1. A villain appears and the same low, dark tune plays every time. What is that tune called?
  2. Why might a composer use an ostinato in a chase scene?
  3. Why does underscore need to be quiet?

Task B: Diegetic or non-diegetic?

Write D (diegetic) or N (non-diegetic) for each one.

  1. A band playing at a wedding that the characters are dancing to
  2. Sad strings playing while a character walks alone in the rain
  3. A character's phone ringing
  4. Loud drums as the hero runs towards the camera in slow motion
  5. A radio playing in a car the characters are sitting in
  6. A sudden loud chord as a monster jumps out
  7. A character singing in the shower
  8. Gentle piano music during the closing credits

Answer Key

Task A

  1. A leitmotif.
  2. Any sensible answer. For example: it repeats again and again, which builds tension and a feeling of being chased or of something that won't stop. A fast ostinato also adds energy.
  3. Any sensible answer. For example: so the audience can still hear what the characters are saying. The music sets the mood without distracting from the dialogue.

Task B

  1. D. The characters can hear the band.
  2. N. Only the audience hears the strings.
  3. D
  4. N
  5. D
  6. N. This is a stinger.
  7. D
  8. N

Students who mark 1, 5 and 7 as N have usually mixed up the two terms. Remind them that diegetic sound comes from inside the story world.

Task C (model answers; accept any choice that is well explained)

  • Scene 1: Slow or medium tempo, quiet and gradually getting louder (a crescendo), mostly low pitch with occasional high notes. Low strings, a deep piano or a low drone, perhaps with a repeating ostinato. *Why:* quiet, low music feels dark and uncertain, and a slow build makes the audience expect something to happen.
  • Scene 2: Slow tempo, quiet, medium to high pitch. Solo piano, strings or a solo violin or cello. *Why:* slow, gentle music played by a single instrument makes the moment feel sad and lonely.
  • Scene 3: Fast tempo, loud, high and low together. Full orchestra, brass, drums and cymbals. *Why:* loud, fast music with lots of instruments feels exciting and triumphant, like the crowd.

Task D (marking guide; there are no single right answers)

Good answers:

  • Use real element words (tempo, dynamics, pitch, texture, instruments) instead of only "happy" or "scary".
  • Show that the music changes between levels. For example, the tempo speeds up when the enemy appears, or drums and brass are added for the boss.
  • Make the game over music slower, quieter or lower than the victory jingle.
  • Keep the victory jingle short (a few seconds), loud and in a high pitch.

Extension: look for a clear link between the music and the character. For example: "A brave knight gets a loud trumpet tune that rises in pitch, because trumpets sound bold and heroic."

Tips for the Music Teacher Setting This

  • Teach the plenary question next lesson. Asking "What's a leitmotif?" at the start of your next lesson shows you value the cover work.
  • Follow it up with listening. When you're back, play a scene with the sound off, then on, and ask students to compare it with their Task C designs. That turns the cover lesson into the first half of a real listening lesson.
  • Adjust the level. For Year 7, drop the term "mickey-mousing" and give sentence starters for Task C, such as "I chose a slow tempo because...". For Year 9, ask for Italian terms (piano, forte, crescendo, allegro, adagio) in Task C.
  • Leave the key with the supervisor. Tasks A and B can be peer-marked in the last few minutes if you leave the answer key with the cover supervisor.

Quick checklist before you print

  • One copy of the script and answer key for the supervisor
  • One student sheet per student (or display the tasks and let students answer in their books)
  • The lesson title and date written on the cover note
  • A clear instruction to collect Tasks C and D
