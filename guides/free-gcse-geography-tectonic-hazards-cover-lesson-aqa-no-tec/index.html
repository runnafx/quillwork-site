A complete, print-and-go AQA GCSE Geography cover lesson on tectonic hazards with a supervisor script, an information sheet, 10 questions, a 6-mark exam question and a full answer key. A non-specialist can run it with no slides or computers.

This lesson is for a Year 10 or Year 11 AQA GCSE Geography class (8035, Paper 1, The Challenge of Natural Hazards). It lasts 50 to 60 minutes. A cover supervisor with no Geography background can run it from paper alone.

It covers plate margins and compares the effects of, and responses to, earthquakes in places with different levels of wealth. The examples are Nepal 2015 and Chile 2010, which many schools teach. If your department uses other case studies, the structure still works. Just swap in your own facts.

Part 1: Cover Supervisor Sheet

What you need

  • Student copies of the information sheet and the question sheet
  • Lined paper, or exercise books
  • This sheet and the answer key (keep the answer key with you until the last 10 minutes)

Timings and script

0–5 min: Settle and register. Say: "Your teacher has set a lesson on earthquakes for your GCSE Paper 1. You'll read an information sheet, answer questions, then write one exam-style answer. Work in silence for the first part."

5–20 min: Reading. Hand out the information sheet. Say: "Read the whole sheet. Underline every key term in bold and every number." Walk around the room and check that pens are moving.

20–35 min: Knowledge check. Hand out the questions. Say: "Answer questions 1 to 10 in full sentences. You can use the information sheet." Students who finish early can start the 6-mark question.

35–50 min: Exam question. Say: "Now answer the 6-mark question. You have 10 minutes, which is close to real exam timing. Use both examples and use comparison words like 'whereas' and 'however'."

50–60 min: Self-mark. Read out the answers to questions 1 to 10 from Part 4. Students mark their own work in a different colour. Then read out the 6-mark level descriptions and ask each student to write the level they think they reached at the bottom of the page.

Behaviour notes

  • If someone says "We haven't done this", tell them all the facts they need are on the sheet.
  • Students who finish early can do the extension at the bottom of the question sheet.

What to collect

Collect the books or loose sheets with names on them, and leave them for the class teacher. Note anyone who did not complete the 6-mark answer.

Part 2: Student Information Sheet

Why earthquakes happen

The Earth's crust is split into large slabs called tectonic plates. They move a few centimetres a year, driven by forces in the mantle. The boundary between two plates is called a plate margin. Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen at or near plate margins.

  • Destructive margin: two plates move towards each other. If an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks underneath it. This is called subduction. Earthquakes and explosive volcanoes are common.
  • Collision margin: two continental plates move towards each other. Neither sinks, so the crust is pushed up into fold mountains such as the Himalayas. Earthquakes are common, but there are few or no volcanoes.
  • Constructive margin: two plates move apart and magma rises to fill the gap. Earthquakes are usually small and the volcanoes are gentle.
  • Conservative margin: two plates slide past each other. Friction builds up pressure that is released suddenly as an earthquake. There are no volcanoes.

The point underground where the earthquake starts is the focus. The point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre. The size of an earthquake is measured on the moment magnitude scale.

Effects and responses: key terms

  • Primary effects: caused directly by the ground shaking. Examples are collapsed buildings, deaths from falling buildings and damaged roads.
  • Secondary effects: happen later, as a result of the primary effects. Examples are landslides, tsunamis, fires, disease, homelessness and economic losses.
  • Immediate responses: in the hours and days afterwards. Examples are search and rescue, emergency shelter, food, water and medical care.
  • Long-term responses: in the months and years afterwards. Examples are rebuilding, new building regulations and economic recovery.

Example 1: Nepal, 25 April 2015 (a lower-income country)

  • Magnitude 7.8. The epicentre was in the Gorkha district, north-west of the capital, Kathmandu.
  • Cause: a collision margin where the Indian (Indo-Australian) plate is moving into the Eurasian plate.
  • Primary effects: around 9,000 people died. Hundreds of thousands of homes were destroyed. Historic buildings in Kathmandu were damaged, and the Dharahara tower collapsed.
  • Secondary effects: landslides blocked roads in mountain areas, which made it hard to reach villages. An avalanche hit Everest base camp. Many families were left living in temporary shelters through the monsoon season.
  • Responses: international teams from many countries helped with search and rescue. Helicopters were used to reach cut-off villages. Rebuilding was slow, partly because many rural areas are hard to reach.

Example 2: Chile, 27 February 2010 (a higher-income country)

  • Magnitude 8.8, which is much stronger than Nepal. The epicentre was off the coast in the Maule region.
  • Cause: a destructive margin where the oceanic Nazca plate is subducting under the South American plate.
  • Primary effects: around 500 people died. Many buildings were damaged, especially older ones, but modern buildings built to strict earthquake regulations mostly stayed standing. Roads, bridges and ports were damaged.
  • Secondary effects: a tsunami hit coastal towns and caused many of the deaths. Some areas lost power and water, and there was looting in Concepción in the first days.
  • Responses: the government brought in the army to restore order, and emergency services restored many key services quickly. Chile had the money and organisation to lead its own recovery and needed less outside help.

The big idea

Chile's earthquake was far stronger, but far fewer people died. Wealth affects building quality, preparation, emergency services and how fast a country can recover. Geography examiners reward answers that explain this link.

Part 3: Student Questions

Knowledge check (1 mark each unless stated)

  1. What is a plate margin?
  2. Name the type of plate margin that caused the Nepal earthquake.
  3. Explain what subduction is. (2 marks)
  4. What is the difference between the focus and the epicentre? (2 marks)
  5. Give one primary effect of the Nepal earthquake.
  6. Give one secondary effect of the Chile earthquake.
  7. What is the difference between an immediate and a long-term response? (2 marks)
  8. Which earthquake had the higher magnitude?
  9. Why do volcanoes not form at collision margins?
  10. Suggest one reason why fewer people died in Chile than in Nepal. (2 marks)

Exam-style question (6 marks)

Using named examples, compare the effects of a tectonic hazard in two areas of contrasting levels of wealth.

Tips: write about both places in every paragraph, use numbers from the sheet, and explain why the effects were different.

Extension

Draw and label a diagram of a destructive plate margin. Show the oceanic plate, the continental plate, subduction and where earthquakes happen.

Part 4: Answer Key and Mark Scheme

Knowledge check answers

  1. The boundary where two tectonic plates meet.
  2. A collision margin.
  3. An oceanic plate sinks under a continental plate (1) because it is denser (1).
  4. The focus is the point underground where the earthquake starts (1). The epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it (1).
  5. Any one of: about 9,000 deaths, homes destroyed, historic buildings damaged, Dharahara tower collapsed.
  6. Any one of: a tsunami, loss of power or water, looting in Concepción.
  7. Immediate responses happen in the hours or days after the event, such as rescue and shelter (1). Long-term responses happen over months or years, such as rebuilding and new regulations (1).
  8. Chile (8.8, compared with 7.8 in Nepal).
  9. The two plates are both continental and neither sinks, so there is no subduction to create magma.
  10. Any reason with development (2), for example: Chile has strict building regulations, so modern buildings stayed standing (1) and fewer people were crushed (1). Also accept: better emergency services, more money to respond, easier access than Nepal's mountain villages.

6-mark levels mark scheme

Level 3 (5–6 marks): Detailed, well-developed comparison of effects in both places. Accurate place-specific facts (such as deaths, magnitude and named places) are used throughout. Clearly explains why effects differed, linking this to wealth.

Level 2 (3–4 marks): Some comparison, but it may be unbalanced or partly descriptive. Some accurate facts are used. Some explanation of differences.

Level 1 (1–2 marks): Basic, mostly separate descriptions of each place with little or no comparison. Facts are vague or missing.

0 marks: Nothing worthy of credit.

What a strong answer includes

  • A direct comparison: "Chile's earthquake was stronger (8.8) than Nepal's (7.8), whereas far fewer people died in Chile (around 500) than in Nepal (around 9,000)."
  • Primary and secondary effects for both places, such as Nepal's landslides cutting off villages compared with Chile's tsunami along the coast.
  • An explanation tied to wealth: building regulations, money for emergency services, and how easily rescuers could reach people.
  • Comparison words such as "whereas", "however", "in contrast" and "similarly".

Example Level 3 opening paragraph

"Although the 2010 Chile earthquake was much stronger at magnitude 8.8, it caused around 500 deaths, whereas the weaker 7.8 earthquake in Nepal in 2015 killed around 9,000 people. This is mainly because Chile, as a higher-income country, enforces strict building regulations, so many modern buildings withstood the shaking. In contrast, many homes in Nepal were poorly built and collapsed, crushing the people inside."

Tips for the Class Teacher Afterwards

  • Check the self-assessed levels against your own marking of three or four scripts to judge how accurate the class is at self-marking.
  • Students who reached only Level 1 usually wrote about each country separately. Next lesson, show them how to write a "whereas" sentence.
  • If your department teaches different case studies, keep this structure and replace the two fact boxes.
