How to Calculate Your Max Bid on HiBid Before Reselling on eBay

A step-by-step way to turn a liquidation auction lot into one firm max-bid number. It covers buyer's premium, sales tax, card fees, missing accessories, eBay fees and the risk of buying "not tested" items, with a fill-in worksheet and a worked example.

The $5 current bid is the cheap part of an online liquidation auction. The real cost comes later: a buyer's premium, sales tax, a card fee, shipping or a drive to pick up, and often a missing charger. On the resale side, eBay takes its cut and you pay for the label.

If you bid by feel, a lot that looked like a $100 profit can end up paying you $10, or losing money if the item turns out to be dead.

This guide gives you one number to decide on before the auction closes: your max bid. You enter it once and then leave the lot alone.

Step 1: Read the lot terms before you look at the price

Every auctioneer on HiBid and similar platforms sets its own terms. Write these down from the lot page and the auction's terms tab:

  • Buyer's premium (a percentage added to the hammer price)
  • Sales tax rate, and whether it is charged on the hammer alone or on hammer plus premium (usually the second)
  • Card or processing fee, if any, and what it applies to
  • Handling or lot fees (a flat amount per lot)
  • Shipping: is it offered, at what cost, or is it pickup only? Is there a pickup deadline?
  • Condition line, word for word (see Step 2)
  • What's included: box, charger, cables, bands, manuals

If you can't find a term, assume the worse version, or message the auctioneer before you bid.

Step 2: Translate the condition line honestly

Liquidation condition labels are short and they matter a lot:

  • New / Sealed: the safest bet, but it usually draws the most bidders.
  • Open Box: the package has been opened. It may be complete, or it may be missing parts.
  • Not Tested / Untested: nobody checked whether it works. Treat working as a possibility, not a given.
  • Damaged / For Parts / Salvage: price it as parts unless the photos clearly show otherwise.
  • Missing charger / accessories: add the replacement cost, and check that a genuine or compatible part exists and is cheap.

Look at every photo. Check for screen cracks, swollen batteries (a case or back that bulges), corrosion on charging contacts and signs of a return-to-store sticker. A "not tested" item in clean, original packaging is a different bet from a loose one in a bin.

Step 3: Find a realistic resale price, not a hopeful one

Use eBay's Sold filter, not active listings. Asking prices are not sale prices. Filter by:

  • The exact model (for example, "Lily 2" and not just "Lily", and the right size or variant)
  • Condition matching what you'll be able to sell: Used or Open Box, not New
  • The last 90 days

Note the lower half of the sold prices, not the top. Ignore sold listings that included extras you won't have, and watch for "for parts" sales mixed into the results, because they drag the numbers around. Your conservative sale price should be a price you'd be comfortable listing at Buy It Now and still getting sold within a few weeks.

Also estimate the parts value: what a non-working unit of this model sells for under "For parts or not working." Sometimes it's meaningful, sometimes it's close to zero.

Step 4: Work out what you actually keep from a sale

From your conservative sale price, subtract:

  • eBay selling fees. For most categories this is a percentage of the total sale plus a small per-order fee. Check eBay's current fee page for your category, because the rates change.
  • Shipping label cost for the packed size and weight (a small padded box for a watch, for example)
  • Packing supplies, if you don't already have them

What's left is your net if it works.

Step 5: Price in the "not tested" risk

For untested items, don't assume the best case. Pick your own honest estimate of the chance it works. If you have no idea, call it a coin flip. Then:

Expected net = (chance it works × net if it works) + (chance it doesn't × parts value)

For new or tested items, the chance it works is close to 1, but leave some room for returns.

Step 6: Subtract your required profit and fixed costs

Decide the smallest profit that makes the time worth it: listing, photos, testing, packing, answering questions. Write it down before you see the bidding.

Max all-in cost = Expected net − required profit

Then subtract costs that don't depend on the hammer price:

  • Shipping from the auctioneer (or your gas and time for pickup)
  • Flat handling or lot fees
  • Replacement charger or cable
  • Cleaning or small repairs

What remains is the amount you can spend on hammer plus the percentage-based fees.

Step 7: Convert that into a max bid

The premium, tax and card fee all scale with the hammer price, so divide by a combined multiplier:

Multiplier = (1 + premium) × (1 + tax rate) × (1 + card fee)

Max bid = (Max all-in cost − fixed costs) ÷ Multiplier

Round down to the auction's bid increment. If the card fee applies to the whole invoice, including shipping, apply it to the shipping too. Small errors here add up.

Fill-in worksheet

Copy this into a note for each lot:

  • Lot / item: ____________
  • Condition line (exact words): ____________
  • Buyer's premium: ____ %
  • Sales tax: ____ % (on hammer + premium? Y / N)
  • Card fee: ____ %
  • Flat handling/lot fee: $____
  • Shipping to me or pickup cost: $____
  • Missing accessories cost: $____
  • Conservative sold price (Used, last 90 days): $____
  • eBay fees on that price: $____
  • My outbound shipping + packing: $____
  • Net if it works: $____
  • Chance it works (my estimate): ____
  • Parts value if dead: $____
  • Expected net: $____
  • Required profit: $____
  • Max all-in cost: $____
  • Fixed costs (shipping + handling + accessories): $____
  • Multiplier: ____
  • MAX BID: $____ (rounded down)

Worked example: an open-box, untested smartwatch

Here's the full method on a hypothetical lot: an open-box women's fitness smartwatch, marked "Not Tested," in its packaging, with no charger. All numbers below are example inputs. Replace them with what you see on your lot and in your own sold search.

Lot terms (example): 15% buyer's premium, 8.25% sales tax on hammer + premium, 3% card fee, shipping about $10 (estimate).

Fixed costs: $10 shipping in + $8 replacement charging cable = $18.

Resale side: say your Used sold search supports a conservative price of $125.

  • eBay fees: if your category runs about 13.6% plus $0.40 per order, that's about $17.40. Check the current rate.
  • Outbound label and padded box: about $6 (estimate)
  • Net if it works: $125 − $17.40 − $6 = $101.60

Risk: untested, so call it a coin flip. Parts value if dead: say $20.

  • Expected net = 0.5 × $101.60 + 0.5 × $20 = $60.80

Required profit: $15.

  • Max all-in cost = $60.80 − $15 = $45.80
  • Minus fixed costs: $45.80 − $18 = $27.80 left for hammer plus fees

Multiplier: 1.15 × 1.0825 × 1.03 ≈ 1.282

Max bid: $27.80 ÷ 1.282 ≈ $21.68, so bid $21.

Check it forward at a $21 hammer:

  • Premium $3.15 → $24.15
  • Tax $1.99 → $26.14
  • Card fee $0.78 → $26.92
  • Plus $18 fixed → $44.92 all-in, under the $45.80 limit

If you thought the chance it works was higher, say because the photos show a sealed inner tray, the same math gives a higher max bid. If the auctioneer is pickup-only and two hours away, it gives a lower one. The method stays the same; only your inputs change.

Bidding checklist

  • Enter your max bid once, as a proxy or max bid, and don't raise it in the final minutes.
  • Check whether the auction uses soft close (time extends after late bids), so you aren't caught off guard.
  • Confirm the pickup window or shipping turnaround fits your schedule. Missed pickups can forfeit the item.
  • If the bidding passes your max, let it go. There will be another lot.
  • Skip lots where the current bid is already above your max. Don't hope it stalls.

After you win: protect the margin

  1. Test right away with a known-good charger. Check that it powers on, charges, pairs and the screen works.
  2. Reset it to factory settings and check for any account or activation lock. A locked device may not be sellable as working.
  3. Clean it and photograph everything, including what's not included.
  4. List it honestly. State it's open box, what you tested, what's included and what isn't. Clear descriptions mean fewer returns.
  5. If it's dead, list it as "For parts or not working" with a plain description. Your worksheet already accounted for that outcome.

Bid using the worksheet number and every lot is a decision you made in advance.
