Is It Ever Right to Lie? Free KS3 RE / Grades 6-8 Sub Plan

A complete no-tech ethics lesson for a 45 to 60 minute period, with a script for the substitute, a short reading on what four world faiths and two thinkers say about lying, discussion scenarios and a model answer guide. A non-specialist substitute or cover supervisor can run it.

This lesson runs for 45 to 60 minutes and suits grades 6-8 or KS3 RE. The substitute needs no subject knowledge, no technology and no prep beyond printing. Students compare what religions and philosophers say about telling the truth, then apply those ideas to real situations. No one has to share a personal lie or a personal belief. Students are marked on the reasons they give, not on which side they pick.

Script for the Substitute

Start (5 minutes). Write on the board: "Is it ever right to lie?" Say:

"Today's question has no single correct answer. People in every religion have argued about it for thousands of years. You get marks for good reasons, not for picking the 'right' side. Please don't tell us about real lies you've told. Use made-up examples."

Students write YES, NO or IT DEPENDS with one sentence explaining why. Ask for a show of hands for each option and write the numbers on the board. You'll ask again at the end.

Reading (12 minutes). Students read the six short sections aloud or in pairs. Pronunciation guide:

  • Kant: KANT (rhymes with "pant")
  • Talmud: TAHL-mood
  • Hadith: ha-DEETH
  • Satyameva Jayate: SUT-ya-MAY-va JAI-a-tay
  • Guru Nanak: GOO-roo NAH-nuk

If you aren't sure how to say a word, say so and carry on.

Task A (8 minutes). Students match each view to its source on their own. Read the answers out from the key.

Task B (15 minutes). In pairs, students discuss the four scenarios and write a decision plus one reason for each. Walk around and ask, "Which view from the reading backs you up?"

Task C (10 minutes). Students write a short paragraph on their own.

Finish (5 minutes). Take the YES / NO / IT DEPENDS vote again. Ask: "Did anyone change their mind? What changed it?" Collect the work.

Behaviour and handling notes:

  • If a student gives a personal example, say: "Thanks. Let's use a made-up example so nobody's private business is in the room," and move on.
  • Students may disagree strongly. One rule covers it: criticise the argument, not the person or their religion.
  • If a student says "Religion X says lying is fine," point back to the reading. Every tradition here values honesty. They disagree only about rare exceptions.
  • If anything comes up that worries you about a student's safety, follow your school's safeguarding procedure. Don't try to deal with it in the lesson.

Student Reading: Six Views on Lying

1. Christianity. One of the Ten Commandments says, "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbour." That means you must not lie about other people, especially to get them into trouble. The Bible also tells stories of people who misled powerful rulers to save lives. In Exodus, two midwives didn't tell Pharaoh the truth so they could protect Hebrew baby boys. In the book of Joshua, Rahab hid two spies and sent soldiers the wrong way. Christians have long debated whether those stories make lying acceptable or only show that the people were brave.

2. Judaism. The Ten Commandments are also part of the Jewish scriptures, and honesty is a central value. The Talmud, an important collection of Jewish teaching, discusses cases where it is allowed to change the truth for the sake of peace, for example to stop a quarrel between people.

3. Islam. The Qur'an tells believers to be truthful and to avoid false statements. The Prophet Muhammad was known as "al-Amin", the trustworthy one. One hadith (a reported saying of the Prophet) names three narrow situations where saying something untrue is allowed: in war, to make peace between people who are arguing, and between a husband and wife to keep their love strong.

4. Buddhism. One of the Five Precepts that Buddhists try to follow is to avoid false speech. "Right Speech" is part of the Noble Eightfold Path. It means speaking truthfully, kindly, at the right time and in a way that helps.

5. Hinduism and Sikhism. India's national motto, "Satyameva Jayate" ("Truth alone triumphs"), comes from an ancient Hindu text called the Mundaka Upanishad. In Sikhism, Guru Nanak taught that truth is high, but truthful living is higher still. Sikhs believe honesty has to show in what you do, not only in what you say.

6. Two philosophers. Immanuel Kant was an 18th-century German thinker. He argued that lying is always wrong, even to someone who asks where your friend is hiding so they can hurt them. He believed that if everyone lied whenever it suited them, nobody could trust anything anyone said. Utilitarian thinkers such as John Stuart Mill judge an action by its results. On that view, a lie that prevents great harm could be the right thing to do.

Student Tasks

Task A: Match each view to its source.

  1. "Truth alone triumphs"
  2. A lie can be right if it leads to the best results
  3. Do not bear false witness against your neighbour
  4. Truthful living is higher than truth itself
  5. Changing the truth is allowed for the sake of peace
  6. Lying is wrong even to save a friend
  7. Avoid false speech, one of the Five Precepts
  8. Untrue words allowed only in war, peacemaking and between husband and wife

Sources: Kant / Buddhism / Islam (hadith) / Ten Commandments / Utilitarianism / Hindu Mundaka Upanishad / Guru Nanak / Talmud

Task B: What should they do? Give a decision and one reason for each.

  1. Your friend shows you a drawing they're proud of. You think it's not very good. They ask what you think.
  2. A stranger with an angry face asks which way your classmate ran.
  3. You're planning a surprise party, and the birthday person asks, "Are you planning something?"
  4. Your friend copied homework and the teacher asks you if you know anything about it.

Task C: Paragraph. Answer "Is it ever right to lie?" in 6 to 8 sentences. Include:

  • your view
  • one religious or philosophical view that agrees with you
  • one view that disagrees with you
  • why you still hold your view

Answer Key and Marking Guide

Task A:

  1. Hindu Mundaka Upanishad
  2. Utilitarianism
  3. Ten Commandments
  4. Guru Nanak
  5. Talmud
  6. Kant
  7. Buddhism
  8. Islam (hadith)

Task B: model reasons (accept any well-reasoned answer).

  1. *Drawing.* "Tell a kind truth. Say what you like about it and suggest one improvement. That fits Buddhist Right Speech, which is truthful and kind." A good opposing answer: "A small white lie protects their feelings and does no harm (utilitarian)."
  2. *Angry stranger.* Many students will say lie or refuse to answer to protect the classmate. That links to Rahab and the midwives, and to utilitarian thinking. Strong answers notice that Kant would not lie, but you could stay silent or get an adult. Silence isn't a lie.
  3. *Surprise party.* Many say the lie is harmless and done for the person's benefit. Others say "You'll have to wait and see" avoids lying altogether. Credit students who spot that avoiding the question is different from lying.
  4. *Copied homework.* Most strong answers say don't lie to the teacher. A false statement here protects wrongdoing rather than preventing harm, and it could be unfair to the students who did their own work. Credit answers that weigh loyalty against honesty.

Task C: what a strong paragraph looks like.

  • Basic: gives an opinion with one reason.
  • Good: names a specific religious or philosophical view correctly and links it to their opinion.
  • Strong: also describes an opposing view fairly and explains why they still disagree, for example: "Kant says lying is always wrong because trust would collapse, but I think saving a life matters more than one broken rule."

Example strong answer: "I think lying is usually wrong but can be right to protect someone from serious harm. The Ten Commandments and the Buddhist Five Precepts both teach that truthfulness matters, and I agree that trust depends on it. But in the Bible, the midwives misled Pharaoh to save babies, and a utilitarian would say that was right because it prevented death. Kant would disagree, because he thought even one lie damages trust for everyone. I still think a life matters more than a rule. So lying is only right when the harm it prevents is much bigger than the harm the lie causes, and that's rare."

Quick Tips for Leaving This as Cover Work

  • Write the timings on the board so the substitute doesn't have to keep checking this sheet.
  • If the period is short (40 minutes), cut Task C and set it for homework.
  • If the period is long (60 minutes or more), add a class debate: YES on one side of the room, NO on the other, IT DEPENDS in the middle. Students can move if they change their mind.
  • Ask the substitute to note any students who argued especially well. That's useful for your assessment records.
