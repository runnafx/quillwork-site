Page 1: Cover supervisor script (60 minutes)

Before students arrive: put the information sheet and task sheet on each desk, face down. Write on the board: "River landforms: read, answer, then exam question. Silent individual work."

  1. 0–5 min: Settle and register. Say: "Today's work is set by your Geography teacher. It's exam practice for Paper 1. Work on your own and in silence. Everything you need is on the sheets."
  2. 5–20 min: Reading. Say: "Turn over the information sheet. Read it all and underline every key term in bold. You have 15 minutes." Walk the room. Anyone who finishes early can start Task A.
  3. 20–35 min: Task A (knowledge check). Say: "Answer the questions in full sentences. Use the information sheet." Students may use it.
  4. 35–40 min: Task B (sequence the diagram). Short and quick to finish.
  5. 40–55 min: Task C (6-mark exam question). Say: "This is exam conditions. Turn the information sheet face down. You have 12 minutes to plan and write." At 52 minutes say: "Three minutes left."
  6. 55–60 min: Self-check and collect. Read out the "What a good answer includes" list from the mark scheme. Students tick each point they included in a different colour. Then collect the task sheets and any lined paper, with names on everything.

Behaviour notes: reading in silence is the hardest part for some students. If someone says "we haven't done this", reply: "Do your best from the sheet. Your teacher wants to see what you can work out." Do not let students work in pairs on Task C.

Leave for the teacher: a list of absent students, anyone who did little or no work, and any questions students kept asking.

Page 2: Student information sheet

How rivers shape the land

Rivers erode, transport and deposit material. Erosion happens through four processes:

  • Hydraulic action: the force of the water hits the bed and banks and forces air into cracks, which weakens the rock.
  • Abrasion: rocks carried by the river scrape against the bed and banks and wear them away.
  • Attrition: rocks carried by the river knock into each other and become smaller and more rounded.
  • Solution: some rocks, such as limestone, are dissolved by slightly acidic water.

In the upper course, vertical erosion is dominant: the river cuts down into its bed. In the middle and lower course, lateral erosion (sideways erosion) and deposition become more important.

Waterfalls and gorges (upper course)

A waterfall forms where a band of hard (resistant) rock lies on top of soft (less resistant) rock. The soft rock erodes faster, so a step forms in the river bed. As water falls over the step, hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft rock underneath. This creates an overhang of hard rock. Swirling water and rocks wear out a deep plunge pool at the bottom. In time the overhang has nothing holding it up and it collapses. The fallen rocks add to abrasion in the plunge pool. Each collapse moves the waterfall a little further upstream. Over thousands of years this retreat leaves a steep-sided valley called a gorge.

Example: High Force on the River Tees in County Durham. Here the river drops around 21 metres over a hard layer of igneous rock called the Whin Sill, which lies above softer rock layers. A gorge has formed downstream of the falls as the waterfall has retreated.

Meanders and ox-bow lakes (middle and lower course)

A meander is a bend in a river. Water flows fastest on the outside of the bend, where the channel is deeper. This causes lateral erosion and forms a steep river cliff. On the inside of the bend the water is slower and shallower, so it loses energy and deposits sand and gravel. This builds a gently sloping slip-off slope.

Over time erosion on the outer bends brings two bends closer together, and the neck of the meander gets narrower. During a flood the river may cut through the neck. Water then takes the shorter, straighter route. Deposition seals off the old bend, which leaves an ox-bow lake.

Floodplains and levees (lower course)

A floodplain is the wide, flat land on either side of a river that floods. When the river spills over its banks, the water slows down and loses energy, so it deposits sediment (alluvium). The coarsest, heaviest material is dropped first, right next to the channel. After many floods this builds raised banks called levees. Finer material is carried further and spread across the floodplain.

Key terms to learn

Vertical erosion · lateral erosion · hydraulic action · abrasion · overhang · plunge pool · gorge · river cliff · slip-off slope · neck · ox-bow lake · floodplain · levee · deposition

Page 3: Student task sheet

Task A: Knowledge check (use the information sheet)

  1. Define lateral erosion. (1 mark)
  2. Describe the difference between abrasion and attrition. (2 marks)
  3. Explain why a waterfall needs both hard and soft rock to form. (2 marks)
  4. Explain why water deposits material on the inside of a meander bend. (2 marks)
  5. Name a UK waterfall and the river it is on. (1 mark)
  6. Explain why the coarsest sediment is deposited closest to the river channel during a flood. (2 marks)

Task B: Sequence the formation of an ox-bow lake

Put these stages in the correct order (write the letters):

  • A: The river floods and cuts through the narrow neck.
  • B: Erosion on the outside bends brings two bends closer together.
  • C: Deposition seals off the old meander, leaving an ox-bow lake.
  • D: Water flows fastest on the outside of a meander bend.
  • E: The neck of land between the bends becomes very narrow.

Extension: draw a labelled sketch for each stage.

Task C: Exam-style question (information sheet face down)

Explain how a waterfall and gorge are formed. Use a labelled diagram in your answer. (6 marks)

Planning tip: think in this order: rock types, then erosion processes, then overhang, then collapse, then retreat, then gorge.

Task A answers

  1. Erosion that wears away the sides (banks) of the river channel (sideways erosion).
  2. Abrasion is the load scraping or grinding against the bed and banks (1). Attrition is pieces of load knocking into each other and becoming smaller and rounder (1).
  3. The soft rock erodes faster than the hard rock (1), so a step or overhang forms where the hard rock is left sticking out (1).
  4. The water is slower and shallower on the inside bend (1), so it has less energy and cannot carry its load (1).
  5. High Force, River Tees (accept other correct UK examples the class has studied).
  6. When the water leaves the channel it slows down and loses energy (1). Heavy material needs the most energy to carry, so it is dropped first (1).

Task B answer

D, B, E, A, C

Task C: Levels mark scheme (6 marks)

This follows the levels structure GCSE Geography uses for extended answers. It is an original question written in that style. It is not a past-paper question.

  • Level 3 (5–6 marks): Clear, logical sequence of formation. Accurate use of processes (hydraulic action and/or abrasion). Explains undercutting, the overhang, collapse and the upstream retreat that forms the gorge. The diagram is accurate and labelled.
  • Level 2 (3–4 marks): Some sequence and some processes named, but the explanation is partly missing or unclear. For example, describes the overhang collapsing but does not explain how the gorge forms.
  • Level 1 (1–2 marks): Basic statements, such as "water falls over hard rock", with little explanation or sequence.
  • 0 marks: Nothing worth credit.

What a good answer includes (read this out for self-checking)

  • Hard rock on top of soft rock
  • Soft rock eroded faster by hydraulic action and abrasion
  • Undercutting creates an overhang
  • Plunge pool deepened by swirling water and rocks
  • Overhang collapses because it has nothing holding it up
  • Waterfall retreats upstream
  • Repeated retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge
  • Labelled diagram (hard rock, soft rock, overhang, plunge pool, gorge)

How to adapt this lesson in five minutes

  • Year 11 near mocks: cut Task A down to questions 3, 4 and 6, and give Task C 15 minutes.
  • Lower-attainment groups: let students keep the information sheet face up for Task C and give them the planning order as sentence starters.
  • Higher-attainment groups: add a second question: "Explain the formation of levees" (4 marks). Expect sediment sorting and repeated flooding.
  • Your class studied coasts instead of rivers: keep the same structure (script, information sheet, Tasks A to C, levels mark scheme) and swap in a coastal landform such as wave-cut platforms or spits.
  • Marking load: mark only Task C. The self-check at the end already gives students feedback on it.
