Year 9 WWI Trenches Cover Lesson: No-Tech Plan to Print

A complete 55-minute cover lesson on life in the First World War trenches. It includes a script for the cover supervisor, a reading, source questions, an extension task and answers, so anyone can run it with no laptop or projector.

If you need cover work for a Year 9 history class and the person covering isn't a historian, this page has everything. Print it, or copy the student sections onto a sheet, and the lesson is ready. It runs for about 55 minutes and needs only paper and pens.

Lesson question: What was life really like for soldiers in the trenches of the Western Front?

By the end, students should be able to:

  • describe the main parts of a trench system
  • explain at least three dangers or hardships soldiers faced
  • use a primary source to support a point, and say what its limits are

What the Cover Supervisor Needs

  • One copy of the student sheet per student (the Reading, Source and Tasks sections below)
  • Lined paper or exercise books
  • A board or flip chart for writing the lesson question (useful but not essential)

You don't need any history knowledge. Answers are at the bottom of this page.

Cover Supervisor Script and Timings

0-5 minutes: settle and starter Write the lesson question on the board. Say: *"Today's lesson is about soldiers' lives in the First World War trenches. Write today's date and the question. Then write down three words you think of when you hear 'trenches'. You have three minutes."* Take four or five answers and don't correct them yet. Say: *"By the end of the lesson we'll know which of those are accurate."*

5-20 minutes: reading and Task 1 Hand out the sheets. Say: *"Read the information silently, then answer Task 1 in full sentences."* If students struggle with reading, pair them up to read a paragraph each.

20-35 minutes: source work (Task 2) Read the poem extract aloud yourself, or ask a confident reader to do it. Say: *"Answer Task 2 on your own. You'll need to use evidence from the source, so quote the words."*

35-50 minutes: extended writing (Task 3) Say: *"This is the main piece of work. Aim for at least two paragraphs."* Students who finish early move on to the Extension.

50-55 minutes: plenary Go back to the starter words. Ask: *"Which of your words were right? What would you add now?"* Take three answers, then collect the work or make sure it's in books.

Behaviour tips

  • Silent work is fine for Tasks 1 and 3. Allow quiet pair talk for Task 2 only.
  • If a student says "I don't get it", point them to the sentence starters in Task 3.
  • Write names of students who finish everything on the board for the class teacher.

Student Sheet: Reading

Life in the Trenches, 1914-1918

By the end of 1914, the war on the Western Front had become stuck. Both sides dug long lines of trenches stretching more than 400 miles, from the Belgian coast to the Swiss border. The land between the enemy lines was called No Man's Land. It was full of barbed wire, shell craters and mud.

A trench system had several lines. The front line faced the enemy. Behind it were support and reserve trenches, linked by communication trenches that carried men, food and supplies forward. Trenches were dug in a zig-zag pattern so that an enemy who got in couldn't fire straight along the whole line. Soldiers stood on a fire step to look or shoot over the top, and walked on wooden duckboards laid on the muddy floor.

Soldiers didn't stay in the front line all the time. Units rotated: a period in the front line, then time in support, reserve or rest areas behind the lines. Much of daily life was boring routine. Soldiers repaired trenches, fetched supplies, did sentry duty and stood ready at dawn and dusk, when attacks were most likely. This was called "stand-to".

Conditions were often grim. Trenches flooded, and standing for days in cold water caused trench foot, a painful condition that could lead to amputation if it became infected. Officers checked feet, and soldiers were told to change into dry socks and rub whale oil into their feet. Rats fed on food scraps and rubbish, and lice lived in the seams of uniforms and spread an illness called trench fever.

The biggest dangers were enemy weapons. Artillery shells caused most of the deaths and injuries. Snipers shot anyone who raised their head above the parapet. From 1915 both sides used poison gas: first chlorine, then phosgene, then mustard gas, which burned the skin and eyes. Gas masks improved over the war, but gas attacks still caused panic. Big attacks across No Man's Land could be deadly. On the first day of the Battle of the Somme, 1 July 1916, the British Army suffered close to 60,000 casualties (killed, wounded, missing or captured).

Student Sheet: Source

Source A: an extract from the poem *Dulce et Decorum Est* by Wilfred Owen, a British officer who served on the Western Front. He wrote it in 1917-1918 and was killed in action in November 1918, a week before the war ended.

> Bent double, like old beggars under sacks, > Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge, > ... > Gas! GAS! Quick, boys! – An ecstasy of fumbling, > Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time;

Student Sheet: Tasks

Task 1: Check your understanding (answer in full sentences)

  1. What was No Man's Land?
  2. Name the three main lines of a trench system.
  3. Why were trenches dug in a zig-zag pattern?
  4. What was "stand-to" and why did it happen at dawn and dusk?
  5. Give two ways soldiers tried to prevent trench foot.
  6. What caused the most deaths and injuries in the trenches?

Task 2: Source work

  1. Pick out two words or phrases from Source A that show how the soldiers felt physically. Explain what each one suggests.
  2. What does the second part of the extract tell us about gas attacks?
  3. Wilfred Owen was a soldier who served on the Western Front. Give one reason this makes Source A useful to a historian.
  4. Give one reason why a historian should be careful when using a poem as evidence.

Task 3: Extended writing *"The worst thing about the trenches was the enemy's weapons." How far do you agree?*

Write at least two paragraphs. Use these starters if you need them:

  • *One reason to agree is... because...*
  • *However, soldiers also suffered from... For example...*
  • *Overall, I think... because...*

Extension (if you finish early) Write a letter home from a soldier who has just come out of the front line. Include at least five facts from the reading. Remember that letters were censored, so your soldier can't say exactly where he is.

Answers for the Cover Supervisor

Task 1

  1. The land between the two sides' front-line trenches, full of barbed wire, craters and mud.
  2. Front line, support and reserve trenches.
  3. So an enemy who got into the trench couldn't fire straight along it, and so a shell blast wouldn't travel along the whole line.
  4. Soldiers standing ready on the fire step, because attacks were most likely at dawn and dusk.
  5. Any two of: changing into dry socks, rubbing whale oil into their feet, officers carrying out foot inspections.
  6. Artillery shells.

Task 2 (good answers will include)

  1. "Bent double" suggests exhaustion and heavy loads. "Coughing like hags" suggests illness and old age even though the soldiers were young. "Cursed through sludge" suggests mud, misery and anger.
  2. Gas attacks were sudden and frightening ("Gas! GAS!"). Men panicked and struggled to put masks on ("fumbling", "clumsy helmets"), and only just managed it in time.
  3. He saw the conditions first-hand, so the poem comes from someone who was there.
  4. Poems are written to create feelings and may exaggerate or pick the worst moments. Owen also wanted to persuade readers that war wasn't glorious, so the poem has a purpose. It describes one person's experience, which may not be typical.

Task 3 (what to look for)

  • Agrees, with evidence about shells, snipers, gas or big attacks such as the Somme.
  • Gives the other side: trench foot, rats, lice, flooding, boredom, exhaustion.
  • Ends with a judgement. The strongest answers explain *why* one factor mattered more, for example that weapons caused most deaths while hardships affected soldiers every day.

Tips for Adapting This Lesson

  • Lower-attaining groups: set Task 1 and Task 2 questions 1-2, then the letter instead of Task 3.
  • Higher-attaining groups: add to Task 3: *"Do you think trench life was the same for every soldier, all of the time?"* This pushes them to use the point about rotation.
  • Year 8 class: this works as an introduction to the First World War, if you add a sentence at the start explaining that the war began in 1914 between the Allies (including Britain and France) and the Central Powers (including Germany).
  • Double lesson: have students draw and label a cross-section of a trench (fire step, parapet, duckboards, sandbags, barbed wire) using only details from the reading.

Leave a note telling the class teacher which tasks were finished, so they can follow up next lesson.
