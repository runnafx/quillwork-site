How to Run a Beginner D&D Night at Your Game Store: Checklist

A practical checklist and set of fill-in templates for game stores hosting new-player D&D nights. It covers sign-ups, GMs, table kits, a 3-hour session timeline and follow-up.

How to Run a Beginner D&D Night at Your Game Store

A new-player night is one of the best ways for a game store to turn curious browsers into regulars. It only works if the first session feels welcoming, finishes on time and leaves people wanting a second one. This page walks through the whole event, from the week before to the follow-up message afterward, with templates you can copy.

Step 1: Decide the Format Before You Announce Anything

Settle these questions first. Every later decision depends on them.

  • Cadence: weekly, every other week or monthly. Pick one you can sustain for at least three months. Regulars need a predictable night.
  • Session length: 3 to 4 hours is realistic for a complete one-shot with beginners, including character handout and rules explanation.
  • Table size: plan for 3 to 6 players per GM. Four or five is the comfortable middle for new players.
  • Number of tables: one table per GM you can actually rely on. Don't open seats you can't staff.
  • Price: free, a small table fee or a store credit voucher. If you charge, many stores return the fee as credit toward dice or a starter product. That gives new players a reason to browse.
  • Age policy: state clearly whether the event is all ages, 13+, 18+ or a separate kids' table.
  • One-shot or series: one-shots are easiest for drop-ins. A short series of linked sessions builds regulars but punishes people who miss a week.

Step 2: Recruit and Brief Your GMs

Your GM matters more than the adventure. A friendly, patient GM with a simple scenario beats a brilliant GM who runs long or overwhelms people.

What to look for in a new-player GM:

  • Explains rules in one sentence, then lets players try
  • Says "yes, and" or "you can try, roll for it" more than "no"
  • Watches the clock and ends on a satisfying beat
  • Notices quiet players and gives them the spotlight

GM briefing template (copy and fill in):

> Event: [store name] New Player Night > Date and time: [date], doors [time], play [start] to [hard stop] > Your table: up to [number] players, mostly first-timers > Adventure: [title], provided in print/PDF > Characters: pregenerated, level [1–3], in the table kit > Your job: teach by playing, keep the energy friendly, finish by [time] > Store support: [staff name] handles sign-ups, latecomers and any problems > Thank-you: [store credit amount / free play / other]

Step 3: Pick or Build a Beginner-Friendly Adventure

Look for these qualities in any one-shot you use with new players:

  1. A clear hook in the first five minutes. Someone asks for help, something is missing, a door has appeared.
  2. Three distinct scenes, ideally one social, one exploration and one combat. Every type of player gets something they enjoy, and everyone sees the main ways D&D is played.
  3. One combat, not four. Combat takes longer than you expect with beginners. One well-built fight is plenty for a first session.
  4. Scaling notes so the GM can adjust for 3 players or 6 without doing math at the table.
  5. Read-aloud text for key moments, so a less experienced GM can still set the scene.
  6. A real ending. New players should leave feeling they finished something.

Make it feel like your store. Many stores set the adventure in a fictional version of their own town, or start it inside a tavern or shop modeled on the store. They sometimes add an NPC based on the store mascot or owner. Players remember that, and it makes the night feel like an event they can only get at your shop.

Step 4: Build a Table Kit

Pack one kit per table in a box or folder so any GM can sit down and start.

  • [ ] Printed adventure or a tablet with the PDF
  • [ ] 6 to 8 pregenerated characters, a mix of simple classes (fighter, rogue, cleric, wizard are common choices)
  • [ ] One-page "how to play" cheat sheet per player: how to roll a d20 check, what your turn looks like in combat, what advantage means
  • [ ] 2 to 3 full sets of loaner dice, plus a few extra d20s
  • [ ] Pencils and blank index cards for notes and name tents
  • [ ] A battle map or dry-erase mat, markers and simple tokens or minis
  • [ ] A printed timeline card for the GM (see Step 6)
  • [ ] Sign-in sheet or tablet for contact opt-ins

Pregen tip: Highlight the two or three things each character does best directly on the sheet, for example "Your best attack: longsword, roll d20+5." New players then aren't hunting through a full page of numbers.

Step 5: Announce the Event and Take Sign-Ups

Event listing template:

> New to D&D? Start here. > Join us at [store name] on [day/date] from [time] to [time] for a beginner-friendly Dungeons & Dragons–style adventure. No experience, books or dice needed. We provide ready-made characters and a friendly GM who will teach as you play. Seats are limited to [number] per table. [Free / $X, returned as store credit]. Ages [policy]. Reserve your seat at the counter or by [method].

Sign-up details worth collecting:

  • Name (or the name they want on the table tent)
  • Have you played a tabletop RPG before? (never / a little / yes)
  • Coming with friends? (so you can seat groups together)
  • Anything that would make the night more comfortable? (accessibility, content to avoid)
  • Opt-in to hear about future events (only if you'll actually use it)

Seating rule of thumb: Seat friends together and spread the total beginners out. Then no table has six first-timers and a nervous GM.

Step 6: Run the Night With a Timeline

Here is a sample 3.5-hour plan. Give each GM a printed copy.

  1. 0:00–0:15 Arrival and seating. Name tents, loaner dice, a quick hello.
  2. 0:15–0:30 Choose characters. GM describes each pregen in one sentence. Players pick.
  3. 0:30–0:40 How to play in ten minutes. Roll a d20, add the number, beat the target. That's most of the game.
  4. 0:40–1:20 Scene 1, social. Meet the quest-giver, ask questions, make a plan.
  5. 1:20–2:00 Scene 2, exploration. Travel, a puzzle or a hazard. Everyone rolls at least once.
  6. 2:00–2:10 Short break.
  7. 2:10–3:05 Scene 3, combat. The big fight. GM explains turns once, then keeps it moving.
  8. 3:05–3:20 Wrap-up. Read the ending, let each player describe one heroic moment.
  9. 3:20–3:30 Next steps. Mention the next session, the starter products on the shelf and the sign-up list.

If you're running behind: cut the exploration scene short, not the ending. A rushed finish is worse than a shortened middle.

Step 7: Set Simple Table Expectations

Read these aloud at the start, or print them on the cheat sheet:

  • Everyone gets a turn to talk and act
  • It's fine to ask "what can I do?" at any time
  • The GM can pause or skip anything that makes someone uncomfortable
  • Phones down during play, except for dice apps if needed
  • Staff member [name] is available for any concerns

Step 8: Follow Up Within a Few Days

The follow-up is how one night becomes a regular group.

Follow-up message template:

> Thanks for joining New Player Night at [store name]! We hope you had fun as [character name]. Our next session is [date/time]. Reply or stop by the counter to save your seat. If you'd like to keep playing at home, we stock [starter products] and our staff are happy to help you pick dice or a first book.

Debrief with your GMs: ask what ran long, what players enjoyed and whether the pregens worked. Adjust the kit before the next event.

Quick Checklist

  • [ ] Format decided: cadence, length, table size, price, age policy
  • [ ] GMs recruited and briefed in writing
  • [ ] Beginner-friendly one-shot chosen, with three scenes and scaling notes
  • [ ] Table kits packed and checked
  • [ ] Event listed with clear "no experience needed" language
  • [ ] Sign-ups taken, groups seated thoughtfully
  • [ ] Timeline card printed for each GM
  • [ ] Table expectations ready to share
  • [ ] Follow-up message drafted and next date set

Run it the same way a few times and refine the kit after each session. You'll end up with a new-player night your staff can host without stress and newcomers will want to come back to.
