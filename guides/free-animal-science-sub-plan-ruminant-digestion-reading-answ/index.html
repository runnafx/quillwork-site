Timed Plan the Sub Can Read Aloud

  1. 0-5 minutes: Attendance and start. Take attendance and hand out the reading. Say: "Today you are learning why different farm animals need different feed. You will read, fill in a chart and answer questions. Your teacher will collect your work."
  2. 5-10 minutes: Warm-up. Write on the board: "A cow can live on grass. Could you? Why or why not?" Give students 3 minutes to write, then ask two or three to share. No one needs the right answer yet.
  3. 10-22 minutes: Reading. Students read silently, or volunteers read one paragraph each.
  4. 22-30 minutes: Comparison chart. Students complete the chart on their own paper.
  5. 30-45 minutes: Questions. Students answer Questions 1-10.
  6. 45-50 minutes: Exit ticket and collect. Collect every paper and leave the stack on the teacher's desk.

A note for the sub: You do not need to know this topic. If a student asks something you can't answer, say, "Write that on the back of your paper for your teacher."

Student Reading: One Stomach or Four Compartments?

Farm animals eat very different diets because their digestive systems work differently. Most can be placed in one of two groups: ruminants and monogastrics.

Ruminants include cattle, sheep and goats. A ruminant has one stomach divided into four compartments. Much of a grass plant is cellulose, a tough fiber that the animal's own enzymes cannot break down. Billions of microbes living in the stomach can do it for them.

Food travels through these compartments:

  • Rumen: the largest compartment. It works like a fermentation vat, where microbes break down fiber. The fermentation produces fatty acids that the animal absorbs for energy. It also produces gas, which the animal belches out.
  • Reticulum: a compartment with a honeycomb-patterned lining. It works closely with the rumen and catches heavy objects the animal swallows by accident, such as bits of wire.
  • Omasum: a compartment with many folds that absorbs water and some nutrients.
  • Abomasum: the "true stomach." It uses acid and enzymes, much like a human stomach.

Ruminants swallow food quickly and bring it back up later to chew again. This re-chewed food is called cud, and the process is called rumination. Chewing breaks the fiber into smaller pieces so microbes can reach more of it.

A newborn calf is a special case. Its rumen is not developed yet, so a fold called the esophageal groove sends milk straight toward the abomasum. The rumen develops as the calf begins to eat solid feed.

Monogastrics have one simple stomach. Pigs and humans are examples. Food is broken down by acid and enzymes, then nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine. Pigs can digest only a limited amount of fiber, so their diets are usually built on concentrated feeds such as grain and soybean meal.

Poultry are monogastric too, but birds have no teeth and have some unique parts. The crop stores food. The proventriculus adds acid and enzymes. The muscular gizzard grinds food, often with help from small stones called grit.

Horses are an interesting exception. A horse has a single stomach but eats forage such as grass and hay. Microbes ferment fiber in its large cecum and large intestine, after the small intestine. Because of this, a horse is called a hindgut fermenter.

These differences matter. Ruminants can turn grass and other forages into meat and milk, including on land too rough or dry for growing crops. Pigs and poultry need higher-quality feed, but it is often easier for them to digest. Feeding an animal well starts with knowing how its digestive system works.

Comparison Chart

Copy this chart onto your paper and fill it in. Make three columns with these headings: Ruminant, Pig, Chicken. Make these five rows:

  • Two example species, or "only one" if the column is one species
  • Number of stomachs or compartments
  • Main way food is broken down
  • Typical diet
  • One unique body part

Student Questions

  1. Name the four compartments of a ruminant's stomach in the order given in the reading.
  2. What is cellulose, and why can't a cow digest it without help?
  3. What is the job of the rumen?
  4. Which compartment is called the "true stomach"?
  5. What is cud? Why does chewing it help the animal?
  6. How does a newborn calf digest milk if its rumen is not developed?
  7. Why are grain and soybean meal common in pig diets?
  8. A chicken has no teeth. Explain how its body breaks down hard feed.
  9. How is a horse similar to a cow, and how is it different? Use complete sentences.
  10. A rancher owns dry, rocky land that cannot grow crops, but grass grows there. Would you recommend cattle or pigs? Explain using facts from the reading.

Exit ticket: In one sentence, answer the warm-up again. Why can a cow live on grass when you can't?

Early finisher: Draw the path of grass through a cow from mouth to small intestine. Label each compartment, and use an arrow to show where cud comes back up.

If you have a 90-minute block: Students write a one-page letter to a new farmer explaining why pigs and sheep should not be fed the same way. The letter must use at least six bold vocabulary words from the reading.

Answer Key

Chart (sample answers)

  • Examples: Ruminant: any two of cattle, sheep, goats. Pig: only one. Chicken: only one.
  • Stomachs: Ruminant: one stomach with four compartments. Pig: one simple stomach. Chicken: one digestive tract with a crop, proventriculus and gizzard (accept "one" or "monogastric").
  • Breakdown: Ruminant: microbial fermentation, then acid and enzymes. Pig: acid and enzymes. Chicken: acid and enzymes, plus grinding in the gizzard.
  • Diet: Ruminant: grass and other forages. Pig: concentrated feeds such as grain and soybean meal. Chicken: accept grain or prepared feed, since the reading says only that poultry need higher-quality feed.
  • Unique part: Ruminant: any compartment. Pig: accept "simple stomach." Chicken: crop, proventriculus or gizzard.

Questions

  1. Rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum.
  2. Cellulose is a tough fiber in plants. A cow's own enzymes cannot break it down, so microbes in the stomach do it.
  3. It works like a fermentation vat where microbes break down fiber. The fatty acids produced are absorbed for energy.
  4. The abomasum.
  5. Cud is swallowed food brought back up to chew again. Chewing makes smaller pieces, so microbes can reach more of the fiber.
  6. The esophageal groove sends milk straight toward the abomasum.
  7. Pigs digest only a limited amount of fiber, so they need concentrated feeds.
  8. The crop stores the feed, the proventriculus adds acid and enzymes, and the gizzard grinds it, often with grit.
  9. Both eat forage and rely on microbes to ferment fiber. A cow ferments it in the rumen before the small intestine. A horse has a single stomach and ferments it in the cecum and large intestine after the small intestine.
  10. Cattle. They are ruminants, so microbes let them turn grass into meat and milk on land that can't grow crops. Pigs digest little fiber and would need grain and soybean meal brought in.

Exit ticket (sample answer): A cow has rumen microbes that break down the cellulose in grass, and humans don't have that system.
