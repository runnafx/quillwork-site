How to Run a Christmas Quiz Night: Host Script + Free Round

A step-by-step plan for hosting a Christmas quiz at work, at home, in a pub or for a PTA. It includes timings, rules to read out, a marking system, a free 10-question round and tiebreakers.

A Christmas quiz rarely goes wrong because the questions are too hard. It goes wrong because it runs long, the marking drags, or two tables end up arguing over an answer. This page gives you a plan that avoids all three, plus a free round you can use as it stands.

Step 1: Work out how many rounds you have time for

Allow about 45 seconds per question, which covers reading it twice and giving teams time to write. Then add about 3 minutes of marking per round. A 10-question round takes roughly 11 minutes. These are planning figures, so round up if your crowd is chatty.

  • 30 minutes (lunch break): welcome 3 minutes, 2 rounds, results 5 minutes
  • 60 minutes (end of a party): welcome 5 minutes, 4 rounds, results 6 minutes, 5 minutes spare
  • 90 minutes (a good default): welcome 5 minutes, 3 rounds, break 10 minutes, 3 rounds, results and tiebreaker 9 minutes
  • 2 hours (pub, club or fundraiser): welcome 5 minutes, 4 rounds, break 15 minutes, 4 rounds, results and tiebreaker 12 minutes

If you're unsure, plan fewer rounds. Nobody complains that a quiz finished ten minutes early.

Step 2: Choose the rounds

Variety matters more than difficulty. A mixed room usually has someone who knows films, someone who knows carols and someone who just likes puzzles, so give each of them a round.

A balanced six-round quiz looks like this:

  1. Christmas around the world
  2. Films and TV
  3. Songs and carols
  4. Food and drink
  5. Winter science and nature
  6. Wordplay and anagrams

Put an easy round first so every table scores early. Save the hardest round for just after the break, when people are settled and refreshed.

Step 3: Check every question for arguments

Run each question through this list before the night:

  • Does it have only one correct answer?
  • Could a reasonable person give a different answer and be right? If so, reword it or write down what you'll accept.
  • Is it based on something that could have changed, such as a record or "the most recent" anything? If so, replace it or check it again.
  • Does it depend on where people grew up? Christmas traditions vary, especially between the UK and US.
  • Will anyone feel singled out? Avoid jokes about colleagues, and remember that not everyone in the room celebrates Christmas.
  • Have you looked up the answer in a reliable source? Quiz sheets passed around online can repeat the same mistakes.

Step 4: Get your kit ready

  • One copy of the questions and answers for you, kept where no one can read them
  • Answer sheets for each team, plus a few spares
  • At least two pens per table
  • A scoreboard, whether that's a flipchart, a whiteboard or a sheet of paper
  • A timer on your phone
  • A small prize, plus a joke prize for last place
  • A microphone if the room is big or noisy

Two practical notes. Stand where the farthest table can hear you and test your voice before you begin. If you're at a pub or public venue and want to play music clips, ask the venue whether its music licence covers them.

Step 5: Set the teams

Teams of 4 to 6 work well in most rooms. Fewer than 4 can feel exposed, and with more than 6, some people stop taking part. At work, consider mixing departments so that one team doesn't hold all the long-serving staff. At a family gathering, split the generations across teams so that children and grandparents each have something to offer.

Step 6: Read the rules aloud

Use this script or adapt it:

"Welcome, everyone, and thanks for coming. Before we start, a few rules. There are [number] rounds of 10 questions. I'll read each question twice, and I can't go back once we've moved on. Phones away, please: any team caught looking something up loses that round's points. Spelling doesn't matter as long as the answer is clearly right. Each team has one Joker, which doubles your score for one round. Write the round number on your sheet before we begin. And my decision is final, even when I'm wrong. Good luck!"

Step 7: Mark quickly with swap-and-mark

Marking every sheet yourself is the slowest way to run a quiz. Do this instead:

  1. At the end of each round, each team passes its sheet to the team on its left.
  2. You read out the answers, and teams mark the sheet in front of them.
  3. Teams write the total at the top and return the sheet.
  4. A helper collects the scores or you call for them table by table.
  5. Update the scoreboard, and read out the top three at the break to build some tension.

If an answer is disputed, decide once, say why, and apply the same ruling to every team. Consistency matters more than getting every borderline call perfect.

Free round: a 10-question Christmas mix

Read each question twice.

  1. Christmas Island, in the Indian Ocean, is a territory of which country?
  2. Which English poet wrote the words of "In the Bleak Midwinter"?
  3. In *A Christmas Carol*, what is the name of Bob Cratchit's youngest son?
  4. Which actor plays the father hunting for a Turbo-Man toy in the 1996 film *Jingle All the Way*?
  5. The Three Wise Men brought gold, frankincense and which other gift?
  6. In the carol, which town is described as "little"?
  7. Which nut is the main ingredient of marzipan?
  8. The bright red "petals" of which Christmas plant are actually leaves?
  9. Rearrange the letters of SATAN to make the name of a Christmas visitor.
  10. In the poem that begins "'Twas the night before Christmas", which creature was not stirring?

Answers

  1. Australia
  2. Christina Rossetti
  3. Tiny Tim (accept "Tim")
  4. Arnold Schwarzenegger
  5. Myrrh
  6. Bethlehem
  7. Almond
  8. Poinsettia
  9. Santa
  10. A mouse

We wrote these questions with AI help, using well-known facts. Please look up anything you're unsure of before the night, as you would with any quiz you didn't write yourself.

Three tiebreakers

Ask both teams to write down a number. The closest answer wins.

  1. In what year was Charles Dickens's *A Christmas Carol* first published? (1843)
  2. In what year was the film *It's a Wonderful Life* released? (1946)
  3. In a non-leap year, counting 1 January as day 1, what day of the year is Christmas Day? (359)

Have all three ready. The first tiebreak can end in another tie.

Adapting the quiz for your group

  • Office party: keep it to 2 to 4 rounds, and avoid anything alcohol-heavy if colleagues don't drink. Pick a time when people on shifts or remote workers can join.
  • Family at home: let younger children have one "ask a grown-up" per round. Play in pairs rather than teams if there are fewer than eight of you.
  • Pub or club: use a microphone, hold a proper break so people can buy drinks, and announce the running order at the start.
  • PTA or church fundraiser: decide your entry fee and prize in advance. If you add a raffle, check the current rules for your country and venue first.
  • Last lesson of term: one or two rounds fit a single lesson. Check that the content suits the age group, and swap out any film the class is too young to have seen.

Night-before checklist

  • Questions and answers printed and read through once
  • Disputed answers decided and written down
  • Answer sheets counted, with spares
  • Pens on the tables
  • Scoreboard and helper sorted
  • Prizes bought
  • Tiebreakers ready
  • Running order and finish time written at the top of your host copy
