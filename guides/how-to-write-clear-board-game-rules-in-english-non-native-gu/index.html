How to Write Clear Board Game Rules in English (Non-Native Guide)

A step-by-step checklist with before-and-after examples for tabletop designers whose first language isn't English. Use it to make your rulebook easy to read, consistent and hard to misread before playtesters or backers see it.

A rulebook doesn't need beautiful English. It needs English that can only be read one way. Most rules problems in non-native rulebooks aren't grammar mistakes. They're ambiguity problems: one term used for two things, a timing word that could mean two moments, or a sentence where "it" could refer to three different cards.

This guide goes through the fixes in order, from the ones that matter most to the ones that matter least. Work through your rulebook once for each section. Every example below is generic and written for this guide.

Step 1: Build a term list before you edit anything

Make a two-column list: Game term and What it means. Put every noun and verb with a rules meaning on it: card types, zones, resources, actions and states.

  • One concept gets one word, every time. If you call it a "Hand" on page 2, don't call it "your cards" on page 6.
  • One word means one concept. If "discard" sometimes means "put in the discard pile" and sometimes "remove from the game", split them into two terms.
  • Capitalise defined game terms, for example Exhaust, Supply or Reserve, so readers can see which words have a rules meaning.
  • Check the text printed on cards and components against the list as well. Mismatches between the rulebook and the cards are one of the most common sources of rules questions.

Before: "Take 2 coins from the bank. Then pay 1 gold to the reserve." After: "Take 2 Coins from the Supply. Then pay 1 Coin to the Supply."

Step 2: Use "must", "may" and "can" with care

Players argue about these three words more than any others.

  • must = required. The player has no choice.
  • may = optional. The player chooses.
  • can = it's possible, but it's often unclear whether the action is optional. Replace it with "may" when you mean permission.
  • Don't use "should" for rules. Readers don't know whether it's a rule or advice. Keep "should" for strategy tips, and put tips in a separate box.

Before: "The player should draw a card at the end of the turn." After: "At the end of your turn, you must draw 1 card."

Step 3: Fix your timing words

Timing words often don't translate one-to-one, so check each one.

  • when / whenever: the effect happens at that moment, every time.
  • after: the effect happens once the event is complete.
  • before: the effect happens first, before the event resolves.
  • until: the effect lasts up to that point and then stops. Say whether "until the end of the turn" means your turn or the current turn.
  • then: put it between steps that happen in order.
  • instead: the new effect replaces the old one. Don't use "also" here, which means both effects happen.

Before: "When you attack, you can play a Shield card, then the damage is reduced." After: "When an opponent attacks you, before damage is dealt, you may play 1 Shield card. If you do, reduce that damage by 2."

Step 4: Get rid of unclear "it", "this" and "they"

Read every pronoun and ask what it points to. If more than one thing could fit, repeat the noun. Repetition sounds dull in fiction, but in rules it prevents misreadings.

Before: "Place the token on the card. If it is removed, return it to the supply." After: "Place the token on the card. If the card is removed, return the token to the Supply."

Step 5: Be exact about numbers and quantity words

  • up to 3 = 0, 1, 2 or 3. Say "exactly 3" when the number is fixed.
  • at least 3 = 3 or more. more than 3 = 4 or more. Many languages blur this difference, so check it carefully.
  • each / every = all of them, one at a time. any can mean "one of your choice" or "all", so pick a clearer word.
  • a / an / 1: in rules, write "1 card" rather than "a card" when the count matters.
  • Write numbers as digits in rules text ("draw 2 cards") so readers can scan them quickly.
  • Say which way to round ("round down") and how to break ties ("If tied, the player with the most Coins wins. If still tied, the players share the victory.").

Step 6: Use second person, present tense, active voice

  • Write "you" for the active player and "each player" or "your opponent" for everyone else. Don't switch between "you", "the player" and "he/she" for the same person.
  • Use the present tense: "You draw 2 cards", not "You will draw 2 cards". Look out for tense drift, where one paragraph slides from present to future to past.
  • Use the active voice: "You discard 1 card", not "1 card is discarded". The passive hides who acts, and in rules that is often the whole question.

Step 7: Put the turn structure in a numbered list

If something happens in order, number it. Each step gets one action and one sentence. Put exceptions after the list, not in the middle of a step.

Before: "In your turn you first take resources and also you can build, but if you built you can't trade, and at the end you draw."

After:

  1. Collect: Take 1 Coin from the Supply for each Farm you control.
  2. Act: Choose one: Build or Trade.
  3. Draw: Draw cards until you have 5 cards in your Hand.

Step 8: Watch for false friends

Some English words look like words in your language but mean something different. These are well-known traps:

  • actual / actually: in English this means "real / in fact". Spanish *actual*, French *actuel* and German *aktuell* mean "current". Write "current round", not "actual round".
  • eventually: in English this means "in the end, after some time". German *eventuell*, French *éventuellement* and Spanish *eventualmente* mean "possibly". Write "if needed" or "optionally".
  • sensible: in English this means "reasonable". German *sensibel* and French/Spanish *sensible* mean "sensitive".
  • assist: in English this means "help". French *assister à* means "attend" or "be present at".
  • control: in some languages the matching word also means "check". In English rules, "control a card" means "own or command it", so write "check the card" if that's what you mean.

Keep a short list of the false friends you personally tend to use and search your file for them before every export.

Step 9: Add a worked example after each complex rule

One short example often answers more questions than another paragraph of rules. Use a format like this:

Example: Ana has 3 Farms. In the Collect step, she takes 3 Coins. In the Act step, she chooses Build, so she may not Trade this turn.

Give the characters names, use the exact game terms from your term list, and check that the numbers really work under your rules.

Step 10: Run a final read-through

Go through these checks in order:

  • [ ] Every capitalised term appears on the term list, and every term on the list is used the same way throughout.
  • [ ] Every "can" and "should" has been checked and replaced where needed.
  • [ ] Every "when", "after", "until" and "instead" means exactly what you intend.
  • [ ] No pronoun has two possible meanings.
  • [ ] "Up to", "at least" and "more than" are correct.
  • [ ] The turn structure and setup are numbered lists.
  • [ ] Rounding and tie-breakers are stated.
  • [ ] Card text matches rulebook terms.
  • [ ] Strategy tips are separate from rules.
  • [ ] Your personal false-friend list has been searched.
  • [ ] Someone who has never played reads the setup section aloud and sets up the game without your help.

That last check matters most. If a new reader stops and asks "what does this mean?", mark the sentence and rewrite it using the steps above.

A note on AI tools and spellcheckers

Grammar checkers and AI assistants are good at catching spelling, articles ("a/the") and word order. They're much worse at noticing that "token" on page 3 and "marker" on page 9 are the same component, or that "can" should be "must". They can also "improve" a defined term into a synonym and break your consistency. If you use them, give them your term list, tell them not to change capitalised terms, and check every suggested change against Steps 1 to 5 yourself.

Short template: rules paragraph

Use this shape for any rule:

[Rule name] — [When it applies]. [Who] [must/may] [action with exact numbers and terms]. [What happens next / exception]. Example: [one short worked example].

Example of the template filled in:

Ambush — When an opponent moves into your Region, you may reveal 1 Ambush card from your Hand. If you do, that opponent loses 1 Unit. Then discard the Ambush card. Example: Kenji moves 2 Units into Mia's Forest. Mia reveals Ambush. Kenji loses 1 Unit, so 1 Unit stays in the Forest.
