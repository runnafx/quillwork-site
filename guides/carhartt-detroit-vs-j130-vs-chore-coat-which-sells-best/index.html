Carhartt Detroit vs J130 vs Chore Coat: Which Sells Best?

A side-by-side guide to telling the four common vintage Carhartt duck jackets apart, what each one roughly sells for, and how to set a max bid for each before you buy a single jacket or a lot.

Vintage Carhartt jackets look a lot alike in a blurry auction photo or on a thrift rack: tan or brown duck canvas, a corduroy collar, wear on the cuffs. On the resale market, though, the different models sell for very different prices. If you pay Detroit money for a chore coat, the profit on the flip is gone before you list it.

This page shows how to tell the four common models apart in under a minute, which one usually sells for the most, and how to work out a separate max bid for each.

About the prices on this page: they are rough anchors. They come from sold-listing snippets in search results and from one reseller's own sold-listing scan in early November 2026. They are not official medians and will change. Before you bid, check eBay with the Sold, Used and your-size filters on.

The quick answer

  • Detroit jacket: usually the strongest seller of the four. Lined ones bring the most.
  • J130 Active jacket (hooded): solid middle ground. Usually sells below a Detroit.
  • Chore coat: sells for less than most people expect. Price it on its own comps, never off Detroit numbers.
  • Arctic quilt-lined coat: the widest and lowest price range. After auction fees and shipping it's often a break-even.

How to identify each model fast

Detroit jacket

  • Waist length. The hem sits at the belt line, not over the hips.
  • Zip front with a corduroy collar.
  • Adjustable waist tabs on the sides, near the hem.
  • Comes blanket-lined (striped wool-blend blanket inside) or unlined. The lining changes the price a lot, so check it every time.
  • Look for "Detroit" in the listing title. Sold titles also carry style codes on the tag. Write the code down, but don't assume it means anything until you've checked it.

J130 Active jacket

  • Attached hood, usually with a drawstring.
  • Quilted flannel lining in the body. Look inside the hood and body.
  • Rib-knit cuffs and waistband. These are the giveaway compared with a Detroit's plain hem and waist tabs.
  • Zip front, waist to hip length.

Chore coat

  • Button front, not a zipper. This is the fastest way to tell it apart.
  • Longer, hip-length cut. Squarer and boxier than a Detroit.
  • Corduroy collar. Usually blanket-lined in the vintage versions.
  • Several front patch pockets.
  • Codes seen in sold titles include C001 and 6BLCT.

Arctic quilt-lined coat

  • Longer coat length, often to mid-thigh.
  • Quilted nylon lining. Shiny and puffy inside, not flannel or blanket.
  • Heavier and bulkier than the other three.
  • Codes seen in sold titles include C03 and C26.

The 30-second test: zipper or buttons? Hood or no hood? Look inside: blanket, flannel, shiny quilt or nothing? Where does the hem fall? Those four answers sort almost every vintage duck jacket.

Why the gap matters: a worked max-bid example

Use the same formula for every model, but plug in that model's own sale price. A Detroit price on a chore coat gives you the wrong max bid.

Formula:

Max hammer = (sale price x 0.864 − $0.40 − outbound shipping − target profit − inbound shipping) ÷ ((1 + buyer's premium) x (1 + card fee) x (1 + sales tax))

The 0.864 and $0.40 assume eBay's final value fee is about 13.6% plus a per-order fee. That's an assumption, so check the current fee for your category and account. If the auction charges tax, add it. If it doesn't, use 0.

Example: unlined Detroit, size M, from an online estate auction that ships

  • Conservative sale price: $95
  • $95 x 0.864 = $82.08, minus $0.40 = $81.68
  • Minus outbound shipping $12 = $69.68
  • Minus target profit $15 = $54.68
  • Minus inbound shipping quote $20 = $34.68
  • Divide by fees: 10% premium x 5% card fee = 1.10 x 1.05 = 1.155
  • Max hammer: about $30. Round down, so bid no more than $29.

Same auction, but it turns out to be a chore coat selling around $55:

  • $55 x 0.864 = $47.52, minus $0.40 = $47.12
  • Minus $12 outbound, $15 profit and $20 inbound = $0.12
  • Max hammer: effectively $0. It isn't worth bidding at these costs unless you can pick it up yourself and skip the inbound shipping.

The fees and shipping are identical in both cases. Only the model is different, and that difference is the whole profit.

Pre-bid checklist for any vintage Carhartt listing

  1. Identify the model with the 30-second test above. If the photos don't show the front closure and the inside, ask the seller or auction house, or bid as if it's the cheapest model it could be.
  2. Check the lining. For Detroits, "lined" vs "unlined" is the biggest single price factor here. If the listing doesn't say and the photos don't show it, price it as unlined.
  3. Confirm the size. "Believed medium" or no size at all means you'll measure it on arrival. Price it as the least popular size it could plausibly be.
  4. Look for damage that photos hide. Torn pockets, broken zippers, blown-out cuffs, ink names, holes at the elbows. Distressed wear is part of the appeal for some buyers, but holes and broken hardware still lower the price.
  5. Read the auction terms. Buyer's premium, card fee, sales tax, and whether shipping is in-house or through a third-party packer. Third-party packing quotes on a bulky coat can run high, so get a quote before you bid, not after you win.
  6. Confirm the close time. If the page shows two different end times, contact the auction house and use the earlier one.
  7. Run your own sold search. On eBay, search the exact model and color, then filter to Sold, Used and your size. Write down three to five recent sales and use the lower-middle one as your sale price.
  8. Work out the max bid with the formula and write it down. Don't bid above it, even in the last minute.

Mixed lots: sort before you pay

A lot of 5 to 15 vintage Carhartt jackets can be a good buy or a pile of chore coats. Before bidding:

  • Count how many in the photos are clearly Detroits (waist length, zipper, waist tabs).
  • Count hoods (J130s) and buttons (chore coats) separately.
  • Price each group with its own anchor, add them up, then subtract the fees, shipping and a per-jacket profit target.
  • If you can't tell what most of the jackets are from the photos, assume they're the cheaper models.

When each model is the right buy

  • Detroit: your main target. Bid with confidence, but only up to a max worked out from comps that match its lining and size.
  • J130: a good buy when the hammer price is low and the jacket is clean with working zippers.
  • Chore coat: buy it mainly when it's cheap, local pickup, or a special edition you've confirmed with sold comps.
  • Arctic coat: usually a pass at online auctions with third-party shipping. It can work as a cheap local buy.

Model, lining, size and condition decide the price. The orange tag doesn't. Identify those four things before you set a max bid, and you'll stop paying Detroit prices for chore coats.
