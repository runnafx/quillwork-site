How to Set KS3 Music Cover Work With No Instruments or Audio

A step-by-step method for setting KS3 Music cover that a non-specialist can run with only pens and paper. Includes task ideas, a cover note template and a complete one-hour lesson with script and answers.

Your practical scheme of work depends on keyboards, ukuleles or a working speaker. Your cover lesson may be in a Maths room, with someone who doesn't read music. Setting "carry on with the keyboard project" won't work there, and neither will "listen to this track and answer the questions" if the speakers fail.

The answer is to stop trying to continue the practical unit. Set a paper lesson on knowledge students will use when you get back: vocabulary, notation, instruments and composing on paper.

Step 1: Assume the worst room

Plan for no instruments, no speakers, no student devices and a supervisor who can't read music. If the lesson works under those conditions, it will work anywhere. Treat anything better as a bonus.

Before you set any task, ask these three questions:

  • Does any part require hearing something? If so, rewrite it or drop it.
  • Can the supervisor check every closed answer from a key, with no music knowledge? If not, add the answer or make the task open-ended and collect it for yourself.
  • Would it matter if the room next door were silent for an exam? Any clapping or tapping must be optional and have a written alternative.

Step 2: Choose a task type that works on paper

These all work without sound:

  • Elements of music: match definitions, then identify which element is changing in written scenarios ("The same tune is played on piano, then electric guitar" is timbre).
  • Italian terms: complete a table of dynamics and tempo markings, from pp to ff and largo to presto.
  • Rhythm sums: treat note values as maths. A minim, a crotchet and two quavers come to 2 + 1 + ½ + ½ = 4 beats. Students then fill four-beat bars with different combinations.
  • Treble clef code-breaker: give students the lines (E G B D F) and spaces (F A C E), then have them decode words spelled in notes, such as CABBAGE or FACADE.
  • Instruments of the orchestra: sort instruments into strings, woodwind, brass and percussion, then explain how each family makes its sound.
  • Written composing: write a graphic score, a rhythm grid or a set of original song lyrics with a marked verse and chorus.
  • Film and game music: describe the music a written scene needs, using musical vocabulary and reasons.

Avoid three things that often go wrong in cover:

  • Word searches on their own. They fill time, but students don't need to know what any of the words mean.
  • "Research a composer." Without devices, this can't be done. With devices, it tends to turn into copying.
  • Anything that depends on last lesson's practical work. The supervisor won't know where the class got to, and absent students will be stuck.

Step 3: Structure the hour so it runs itself

A simple shape for 60 minutes:

  1. 5 minutes: a starter that needs no explanation.
  2. 10 minutes: a closed task that teaches the key words.
  3. 10 to 15 minutes: a closed task that applies them.
  4. 25 minutes: one longer task with a clear finished product.
  5. 5 minutes: a written exit task to hand in.

Then add an extension for early finishers. On 50-minute timetables, mark which task to drop, so nobody has to make that decision on the day.

Step 4: Write for the person in the room

Your supervisor needs the words to say, not just the task. Include a short script, define every term on the student sheet, and give pronunciation for awkward words (timbre is "tam-bruh"). State plainly that they don't need any music knowledge. It reduces the chance of a nervous start, and the class will pick up on how the lesson begins.

Step 5: Leave a cover note

Copy this and fill it in:

  • Class / room / period: ________
  • Lesson title: ________
  • Print: ____ student sheets, 1 script and answer key
  • Clapping or tapping allowed today: Yes / No
  • Seating plan is: ________
  • Students who need particular support: ________
  • If they finish early: ________
  • Collect: ________ and leave it ________
  • If printing fails: students copy the questions from the script into their books

Worked example: "Design the Soundtrack" (60 minutes, Years 7 to 9)

Needs: pens and paper. Write the Task A and Task B text on the board or print it.

Starter (0-5). SAY: "Your Music teacher is away, so today we're working as film composers, on paper. Think of the scariest scene you've seen in a film. Write down three words describing what the music was doing." Take three or four answers.

Task A (5-15). SAY: "Match each word to its meaning, then check with your partner."

  1. Tempo
  2. Dynamics
  3. Pitch
  4. Timbre (say "tam-bruh")
  5. Texture
  6. Structure
  • A. How high or low the notes are
  • B. The order of sections in the music, and whether parts return
  • C. The speed of the music
  • D. The type of sound an instrument makes
  • E. How loud or quiet the music is
  • F. How many layers of sound there are

Task B (15-25). SAY: "A director has left these notes for a composer. Write down which word from Task A each note is about."

  1. "Speed up as the car chase begins."
  2. "Drop to almost nothing when the door creaks."
  3. "Make the melody climb higher as she takes off."
  4. "Swap the violins for a solo trumpet."
  5. "Start with one flute, then bring in the whole orchestra."
  6. "Bring the opening theme back in the final scene."

Task C (25-50). SAY: "You are the composer for a scene with three parts. First, a child walks through a quiet forest at dawn. Next, something large starts following them. Finally, they run home and reach safety. Write a plan for each part. Name the tempo, dynamics, pitch, instrument and texture, and give a reason for at least one choice."

Give students this sentence starter: "I would use ____ because it would make the audience feel ____."

Exit task (50-60). SAY: "Draw a line showing how loud the music is across your three parts, from left to right. Label the moments where it changes. Then write one sentence explaining the biggest change."

Extension. Add a short leitmotif idea: a musical signature for the creature. Describe which instrument plays it, and whether it moves up or down.

Answers.

  • Task A: 1-C, 2-E, 3-A, 4-D, 5-F, 6-B
  • Task B: 1 tempo, 2 dynamics, 3 pitch, 4 timbre, 5 texture, 6 structure

Task C and the exit task have no single right answer. A good response uses the correct words and gives a reason, such as "A low, slow cello would feel threatening because low sounds can seem heavy and dark." Collect these for the Music teacher.

To get a 50-minute version, shorten Task C to two parts of the scene.

What to do with the work afterwards

Don't set work you won't use. When you return, spend five minutes on it:

  • Read two or three of the best film plans aloud, and play a keyboard version of one.
  • Use the Task A words as your next starter.
  • Ask students to use one of the terms when evaluating their next performance.

This also gives the class a reason to take cover seriously next time.

Pre-absence checklist

  • No task requires hearing anything.
  • Every term is defined on the student sheet.
  • Every closed question is in the answer key.
  • Timings add up, with a task marked to drop for shorter lessons.
  • Any sound is optional and has a silent alternative.
  • The sheet prints clearly in black and white.
  • The cover note says what to collect and where to leave it.

If you keep two or three lessons like this with your cover manager before you need them, a sudden absence won't mean writing a lesson while you're ill.
