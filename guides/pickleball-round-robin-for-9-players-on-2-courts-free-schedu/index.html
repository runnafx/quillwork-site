Pickleball Round Robin for 9 Players on 2 Courts (Free Schedule)

A complete, checked rotating-partner schedule for 9 players on 2 courts. Everyone partners everyone else exactly once, sits out once, and faces each other player exactly twice. Includes a scoring method and a worked example.

Nine players and two courts is one of the most awkward numbers to host. Eight people fit on the courts, so one person always sits out. If you make the pairings up as you go, the same people end up partnering twice, someone sits out three times, and the group starts keeping score of the schedule instead of the games.

This page gives you a finished schedule you can copy onto a whiteboard or print. I checked it by computer, and it is fully balanced:

  • 9 rounds, 2 games per round
  • Everyone sits out exactly once and plays 8 games
  • Everyone partners every other player exactly once (8 different partners)
  • Everyone plays against every other player exactly twice

The numbers can't do better than that for 9 players. Each player has 8 possible partners, plays 8 games, and gets each of those partners once.

Before You Start: 3-Minute Setup

  1. Assign numbers 1-9. Draw them from a hat or go by sign-in order. Write each name next to its number where everyone can see it.
  2. Name the courts. Call them Court 1 and Court 2, and keep that the same all session.
  3. Pick a game format. Choose one and announce it before Round 1 (see "How long will it take?" below).
  4. Give the bye player a job. The person sitting out keeps time or writes the scores down. That keeps them involved, and you don't have to do it.

The 9-Player, 2-Court Schedule

Read each line as: partners vs partners. The "Sits out" player has the bye that round.

  1. Round 1: Court 1: 2 & 9 vs 4 & 8. Court 2: 3 & 6 vs 5 & 7. Sits out: 1
  2. Round 2: Court 1: 1 & 9 vs 3 & 5. Court 2: 4 & 7 vs 6 & 8. Sits out: 2
  3. Round 3: Court 1: 1 & 8 vs 7 & 9. Court 2: 2 & 5 vs 4 & 6. Sits out: 3
  4. Round 4: Court 1: 1 & 6 vs 3 & 8. Court 2: 2 & 7 vs 5 & 9. Sits out: 4
  5. Round 5: Court 1: 1 & 4 vs 6 & 9. Court 2: 2 & 3 vs 7 & 8. Sits out: 5
  6. Round 6: Court 1: 1 & 5 vs 2 & 8. Court 2: 3 & 7 vs 4 & 9. Sits out: 6
  7. Round 7: Court 1: 1 & 3 vs 2 & 4. Court 2: 5 & 6 vs 8 & 9. Sits out: 7
  8. Round 8: Court 1: 1 & 7 vs 4 & 5. Court 2: 2 & 6 vs 3 & 9. Sits out: 8
  9. Round 9: Court 1: 1 & 2 vs 6 & 7. Court 2: 3 & 4 vs 5 & 8. Sits out: 9

The bye is easy to remember: in Round N, player N sits out.

How to Check It Yourself

If anyone in the group doubts the schedule, here's how to check it in about a minute:

  • Byes: each number appears once in the "Sits out" column.
  • Partners: pick any player, for example 5. Their partners are 7, 3, 2, 9, 1, 6, 4 and 8. That's all eight other players, once each.
  • Opponents: player 5 faces 3 and 6 in Round 1, then 1 and 9 in Round 2, and so on. Count them and each other player shows up exactly twice.

How Long Will It Take?

The answer depends on your format and your group's level, so time a round or two and adjust. Common choices:

  • Games to 11, win by 2: traditional, but games run different lengths, and one court will often wait on the other.
  • Games to 11, win by 1 (or capped at 15): keeps rounds closer to the same length.
  • Timed rounds (for example 12 or 15 minutes): the most predictable option. When time is up, the current rally finishes and the score stands. If you have a fixed court booking, this is usually easiest.

To plan the session, multiply your round length by 9 and add a minute or two between rounds for switching. If you're short on time, play only the first 6 rounds. Players 1-6 will each have sat out once and 7-9 not at all, so tell the group that before you start, or let 7, 8 and 9 sit out first by running Rounds 7, 8 and 9 first.

How to Score It

In a rotating-partner round robin, you score individuals, not teams. Both partners get the result of every game they play.

Pick one method and announce it before Round 1:

  • Method A: Wins, then point differential. Each player gets 1 win per game won. Ties are broken by total point differential (points scored minus points allowed).
  • Method B: Total points scored. Each player adds up the points their team scored in their 8 games. This works well with timed rounds, because every point counts.

Since everyone plays 8 games, totals are fair to compare with no adjusting for byes.

Worked Example: Scoring One Player

Here is how Method A works for player 1, with made-up scores to show the arithmetic:

  • Round 1: sits out
  • Round 2: 1 & 9 beat 3 & 5, 11-7. Win, +4
  • Round 3: 1 & 8 lose to 7 & 9, 9-11. Loss, -2
  • Round 4: 1 & 6 beat 3 & 8, 11-4. Win, +7
  • Round 5: 1 & 4 lose to 6 & 9, 6-11. Loss, -5
  • Round 6: 1 & 5 beat 2 & 8, 11-9. Win, +2
  • Round 7: 1 & 3 beat 2 & 4, 11-8. Win, +3
  • Round 8: 1 & 7 lose to 4 & 5, 10-12. Loss, -2
  • Round 9: 1 & 2 beat 6 & 7, 11-6. Win, +5

Player 1 totals: 5 wins, 3 losses, point differential +12 (4 - 2 + 7 - 5 + 2 + 3 - 2 + 5).

Do this for all nine players and rank them by wins, then by differential. With a simple tally sheet, the bye player in each round can do most of this as the session goes.

What If Numbers Change Mid-Session?

  • Someone leaves partway through: with 8 players left, nobody needs to sit out. Use the leaving player's spot as a "sub slot". Whoever would have sat out that round plays in that spot instead. The schedule stays balanced for everyone else, and the sub just gets an extra game.
  • A 10th player arrives: don't try to fit them into this schedule. Finish the round, then switch to a 10-player schedule (2 courts, 2 sit out per round), or let the new player take over the bye slot each round until the next session.
  • Uneven skill levels: because everyone partners everyone once and faces everyone twice, the strongest players get spread around on their own. Don't swap pairings by hand to fix a matchup, or the schedule stops being balanced.
