How to Make a Class Storybook With Preschoolers (Free Template)

A step-by-step guide for home daycares and small preschools to write, print and assemble a class storybook that the children illustrate themselves. It includes a fill-in story template, drawing prompts, a parent note and a printing checklist.

A class storybook is a short story about *your* group: your room name, your class pet, your town and the children's own first names. Each child illustrates their own copy. It works as an end-of-year keepsake, a welcome-back activity or a holiday take-home, and the main costs are paper and printer ink.

This guide walks you through the whole process, from planning to sending it home. You can do every step yourself using the templates below.

What You Need

  • A word processor or a blank notebook to draft the story
  • Printer paper (heavier paper such as cardstock holds up better to crayons and markers, but regular paper works)
  • A stapler, hole punch with yarn or ribbon, or a binder clip
  • Crayons, markers or colored pencils
  • A list of the children's first names, if you plan to include them
  • About 2 to 4 short sessions with the children for drawing

Step 1: Pick the Occasion and the Size

Decide why you are making the book, because the occasion shapes the story:

  • End of year: a look back at things the class did together
  • Welcome back / start of year: a story about a first day, so children know what to expect
  • Holiday or season: a story about a class outing, a snow day or a celebration
  • Graduation / moving up: a goodbye story about growing up and moving on

Aim for 10 to 12 pages. That is long enough to feel like a real book and short enough for young children to finish illustrating without losing interest.

Step 2: Gather Your Story Ingredients

Fill in this list before you write. These details make the book feel like *theirs*.

  • Class or room name: ______
  • Class pet or mascot (real or pretend): ______
  • Town or neighborhood name: ______
  • 3 to 5 local places the children recognize (the park, the library, a bakery, the fire station, a river or hill): ______
  • A favorite class routine (circle time, snack, outdoor play, nap): ______
  • A favorite song, game or saying: ______
  • Something the class did this year (planted seeds, visited a farm, had a pajama day): ______
  • Children's first names: ______

On names: use first names only, and think about whether any families would prefer their child not to be named in something that goes home with other families. If you are unsure, ask. You can also write the story about "everyone in the Sunflower Room" without listing names at all.

Step 3: Write the Story Using This Template

Keep each page to 2 to 5 short lines of read-aloud text. Preschoolers enjoy repetition, so a repeated line on every page ("And off they went!") helps them join in.

Here is a fill-in structure for a 12-page "adventure around town" story. Swap in your own details.

  1. Cover: "[Room Name] and the Big Day" / "Illustrated by ______"
  2. Page 1, Meet the class: "In [Town], there is a room called [Room Name]. It is full of friends: [names]."
  3. Page 2, Meet the mascot: "[Mascot] lives in [Room Name] too. One morning, [Mascot] had an idea."
  4. Page 3, The problem or idea: "'Let's go on an adventure!' said [Mascot]. 'Let's find [goal: a lost hat, the perfect picnic spot, a surprise for our families].'"
  5. Page 4, Place one: "First they went to [local place 1]. They saw ______. But no [goal] there!"
  6. Page 5, Place two: "Next they went to [local place 2]. [Name] and [Name] ______."
  7. Page 6, Place three: "Then they visited [local place 3]. Everyone ______."
  8. Page 7, A favorite routine: "They got tired, so they stopped for [snack / circle time / a song]. They sang [song]."
  9. Page 8, A small challenge: "Oh no! [It started to rain / they got lost / Mascot ran off]. What would they do?"
  10. Page 9, Working together: "Everyone helped. [Name] ______. [Name] ______. Together they ______."
  11. Page 10, Finding it: "And there it was, right back at [Room Name]! [Goal] was there all along."
  12. Page 11, The ending: "[Mascot] smiled. 'The best adventure is with friends.' The end."

If you have many children, spread names across pages 5, 6, 9 and 10 in small groups of two or three, so nobody is left out. Count the names at the end to check.

Step 4: Write a Drawing Prompt for Every Page

Under each page's text, add a large empty box (at least half the page) and a short prompt. The prompt helps children who freeze at a blank page.

Example prompts:

  • "Draw yourself and your friends in our room."
  • "Draw [Mascot]. What color is it?"
  • "Draw what you see at the park."
  • "Draw the rain. Are you wearing boots?"
  • "Draw everyone helping."
  • "Draw your favorite part of the adventure."

Tip: Leave the box truly empty. Children's own drawings, however simple, are what make each copy one of a kind.

Step 5: Lay Out and Print

  • Use a large, clear font and keep text at the top of the page so the drawing box sits below it.
  • Print single-sided so markers do not bleed through onto the next page's text.
  • Print one copy per child, plus one or two spares for mistakes and a classroom copy.
  • Print a test copy first and read it aloud. Fix anything that is hard to say or too long.
  • Add the child's name to the cover's "Illustrated by" line, or let them write or trace it themselves.

Step 6: Plan the Drawing Sessions

Most preschoolers will not illustrate 12 pages in one sitting. A simple plan:

  • Session 1: Read the whole story aloud. Illustrate the cover and pages 1 to 3.
  • Session 2: Reread, then illustrate pages 4 to 7.
  • Session 3: Reread, then illustrate pages 8 to 11.
  • Session 4 (optional): Catch-up time for anyone who missed a day.

Reading the story before each session builds familiarity, and many children start "reading" along by the end.

For younger or less confident drawers: offer stickers, stamps, cut-paper collage or fingerprint art in the boxes. A scribble with a story behind it counts.

Step 7: Assemble the Books

Choose whichever binding suits your supplies:

  • Staples: two staples down the left edge. Fast and sturdy.
  • Yarn or ribbon: punch two or three holes and tie loosely. Children can help thread.
  • Binder clips or paper fasteners: easy to open if you want to add pages later.

Store finished pages in a labeled folder per child until binding day so nothing gets mixed up.

Step 8: Send It Home With a Parent Note

A short note helps families understand what they are holding. Copy and adapt:

> Dear Families, > > Our class wrote a story together called [Title], and your child illustrated every page of their own copy. The story features our room, [Mascot], and places around [Town] that the children know. > > Please ask your child to "read" it to you. They may remember the words, or tell you about the pictures in their own way. Both are wonderful. > > This book is a keepsake of our time together in [Room Name]. Thank you for sharing your child with us. > > Warmly, > [Your name]

Quick Checklist

  • [ ] Occasion chosen and page count decided (10 to 12 pages)
  • [ ] Story ingredients list filled in
  • [ ] Name permissions checked, first names only
  • [ ] Story drafted with 2 to 5 lines per page
  • [ ] Every child's name appears at least once (if using names)
  • [ ] Drawing prompt and large blank box on every page
  • [ ] Test copy printed and read aloud
  • [ ] One copy per child printed, plus spares
  • [ ] Drawing sessions scheduled
  • [ ] Books assembled and labeled
  • [ ] Parent note printed and attached

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Too much text. If a page takes longer than a few seconds to read aloud, cut it.
  • Tiny drawing boxes. Young children draw big. Give them room.
  • Forgetting a child. Check the name count before printing, not after.
  • Rushing the drawing. Spreading it over several days keeps it fun instead of a chore.
  • Double-sided printing. Marker bleed-through can ruin the text on the next page.
