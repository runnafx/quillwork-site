Whole Steps and Half Steps on Piano: Free Winter Worksheet + Answers

A ready-to-print winter worksheet on whole steps and half steps for Level 1 piano students. It includes a 5-minute teaching script, three short sections with an answer key, and the mistakes students make most often.

Whole and half steps are often the first lesson where students stop reading notes and start thinking about the distance between keys. Many students get this wrong in the same way: they think a "step" means the next white key. This page has a short teaching script, a winter-themed worksheet you can copy straight onto paper, and a full answer key. It suits Level 1 students, and Primer students who already know the white key names.

The two rules students need

  • Half step: go from one key to the very next key, black or white, with no key in between. It is the smallest distance on the piano.
  • Whole step: two half steps. Exactly one key is skipped in between.

That's all students need. Most mistakes come from forgetting that black keys count.

A 5-minute teaching script

  1. Snowflake hops. Ask the student to put a finger on any key and "hop like a snowflake" to the very next key on the right, black or white. Say: "That tiny hop is a half step."
  2. Two hops make a whole. From the same starting key, hop twice. Say: "Two half steps make one whole step. Count the hops out loud, not the keys."
  3. Find the snowdrifts. Ask the student to find the places where two white keys touch with no black key between them. There are only two: E–F and B–C. Say: "These are the snowdrifts. Here two white keys are only a half step apart."
  4. Test it. Play C–D, then E–F. Ask: "Which one skipped a key?" (C–D skipped C#, so it is a whole step. E–F skipped nothing, so it is a half step.)
  5. Hand over the worksheet. Students who know the snowdrift rule can usually do Part A alone.

Tip: Teach students to count hops, not keys. If they count the starting key as "1", every whole step comes out as "3" and they get confused.

Answer key

Part A

  1. E to F: H (a snowdrift, no key between)
  2. C to D: W (skips C#)
  3. B to C: H (a snowdrift)
  4. F to G: W (skips F#)
  5. C to C#: H
  6. E to F#: W (skips F)
  7. B to C#: W (skips C)
  8. F# to G: H
  9. A to B: W (skips A#/B♭)
  10. D to E♭: H
  11. B♭ to C: W (skips B)
  12. G# to A: H

Part B

  1. C → D
  2. E → F#
  3. B → C#
  4. F → G
  5. A → B
  6. B♭ → C
  7. E → F
  8. B → C
  9. D → D# or E♭ (same key, so accept either)
  10. G → G# or A♭ (same key, so accept either)
  11. D → C
  12. G → F
  13. C → B♭ (or A# at this level)
  14. F → E♭ (or D# at this level)

A note on spelling: In formal theory, a whole step keeps the next letter name. For example, a whole step down from C is written B♭, not A#. For a first worksheet it is fine to accept either name, because the student found the right key. Once they are learning scales, start asking for the "next letter" spelling.

Part C

  1. C ladder: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C. This is the C major scale, all white keys.
  2. G ladder: G, A, B, C, D, E, F#, G. This is the G major scale.
  3. Bonus: F#. A whole step up from E lands on F#, not F.

Part C shows students where major scales come from. If a student finishes it, tell them they just built two major scales from one pattern. This gets them ready for scales and key signatures.

The mistakes students make most often (and what to say)

  • Calling E–F or B–C a whole step. On paper they look like any other pair of white keys. Send the student back to the keyboard and ask: "Is there a black key between them?"
  • Counting the starting key. The student says "1, 2, 3" for C–D. Remind them: count the hops, not the keys.
  • Skipping a white key and calling it a whole step. C to E skips D, but it also skips C# and D#. That's four half steps, not a whole step. Ask them to count every key in between, black and white.
  • Thinking black keys don't count. Have them play C, C#, D slowly and say "half, half." This makes it clear that the black key is a real stop.
  • Reading steps from the staff alone. On the staff, E–F and C–D look the same: line to space, the next note up. The staff doesn't show half steps. Students have to remember E–F and B–C or check the keyboard. This is worth explaining because it is why a sharp or flat is needed in a scale.

Ways to use this page

  • In the lesson: Part A only, about 5 minutes, with the student pointing to each key on the piano before writing.
  • As homework: All three parts. Ask the student to play each answer at home and tick it off.
  • For a mixed group or a lesson right before a recital: Turn Part A into a quick game. Play two notes and students hold up an "H" or "W" card made from scrap paper.
  • For a stronger student: Ask them to build a Snowman Ladder starting on F. The answer is F, G, A, B♭, C, D, E, F. Ask why the fourth note has to be B♭ and not A#. (Each letter name is used once, and after A comes B.)

Quick checklist before you print

  • A keyboard diagram is at the top, with black keys drawn in groups of 2 and 3.
  • The student knows all seven white key names (A–G).
  • You have decided whether to accept enharmonic answers (D# or E♭) for Part B.
  • The answer key is on a separate page, so it isn't printed on the student's copy.
  • There is space for a sticker or snowflake stamp when the student checks each answer at the piano.
