Whole Steps and Half Steps on Piano: Free Winter Worksheet + Key

How to teach half steps and whole steps to beginner piano students, with a winter-themed worksheet you can copy, a five-finger pattern challenge and a full answer key.

Half steps and whole steps usually come up around Level 1. Students often learn the rule quickly and then make mistakes when a black key is involved or when they reach E–F and B–C. This page gives you a short teaching sequence, a three-part winter worksheet you can copy onto paper or a whiteboard, and an answer key.

You can teach the whole thing in about 10 minutes of lesson time, or split it over two or three weeks.

The Two Definitions Students Need

Use the same wording every time:

  • Half step: from one key to the very next key, black or white, with no key in between.
  • Whole step: two half steps. Skip exactly one key in between, black or white.

Many UK teachers and exam boards say semitone and tone for these. The meaning is the same, so use whichever words your students will see in their books and exams.

The One Rule That Prevents Most Mistakes

Most mistakes happen in two places on the keyboard: E–F and B–C. These white keys have no black key between them, so they are a half step apart, even though they look like any other pair of white keys.

Here's a quick way to remember it: the two-black-key group sits between C, D and E. The E–F gap comes right after it. The three-black-key group sits between F, G, A and B. The B–C gap comes right after that. Before the worksheet, have students put a finger on each of these "no-black-key gaps" in every octave.

A Simple Teaching Sequence

  1. Count keys. The student plays C and then C-sharp and says "next door, nothing in between: half step." Then they play C and D and say "one key in between: whole step."
  2. Find the gaps. The student finds every E–F and B–C on the piano and says "half step" at each one.
  3. Check by counting. For any pair of notes, the student counts the keys between them. Zero keys means a half step. One key means a whole step. Two or more means it's bigger than a whole step.
  4. Write it down. Use the worksheet below, with a keyboard picture on the page or the real piano beside them.
  5. Use it. Build five-finger patterns from the whole-and-half-step formula (Part C). This is where the idea starts to matter in the music they play.

Steps 1 to 3 work well at the piano. Steps 4 and 5 can go home as written work.

The Worksheet: "Snow Day Steps"

Copy the three parts below onto a page, or read them aloud and have the student write the answers. Tell students they can use a keyboard picture or the piano to check any answer.

Part A: Mitten Pairs (Half or Whole?)

Each pair of mittens holds two notes, going up from the first note to the second. Write H for a half step or W for a whole step.

  1. C to D: ____
  2. E to F: ____
  3. F to G: ____
  4. B to C: ____
  5. C to C-sharp: ____
  6. D to E: ____
  7. F-sharp to G-sharp: ____
  8. A to B-flat: ____
  9. E to F-sharp: ____
  10. B-flat to C: ____
  11. G to A-flat: ____
  12. A to B: ____

Part B: Snowman Hops

Each snowman starts on a note and hops in the direction shown. Write the note where the snowman lands. Use a sharp or flat where you need one.

  1. Start on C. Hop up a whole step: ____
  2. Start on E. Hop up a whole step: ____
  3. Start on B. Hop up a whole step: ____
  4. Start on F. Hop up a whole step: ____
  5. Start on E. Hop up a half step: ____
  6. Start on B. Hop up a half step: ____
  7. Start on G. Hop up a half step: ____
  8. Start on C. Hop down a half step: ____
  9. Start on F. Hop down a half step: ____
  10. Start on C. Hop down a whole step: ____

Tip for students: Questions 2, 3, 5, 6, 8 and 9 all use the E–F or B–C gap. Check them twice.

Part C: Sledding Pattern (Build Major Five-Finger Patterns)

Every major five-finger pattern (pentascale) follows the same path down the sledding hill:

Whole – Whole – Half – Whole

So from the starting note you go up a whole step, up a whole step, up a half step and up a whole step. That gives five notes, one for each finger.

Worked example, starting on C:

  • C up a whole step is D
  • D up a whole step is E
  • E up a half step is F
  • F up a whole step is G
  • Pattern: C D E F G

Now build these five patterns. Write all five notes:

  1. Start on G: ____ ____ ____ ____ ____
  2. Start on D: ____ ____ ____ ____ ____
  3. Start on A: ____ ____ ____ ____ ____
  4. Start on E: ____ ____ ____ ____ ____
  5. Start on F: ____ ____ ____ ____ ____

Bonus: Play each pattern hands separately. Which fingers land on black keys?

Answer Key

Part A

  1. C to D: W
  2. E to F: H (no black key between)
  3. F to G: W
  4. B to C: H (no black key between)
  5. C to C-sharp: H
  6. D to E: W
  7. F-sharp to G-sharp: W (G is the key between)
  8. A to B-flat: H
  9. E to F-sharp: W (F is the key between)
  10. B-flat to C: W (B is the key between)
  11. G to A-flat: H
  12. A to B: W

Part B

  1. C up a whole step: D
  2. E up a whole step: F-sharp (not F)
  3. B up a whole step: C-sharp (not C)
  4. F up a whole step: G
  5. E up a half step: F
  6. B up a half step: C
  7. G up a half step: G-sharp (A-flat names the same key. Accept either at this level, unless you are teaching the letter-name rule.)
  8. C down a half step: B
  9. F down a half step: E
  10. C down a whole step: B-flat (A-sharp names the same key)

Part C

  1. G: G A B C D (all white keys)
  2. D: D E F-sharp G A (finger 3 on a black key)
  3. A: A B C-sharp D E (finger 3 on a black key)
  4. E: E F-sharp G-sharp A B (fingers 2 and 3 on black keys)
  5. F: F G A B-flat C (finger 4 on a black key)

Why F uses B-flat and not A-sharp: Each pattern uses five different letter names in order (F, G, A, B, C), so the fourth note has to be a kind of B. The same rule explains why D uses F-sharp and not G-flat. Older students can learn this rule. Younger students can simply learn the answers.

Common Mistakes and Quick Fixes

  • Calling E–F or B–C a whole step. Ask: "Is there any key in between, black or white?" Then have the student touch the gap.
  • Counting the starting key. Some students count "C, C-sharp, D" and say three. Remind them to count only the keys between the two notes.
  • Thinking only black keys make half steps. E–F and B–C show that two white keys can be a half step apart. Point this out every time it comes up.
  • Writing E–F–G as "W W" in Part C from E. This happens when students learn the patterns by shape and don't check each step. Have them say each step aloud: "E to F-sharp, whole."
  • Mixing up up and down. For the "hop down" questions, have the student point to the starting key first and then move left.

How to Use This Sheet by Level

  • Primer students: Do Part A at the piano, using only the white-key questions (1 to 4, 6, 12). Say "next-door key" and "skip one key" instead of the formal words.
  • Level 1 students: Do all of Parts A and B. Send Part C home with the C example done for them.
  • Level 2 students: Do all three parts. Then ask them to build one more pattern of their own choice, starting on a black key such as D-flat, and check it with the Whole–Whole–Half–Whole formula. (D-flat gives D-flat, E-flat, F, G-flat, A-flat.)

Make Your Own Extra Questions

To write more questions in under a minute:

  1. Choose a starting note.
  2. Decide whether the answer should be a half step or a whole step.
  3. Make about one in three questions use E–F or B–C, because that's where students really show whether they understand.
  4. Check every answer on a keyboard before you print. Count the keys between the two notes: zero means half, one means whole.

Change the winter theme to fit the student. Mittens, snowmen, sleds and gingerbread all suit a two-note pair or a single hop, and none of them are religious.
