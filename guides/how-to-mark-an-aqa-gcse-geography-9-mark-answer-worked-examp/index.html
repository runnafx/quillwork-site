How to Mark an AQA GCSE Geography 9-Mark Answer (Worked Example)

A step-by-step way to mark AQA GCSE Geography 9-mark answers with levels of response, plus three sample answers to one hazards question, each with a mark and a comment. It also has a fill-in feedback template you can use on cover work or mock scripts.

Most marking time for AQA GCSE Geography (8035) goes on the 9-mark questions. They are levels-marked, not point-marked, so two teachers can read the same answer and give it different marks. This page sets out a routine you can follow every time, then applies it to one original Paper 1 hazards question with three sample answers.

The question and sample answers below were written for this page. They are not AQA past paper material. Before a mock, check the levels wording in the current AQA mark scheme for that paper, because the indicative content changes from question to question.

The short version

  • 9-mark questions have three levels: Level 1 (basic, 1–3 marks), Level 2 (clear, 4–6 marks) and Level 3 (detailed, 7–9 marks). An answer with nothing creditworthy scores 0.
  • You mark holistically. First decide which level fits the whole answer best, then decide where in that level it sits.
  • Most 9-mark questions use command words such as "evaluate", "assess" or "to what extent". For those, the top level needs a supported judgement, not just accurate knowledge.
  • If the question asks for a named example or case study, an answer without one usually cannot reach the top level.
  • Some 9-mark questions carry 3 extra marks for SPaG (spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms). The paper shows which ones. You mark SPaG separately: 3 for high performance, 2 for intermediate, 1 for threshold.

Step-by-step: marking one 9-mark answer

  1. Underline the command word and focus in the question before you read any answers. For "To what extent do you agree?", the focus is the statement, and the answer needs a judgement on it.
  2. Read the whole answer once without marking. Don't tick points as you go. Ticking pushes you towards counting points, and levels marking does not work that way.
  3. Ask three questions:
  • Is the geography accurate, and does it use the right terms? (knowledge)
  • Are the points explained and linked, using "this means", "because" or "which led to"? (understanding and application)
  • Does it weigh up both sides and reach a conclusion the evidence supports? (evaluation)
  1. Choose the level that fits best. If an answer is mostly Level 2 with one Level 3 moment, it stays in Level 2 and gets a mark near the top, usually 6.
  2. Place it within the level. If it only just meets the level, give the bottom mark. If it is solidly in the level, give the middle mark. If it nearly reaches the next level, give the top mark.
  3. Mark SPaG separately, if the question carries SPaG. A weak content answer can still earn SPaG marks if it is a genuine attempt at the question.
  4. Write one target the student can act on, not a general comment (see the template below).

The levels in plain English

Level 1 (1–3 marks): basic. Simple statements, often just a list. Points are not linked to each other or to the question. Few geographical terms. Any example is vague, such as "in a poor country". There is no real judgement, or only a bare "I agree".

Level 2 (4–6 marks): clear. Points are explained and some are linked. The answer uses relevant terms and a named example with some accurate detail. It may lean towards one side, or end with a conclusion that is not well supported.

Level 3 (7–9 marks): detailed. The explanations are developed and linked to the question all the way through. The example is used precisely, with specific facts, and is not just mentioned in passing. The answer weighs up both sides and reaches a clear conclusion that it justifies.

The same thinking applies to 6-mark questions, scaled down: Level 1 is 1–2 marks, Level 2 is 3–4 and Level 3 is 5–6.

Worked example: the question

"The effects of a tectonic hazard depend mainly on how developed the country is." Using named examples, to what extent do you agree? (9 marks)

These are the points to look for, using the comparison many AQA classes study: Chile 2010 and Nepal 2015.

  • Chile, February 2010: magnitude 8.8, a higher-income country. Around 500 people died. Strict building codes and well-organised emergency services kept the death toll down compared with the size of the quake. A tsunami caused much of the damage on the coast.
  • Nepal, April 2015: magnitude 7.8, a lower-income country. Around 9,000 people died. Many buildings were poorly built, especially in and around Kathmandu. Landslides and avalanches blocked rural roads, and the country relied heavily on international aid.
  • Points against the statement: magnitude, depth, distance from the epicentre, time of day, population density, terrain and secondary hazards (tsunami, landslides) also matter.

Check these figures against your class textbook, because sources vary slightly.

Sample answer A: Level 1, 2 marks

> "I agree because poor countries have more deaths. In Nepal lots of people died and buildings fell down. Rich countries have more money so they can rebuild. Chile had an earthquake as well. So I agree."

Why 2 marks: The statements are relevant but simple. "Rich countries have more money" is the only point close to an explanation. Nepal is named but without detail. Chile is mentioned but not used. The answer has a judgement ("I agree") but gives no reasons for it and does not consider the other side. It is mid Level 1.

Sample answer B: Level 2, 5 marks

> "I mostly agree. Chile is a higher-income country and the 2010 earthquake was magnitude 8.8, but only around 500 people died. This is because Chile has strict building laws, so most buildings did not collapse. Nepal is a lower-income country and in 2015 a 7.8 earthquake killed around 9,000 people. This was because many buildings in Kathmandu were poorly built and the government relied on aid from other countries, so the response was slower. However, other things matter too, like how deep the earthquake is."

Why 5 marks: Both examples are named and given accurate detail, and the "because" and "so" links show clear explanation. The other side is raised ("how deep the earthquake is") but not developed, and the conclusion is stated at the start without being justified at the end. That puts it solidly in Level 2. If the depth point had been developed, the answer would be worth 6.

Sample answer C: Level 3, 8 marks

> "To a large extent I agree, but development works alongside physical factors rather than alone. Chile's 2010 earthquake was magnitude 8.8, far stronger than Nepal's 7.8 in 2015, yet Chile had around 500 deaths compared with around 9,000 in Nepal. Chile's wealth paid for strict building codes and trained emergency services, so most buildings stayed standing and the response was fast. In Nepal, poorly built homes collapsed and landslides blocked mountain roads, so rescuers could not reach rural villages. This shows that development affects both primary and secondary effects. > > However, development does not explain everything. Nepal's mountainous terrain made landslides and access worse, and that would challenge any country. In Chile, much of the damage came from the tsunami, a physical factor that building codes could not fully prevent. > > Overall, development is the main factor, because it explains why the stronger earthquake caused far fewer deaths, but geography and secondary hazards made the effects worse in both places."

Why 8 marks: The examples are used precisely, with figures put to work in the argument rather than just listed. Primary and secondary effects are linked to the level of development. Counter-arguments are developed with evidence, and the final judgement follows from what came before. It is not 9 because the time of day and population density are not considered, and the point about long-term effects (recovery) is thin.

Common marking traps

  • Long but descriptive. A full page of case-study facts with no judgement stays in Level 2.
  • Generous credit for "both sides". One weak sentence starting "however" is not evaluation.
  • No named example when the question asks for one. Check the trigger wording before you decide the level.
  • Rewarding learned paragraphs. Credit only the material that answers this question.
  • Mixing SPaG into content. Give the content mark first, then SPaG separately.

Fill-in feedback template

Copy this onto a sticker or the bottom of the script:

  • Level and mark: Level __ , __ /9 (SPaG __ /3)
  • What went well: You used ____________ accurately to show ____________.
  • Even better if: Develop your point about ____________ by explaining ____________.
  • Judgement check: Your conclusion should say ____________ because ____________.
  • Redraft task (10 minutes): Rewrite your ____________ paragraph using "this means that…" twice.

Using this in a cover lesson

If you are absent, a non-specialist can run peer-marking from this page. Print the question, the three sample answers with their comments removed, and the plain-English levels. Students write their own answer in 15 minutes, put the three samples in order and give each a level, then you read out the real marks. Finally they give their own answer a level and fill in the feedback template. Collect the answers so you can moderate them when you are back.
