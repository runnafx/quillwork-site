Band Sub Plan With No Instruments: Free Grades 6-12 Lesson + Answers

A print-and-go concert band lesson that a non-musician substitute can run with no instruments, no audio and no screens. It includes a run sheet, a student reading, 12 questions and a full answer key.

If you direct a middle or high school band, you already know what happens when the sub plan says "students practice their concert music." Your substitute can't hear wrong notes or run a sectional, and 60 open instrument cases with no director in the room can turn into a broken reed, a dented bell or a noise complaint from the office.

This page gives you a complete one-period band lesson that needs no instruments, no audio, no screens and no musical knowledge from the sub. Students read a short text about how a concert band works, answer questions and finish a seating-chart task. The substitute marks it from the answer key. Copy what's below into a document, print it and put it in your sub folder.

Before You Print: 3-Minute Teacher Checklist

  • Lock the instrument storage, or write "Instruments stay in cases today" in bold on the sub note.
  • Print one reading and one question sheet per student. The reading can go in sheet protectors and be used again.
  • Leave a seating chart or a class list with photos. Band rooms with risers and no assigned desks are hard for a stranger to manage.
  • Write down one student helper by name who can show the sub where things are. Don't make that student the person who runs the class.
  • Say what happens to the finished work, for example "Collect it in the blue tray on my desk."

Run Sheet for the Substitute (45-55 Minute Period)

Read this aloud or hand it to the sub as is:

  1. 0-5 min: Take attendance. Tell students: "Instruments stay in their cases today. Today is a written lesson about how a band works."
  2. 5-8 min: Hand out the reading and the question sheet.
  3. 8-25 min: Students read "How a Concert Band Works" and answer Questions 1-8 on their own, quietly.
  4. 25-40 min: Students complete the Seating Chart Task (Questions 9-12). They may talk quietly with one neighbor.
  5. 40-end: Collect the work. Early finishers do the Extension Task. Students put the room back the way they found it.

For the substitute: You don't need to know anything about music. Every answer is in the reading or in the answer key below. If a student says "But we always play on Fridays," the answer is "Not today. Your director left written work."

Student Reading: How a Concert Band Works

A concert band is a large group of wind and percussion instruments. Unlike an orchestra, it usually has no violins, violas or cellos. Instruments in a band are grouped into families according to how they make sound.

Woodwinds. Woodwind players make sound by sending air across an edge or by making a reed vibrate. The flute and piccolo work by blowing air across a hole, much like blowing across the top of a bottle. The clarinet and the saxophone each use a single reed, a thin piece of cane held against the mouthpiece. The oboe and bassoon use a double reed, two pieces of cane tied together that vibrate against each other. The saxophone is made of metal, but it counts as a woodwind because it uses a reed.

Brass. Brass players make sound by buzzing their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece. The trumpet, French horn, euphonium and tuba change notes with valves, which send the air through different lengths of tubing. The trombone uses a slide instead. A longer tube makes a lower sound, which is why the tuba is so large.

Percussion. Percussion instruments are struck, shaken or scraped. Some play no definite pitch, such as the snare drum, bass drum and cymbals. Others play definite pitches, such as the timpani and the mallet instruments (xylophone, glockenspiel or "bells," and marimba). Percussionists often switch between several instruments in one piece.

High and low voices. Each family has high and low instruments. The piccolo and flute play high parts, and the bassoon and bass clarinet play low parts. In the brass family, the trumpet plays high and the tuba plays the lowest. Composers spread the melody, harmony and bass line across all of these.

The conductor. The conductor stands at the front on a podium. The right hand (often holding a baton) shows the beat and the tempo, or speed. The first beat of each measure, called the downbeat, is usually shown with a clear downward motion. The left hand is often used for expression, such as signaling players to get louder or softer or cueing a section that is about to come in.

Seating. Bands arrange their seating in different ways, but a common setup puts the quieter woodwinds near the front and the louder brass farther back so the sound is balanced. Percussion usually sits at the very back. Many bands also seat players by part, so first, second and third clarinets sit together.

Tuning. Before rehearsing, a band tunes so that everyone agrees on pitch. Many school bands tune to a note called concert B-flat. Players adjust by moving part of the instrument, such as pulling out a tuning slide or a mouthpiece, which makes the instrument a little longer or shorter.

Questions 1-8 (Work Alone)

  1. Name the three instrument families in a concert band.
  2. What string instruments does an orchestra have that a concert band usually does not?
  3. How does a flute player make a sound?
  4. What is the difference between a single reed and a double reed? Name one instrument that uses each.
  5. The saxophone is made of metal. Why is it still called a woodwind?
  6. How do brass players make a sound?
  7. How does a trombone change notes differently from a trumpet?
  8. What does the conductor's right hand usually show? What might the left hand show?

Seating Chart Task: Questions 9-12 (You May Work With One Neighbor)

Draw a simple band seating chart as a half-circle facing the conductor's podium. Place and label these sections: flutes, clarinets, oboe, bassoon, saxophones, trumpets, French horns, trombones, euphonium, tuba, percussion.

  1. Which sections did you put in the front rows? Use the reading to explain why.
  2. Where did you put the percussion? Give one reason.
  3. List the instruments on your chart from highest sound to lowest within the brass family.
  4. Choose one instrument that you play or would like to play. Write 3-4 sentences on what family it belongs to, how it makes sound and where it sits on your chart.

Extension Task (Early Finishers)

Design a "mystery instrument" card. Write three clues (family, how it makes sound, high or low) without naming the instrument. Swap with a neighbor and guess.

Answer Key for the Substitute

Mark each answer as correct if it contains the main idea in bold. The wording doesn't need to match exactly.

  1. Woodwinds, brass, percussion.
  2. Violins, violas, cellos (accept double bass/string bass too, though some bands include one).
  3. By blowing air across a hole (across an edge, like blowing across a bottle).
  4. A single reed is one piece of cane against the mouthpiece; a double reed is two pieces that vibrate against each other. Single: clarinet or saxophone. Double: oboe or bassoon.
  5. Because it uses a reed to make its sound.
  6. By buzzing their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece.
  7. The trombone uses a slide; the trumpet uses valves.
  8. Right hand: the beat and tempo (speed). Left hand: expression, such as louder/softer, or cueing a section.
  9. Accept woodwinds in front (flutes, clarinets, oboe, bassoon, possibly saxophones) with a reason like they are quieter, so brass go behind them to balance the sound.
  10. Accept at the back with a reason such as they are loud, they need space for many instruments, or the reading says so.
  11. Trumpet → French horn → trombone → euphonium → tuba. Accept French horn and trombone in either order, since they overlap in range. The trumpet must be first and the tuba last.
  12. Any reasonable answer that names the correct family and a sound method that matches the reading. Answers to 9-12 can differ. Give credit if the student uses the reading to explain.

Quick scoring for the sub: Give 1 point each for Questions 1-11 and 2 points for Question 12, a total of 13. Write the score at the top. The director will review the work.

Tips to Make This Lesson Run Smoothly

  • Keep one copy of this lesson in your sub folder all year. It doesn't depend on what you're rehearsing, so it works on a sick day in September or in May.
  • For a middle school class, read the first two paragraphs aloud together before students work on their own.
  • For a high school class, add one more question: "Name an instrument in your section that the reading didn't mention and explain its family."
  • For orchestra or choir, swap in a lesson about that group. A band reading confuses string players and singers.

What This Lesson Does Not Cover

This is one standalone day. It doesn't teach students to read notes on a staff, and it doesn't replace rehearsal time.
