Battle of Hastings Cover Lesson for Year 7: No-Tech Plan + Answers

A complete 60-minute Year 7 cover lesson on 1066 and the Battle of Hastings that any cover supervisor can run with paper only. It includes timings, a script, a student reading, tasks, a source question and a full answer key.

This is a full cover lesson you can print and leave for a cover supervisor who isn't a historian. You don't need a projector, laptops or a textbook. Copy the Student Reading and Tasks sections onto one double-sided sheet per student and give the supervisor the Supervisor Sheet. The Answer Key is for the supervisor or for marking later.

The lesson fits the National Curriculum KS3 study of "the development of Church, state and society in Medieval Britain 1066-1509". It works as an introduction or as a recap.

What to leave on the desk

  • One copy per student of the Student Reading and Tasks (double-sided A4)
  • Lined paper or exercise books
  • This Supervisor Sheet and the Answer Key
  • Optional: a few spare copies of the reading for students who arrive late

Supervisor Sheet: Timings and What to Say

0-5 minutes: Settle and starter. Write on the board: "Three men wanted to be King of England in 1066. Who do you think should get it: the one who was promised it, the one who was chosen, or the strongest?" Say: "Write one sentence answering this in your book. There's no wrong answer. We'll come back to it at the end."

5-20 minutes: Reading. Hand out the sheets. Say: "Read the Student Reading on your own. Underline every date you find." For a weaker group, read it aloud yourself or ask volunteers to read one paragraph each.

20-40 minutes: Tasks 1-3. Say: "Answer Tasks 1 to 3 in full sentences in your book. Use the reading to help you." Walk round the room. If students are stuck, point them to the paragraph that has the answer (the Answer Key says which one).

40-50 minutes: Source task (Task 4). Say: "This is a description of a picture from the Bayeux Tapestry. Read it carefully and answer the questions."

50-57 minutes: Plenary. Go back to the starter question. Ask three or four students: "Has your answer changed? Who do you think had the best claim, and why?"

57-60 minutes: Pack away. Collect sheets if the teacher asked for them. Otherwise students stick them in their books.

Behaviour tips: Say each task out loud as well as writing it on the board. Students who finish early do the Finish-Early Tasks at the bottom. If someone says "we've already done this", say: "Great, then do the extension task, and your teacher will see you know it."

Student Reading: 1066, the Year of Three Kings

1. A king dies with no son. Edward the Confessor, King of England, died in January 1066. He had no children, so it wasn't clear who should be king next. Three powerful men believed the crown should be theirs.

2. The three claimants. Harold Godwinson was the most powerful earl in England. He said Edward had chosen him on his deathbed, and the English nobles (the Witan) agreed. He was crowned the day after Edward died. William, Duke of Normandy (in France) said Edward had promised him the throne years earlier. He also said Harold had sworn an oath on holy relics to support him. Harald Hardrada, King of Norway, said an old agreement between earlier Viking and English kings gave him the right to rule. Harold Godwinson's own brother, Tostig, joined Hardrada.

3. The Vikings attack first. In September 1066, Hardrada and Tostig landed in the north of England. They defeated an English army at the Battle of Fulford, near York. King Harold marched his army north very quickly. On 25 September 1066, he surprised the Vikings at the Battle of Stamford Bridge. Hardrada and Tostig were both killed and Harold won.

4. The Normans land. Only a few days later, William landed at Pevensey on the south coast with an army that included knights on horseback. Harold had to march his tired army all the way south again.

5. The Battle of Hastings. On 14 October 1066, the two armies met on a hill near Hastings. Harold's soldiers stood at the top of the hill in a shield wall, a line of overlapping shields that was very hard to break. His best soldiers were the housecarls, professional warriors with big axes. William had archers, foot soldiers and cavalry (soldiers on horseback). The fighting went on for most of the day. Norman writers say that William's men pretended to run away (a feigned retreat). Some English soldiers chased them down the hill, broke the shield wall, and were then cut down. Late in the day Harold was killed. A famous picture on the Bayeux Tapestry seems to show a soldier with an arrow in his eye, but historians still argue about whether that figure is Harold and exactly how he died.

6. A new king. With Harold dead, the English army collapsed. William became known as "William the Conqueror" and was crowned King of England on Christmas Day 1066 in Westminster Abbey. England was about to change forever.

Tasks

Task 1: Quick facts (write in full sentences)

  1. Why was there a problem when Edward the Confessor died?
  2. Name the three men who wanted to be king.
  3. Where did William of Normandy land?
  4. What was the date of the Battle of Hastings?
  5. When was William crowned?

Task 2: Timeline. Put these events in the right order and add the month to each:

  • William lands at Pevensey
  • Battle of Hastings
  • Edward the Confessor dies
  • Battle of Stamford Bridge
  • William is crowned
  • Battle of Fulford

Task 3: Explain. Give two reasons why William won the Battle of Hastings. For each one, write "This helped William because..." to finish your sentence.

Task 4: Source task. The Bayeux Tapestry is an embroidery about 70 metres long that tells the story of 1066. Historians think it was probably made for William's half-brother, Bishop Odo, within a few years of the battle. One scene shows Harold, before 1066, with his hands on two boxes of holy relics, and William sitting watching him. The words above say Harold is swearing an oath.

  1. What does this scene show Harold doing?
  2. Why would William want people to see this scene?
  3. The tapestry was made for a Norman. Does that mean we should trust it completely? Explain your answer.

Finish-Early Tasks

  • Draw a labelled diagram of the shield wall on the hill, with William's archers and cavalry at the bottom.
  • Write a short diary entry as one of Harold's housecarls on the evening of 13 October 1066.
  • Extension: Who do you think had the *best* claim to the throne? Write a paragraph and include a "However..." sentence.

Answer Key

Task 1 (Paragraphs 1, 2, 4, 5, 6)

  1. He had no son or child to take over, so it wasn't clear who should be king.
  2. Harold Godwinson, William Duke of Normandy, and Harald Hardrada (King of Norway).
  3. Pevensey, on the south coast of England.
  4. 14 October 1066.
  5. Christmas Day (25 December) 1066.

Task 2 (correct order)

  1. Edward the Confessor dies (January)
  2. Battle of Fulford (September)
  3. Battle of Stamford Bridge (25 September)
  4. William lands at Pevensey (September, a few days after Stamford Bridge)
  5. Battle of Hastings (14 October)
  6. William is crowned (25 December)

Task 3 (accept any two that are well explained)

  • Harold's army was tired. They had fought at Stamford Bridge and marched the length of England twice, so they were exhausted before the battle started.
  • Feigned retreat. The English chased the Normans who were pretending to run away, which broke the shield wall that had been protecting them.
  • Different troops. William had archers and cavalry as well as foot soldiers, so he could attack in different ways.
  • Harold was killed. Once their leader was dead, the English stopped fighting as a group.
  • Good answers link two reasons together, for example: the tired army was more likely to break its formation.

Task 4

  1. Swearing an oath on holy relics, which students may describe as a promise to support William.
  2. It makes Harold look like an oath-breaker who went back on a holy promise, so William looks like the rightful king and his invasion looks fair.
  3. No, not completely. It was made for the winning side, so it may show the Normans in a good light and Harold in a bad one. It is still useful because it was made close to the time and shows weapons, armour and events. The strongest answers say it is useful but one-sided.

Tips for the Teacher Setting This

  • If you leave it as a recap, cut the reading to 10 minutes and add the extension paragraph as a compulsory final task.
  • To check understanding next lesson, ask students for the Task 2 order from memory, with sheets turned over.
  • For students who need more support, give a word bank on the board: *shield wall, housecarls, cavalry, archers, feigned retreat, oath, claimant*.
