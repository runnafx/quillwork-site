Festivals of Light Sub Plan: Free Grades 6-8 / KS3 Lesson + Answers

A complete no-tech lesson comparing Diwali, Hanukkah, Christmas and Vesak that a non-specialist substitute or cover supervisor can run. It includes a script, student reading, questions and an answer key.

This is a full 45-60 minute lesson for grades 6-8 or KS3 RE. The substitute needs no subject knowledge and no technology. Students learn about four festivals and compare what light means in each. They are not asked to take part in any religious practice.

  • Print one copy of this page for the substitute.
  • Print the Student Reading and Student Tasks for each student or pair, or display them and have students write in notebooks.

Script for the Substitute

Start (5 minutes). Write on the board: "Why do you think so many festivals use light, especially in dark times?" Say:

"Today we are learning about four festivals from four religions. We're studying what they mean to the people who celebrate them. Nobody has to believe anything or share anything personal. Take two minutes to write down one idea."

Take three or four answers. Any sensible idea is fine.

Reading (10 minutes). Students read the four sections aloud or in pairs. Say the names as follows:

  • Diwali: dee-VAH-lee
  • Diya: DEE-yah
  • Hanukkah: HAH-nuh-kah (some people begin with a throaty "kh" sound)
  • Hanukkiah: hah-noo-KEE-ah
  • Vesak: VESS-ak or WESS-ak

If you are unsure of a pronunciation, say so and carry on.

Questions (10-12 minutes). Students answer Part A alone. Read out the answers from the key.

Comparison grid (10 minutes). Students draw the grid in Part B and complete it in pairs.

Written answer (10-15 minutes). Students answer Part C alone and in silence. For a 45-minute period, shorten this to one paragraph. For 60 minutes, use the full time and add the early finisher task.

Exit question (3 minutes). Students write one thing these festivals have in common and one important difference. Collect all work.

Behaviour and Sensitive Topics

  • Keep the language descriptive. Use phrases like "Hindus believe" or "Christians celebrate" rather than "this is what happened."
  • Never single out a student. Don't ask anyone to speak for their religion. If a student volunteers something, thank them and move on.
  • Allow for students who don't celebrate festivals. Some families celebrate none of these, sometimes for religious reasons. Don't ask students what they do at home, and don't set a task about personal celebrations.
  • Don't add activities. No candles, prayers, songs, or acting out of religious ceremonies.
  • Stop mockery straight away. Say: "We speak respectfully about what people believe. Back to your work." Then follow the school's behaviour policy.

If students ask awkward questions:

  • "Is any of this true?" Say: "People have different beliefs about that. Today we're learning what these festivals mean to those who celebrate them."
  • "What religion are you?" Say: "Today's lesson isn't about me. What does your reading say?"
  • "Is Hanukkah the Jewish Christmas?" Say: "No. It has its own separate history and meaning. They just often fall around the same time of year."
  • A question you can't answer: "Good question. Write it down and I'll leave it for your teacher."

Student Reading

Diwali

Diwali is celebrated by many Hindus each autumn, usually in October or November. Its name comes from a Sanskrit word meaning "row of lights." Many Hindus remember the story of Prince Rama, who defeated the demon king Ravana and returned home with his wife Sita. People lit rows of small clay oil lamps called diyas to welcome them. Many families also clean their homes, make colourful floor patterns called rangoli, and pray to Lakshmi, goddess of wealth and good fortune. For Hindus, the lights can stand for good overcoming evil and knowledge overcoming ignorance. Sikhs and Jains mark their own important events at the same time of year.

Hanukkah

Hanukkah, also spelled Chanukah, is a Jewish festival lasting eight nights, usually in December. The name means "dedication." More than 2,000 years ago, a ruler banned Jewish practices and took over the Temple in Jerusalem. A group of Jews known as the Maccabees fought back and won. When they rededicated the Temple, tradition says there was only enough oil for its lamp to burn for one day, yet it burned for eight. Today, families light a candle holder with nine branches called a hanukkiah. One candle is added each night, lit using a ninth "helper" candle. The lights remember this miracle and are often placed where others can see them.

Christmas

Christmas is the Christian festival celebrating the birth of Jesus, who Christians believe is the Son of God. Most Christians celebrate it on 25 December, while many Orthodox Christians celebrate on 7 January. In John's Gospel, Jesus is called "the light of the world." Many churches light candles during Advent, the weeks before Christmas, and hold candlelit services. For Christians, this light can stand for hope and for Jesus coming into a dark world. Many people who are not religious also celebrate Christmas as a cultural holiday.

Vesak

Vesak, also spelled Wesak and sometimes called Buddha Day, is an important Buddhist festival, usually held around the full moon in May. Many Buddhists use it to remember the birth, enlightenment and death of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha. Enlightenment means fully understanding the truth about life and suffering. Buddhist customs vary by country, but people may visit temples, give food to others, and hang lanterns or light candles. For Buddhists, light can stand for the Buddha's wisdom and the enlightenment that drives away ignorance.

Student Tasks

Part A: Find the answers

  1. What does the name Diwali come from?
  2. Why did people light diyas in the story of Rama and Sita?
  3. What does the word Hanukkah mean?
  4. Why does a hanukkiah have nine branches rather than eight?
  5. What does John's Gospel call Jesus?
  6. Name three events in the Buddha's life remembered at Vesak.
  7. Which festival has a date that stays the same each year for most of its followers?

Part B: Comparison grid

Draw a grid with four rows labelled Diwali, Hanukkah, Christmas and Vesak. Add four columns headed:

  • Religion
  • What it remembers
  • How light is used
  • What light stands for

Part C: Written answer

"Light means the same thing in all four festivals." How far do you agree? Use at least two festivals as examples and give a reason for your view.

Sentence starters:

  • In both ___ and ___, light stands for...
  • However, in ___, light is different because...
  • Overall, I think...

Early finisher task: Design a poster explaining the meaning of light in one festival. Use only facts from the reading.

Answer Key

Part A

  1. A Sanskrit word meaning "row of lights."
  2. To welcome Rama and Sita home.
  3. Dedication.
  4. Eight candles are for the eight nights. The ninth is a helper used to light the others.
  5. "The light of the world."
  6. His birth, enlightenment and death.
  7. Christmas, on 25 December. Accept a note that many Orthodox Christians use 7 January.

Part B

  • Diwali: Hindu. Remembers Rama's victory and return with Sita. Rows of diyas. Stands for good over evil and knowledge over ignorance.
  • Hanukkah: Jewish. Remembers the Maccabees' victory, the Temple's rededication and the oil. Candles on a hanukkiah over eight nights. Stands for remembering the miracle.
  • Christmas: Christian. Celebrates the birth of Jesus. Advent candles and candlelit services. Stands for hope and Jesus as the light of the world.
  • Vesak: Buddhist. Remembers the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death. Lanterns and candles. Stands for wisdom and enlightenment.

Part C: What a good answer includes

There is no single right answer, and students can agree or disagree. Look for:

  • A clear opinion that answers the question.
  • Accurate examples from at least two festivals.
  • A similarity, such as light standing for something good overcoming darkness or ignorance. Diwali and Vesak both connect light with overcoming ignorance.
  • A difference, such as Hanukkah's lights remembering a specific historical event and miracle, while Vesak's light is connected with wisdom.
  • A reason that links the examples back to the opinion.

A sample strong sentence: "In both Diwali and Vesak, light stands for overcoming ignorance, but Hanukkah's candles are mainly about remembering a miracle, so I only partly agree."

Note for the Teacher

Ask the substitute to record who finished, who was absent, and any questions students wrote down. Those questions make a useful starter when you return, and you can use them to correct any misunderstandings. Religious practice varies within every tradition, so treat the reading as a starting point rather than a description of what every believer does.
