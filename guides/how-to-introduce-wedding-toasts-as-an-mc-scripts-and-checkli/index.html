How to Introduce Wedding Toasts as an MC: Scripts and Checklist

A practical guide for wedding DJs and emcees on running the toast segment smoothly. It includes a prep checklist, word-for-word introduction scripts, transition lines, and recovery lines for when things go sideways.

How to Introduce Wedding Toasts as an MC

The toast segment is one of the few moments when the whole room is listening and the person holding the mic isn't you. You can't control what the best man says, but you control everything around it: the setup, the introductions, the handoffs, and the recovery if something goes wrong. Guests read a smooth toast segment as a well-run wedding.

This guide covers what to collect before the day, what to check right before toasts, scripts you can adapt, and lines for handling common problems.

Step 1: Collect Speaker Details Before the Wedding

Most toast-segment problems start with missing information. Gather these at your final planning meeting or in your timeline form, ideally at least two weeks out.

Speaker info template (copy one per speaker):

  • Speaker's full name:
  • Name they want to be introduced as (nickname?):
  • Pronunciation, spelled phonetically:
  • Relationship to the couple (in their words, e.g., "groom's older brother," "bride's college roommate"):
  • Speaking order position:
  • Will they hold the mic, or use a stand?
  • Notes on paper, phone, or memorized?
  • Where will they be seated?
  • Any names in the toast you should know (deceased family members, sensitive topics)?
  • Contact number for day-of:

Also confirm with the couple:

  • The exact speaking order, and who decided it
  • Whether a parent or host gives a welcome first
  • Whether there's a guideline on length (many couples ask speakers to aim for about 3 to 5 minutes)
  • Whether there are "open mic" toasts (many MCs recommend against these; let the couple decide)
  • When toasts happen: before dinner, between courses, or after the meal
  • Whether the couple wants to say a thank-you at the end

Pronunciation tip: Ask for names to be spelled the way they sound ("Siobhan = shiv-AWN"). Say each one back to the couple out loud. If you're unsure on the day, ask the speaker directly during your check-in. They'll appreciate it.

Step 2: Pre-Toast Checklist (15 Minutes Before)

Run through this before you pick up the mic.

  • [ ] Wireless handheld mic tested at the exact spot where speakers will stand
  • [ ] Fresh batteries in, spare batteries within reach
  • [ ] Walked the room for feedback zones near speakers
  • [ ] Each speaker located, reminded of their order, and told where to stand
  • [ ] Quick mic coaching given to each speaker (see below)
  • [ ] Catering staff confirmed that glasses are filled or being filled
  • [ ] Photographer and videographer told toasts are starting in a few minutes
  • [ ] Couple seated and facing the right direction
  • [ ] Background music lowered or ready to fade
  • [ ] Lighting on the speaking area adequate for photos and for reading notes
  • [ ] Tissues somewhere near the head table (seriously)

30-second mic coaching for speakers:

  • Hold the mic about a fist's width from your mouth, pointed at your chin.
  • Keep it there the whole time, even when you look at the couple.
  • Don't walk in front of the speakers.
  • If you hear a squeal, step back toward me.
  • When you're done, hand the mic back to me, not to the next person.

That last point lets you control every transition.

Step 3: Scripts for the Toast Segment

Adjust the wording to fit your style and the couple's vibe. Brackets mark the parts you fill in.

Getting the room's attention

Fade the music first, then speak. Don't talk over a song.

Standard: "Good evening, everyone. If I could have your attention for just a moment. We're about to begin the toasts. If you don't have a glass in front of you, our servers are coming around now. Please make sure you're somewhere you can see [Couple's names]."

Relaxed: "Hi everyone! Could I borrow your ears for a few minutes? It's toast time. Grab a drink if you don't have one, and get comfortable. Some people have prepared some very nice words about [Couple's names], and possibly some embarrassing ones."

Introducing the first speaker

"To start us off, please welcome [relationship to couple], [Name]."

With a little more warmth: "Our first speaker has known [Partner name] for [length of time, if known], and I'm told they have a few stories. Please give it up for [relationship], [Name]!"

Then lead the applause, hand over the mic, and step back out of the sightline.

Between speakers

After each toast, take the mic back right away and say a short thank-you before introducing the next person.

"Thank you, [Name]. That was beautiful."

"Thank you, [Name]. I think we all learned some things tonight."

"Thank you, [Name]. Our next speaker is [relationship], [Name]."

Keep transition lines short. Don't add commentary on the toast content, especially on jokes. Your job is to keep the momentum going, not to review the speech.

Introducing a parent

"Next, please welcome a very proud [father/mother/parent] of the [bride/groom/couple], [Name]."

If the parent is hosting, you might add: "[Name] and [Name] are our hosts this evening, and [Name] would like to say a few words."

Closing the toasts

If the couple will speak: "Thank you to all of our speakers. Before we move on, [Couple's names] would like to say a few words."

If not: "Let's give one more round of applause to all of our speakers tonight. And please raise your glasses one more time to [Couple's names]!"

Then tell the room what's next: "Dinner service will continue," or "In just a few minutes, we'll be cutting the cake." Bring the music back up gently.

Step 4: Recovery Lines for When Things Go Wrong

You won't need all of these at every wedding, but have them ready.

Speaker freezes or loses their place: Step a little closer and say quietly, off-mic: "Take your time, you're doing great." If the room is silent, you can say on-mic: "Let's give [Name] a little encouragement," and start a short round of applause. It buys them time to find their notes.

Speaker gets emotional: Hand them a tissue if you're close. Don't take the mic away. If they ask you for help, say: "Take a moment. We're all here with you." Most rooms respond warmly.

Mic feedback: Calmly gesture for the speaker to step toward you or away from the speaker, and adjust gain. Don't say anything on-mic unless it keeps happening. If it does: "Sorry about that, folks, quick technical adjustment."

Speaker runs very long: Have an agreed signal set up with the couple ahead of time. If the couple wants you to step in, move into the speaker's peripheral view. If needed, wait for a natural pause, lead applause, and say: "Thank you so much, [Name]!" Approach for the mic with a smile. Only do this if the couple has okayed it in advance.

Someone unscheduled grabs the mic: If the couple has said no open mic, step in kindly: "I love the enthusiasm! We're going to keep the program moving, but please find [Couple's names] later and tell them in person." If the couple is fine with it, let it happen and take the mic back at the end.

Speaker goes inappropriate: This is a judgment call. If the content is clearly upsetting the couple or the family, wait for any break, lead applause, and take the mic back with: "Thank you, [Name]! Let's keep the evening rolling." Move straight to the next speaker or the next event.

Step 5: Help Speakers Before They Get to You

The best way to protect the toast segment is to prepare the speakers ahead of time. Many MCs send each speaker a short email around two weeks before the wedding. It confirms their spot in the order, gives a suggested length, and offers a few tips.

Short speaker email template:

"Hi [Name],

I'm [Your name] from [Business name], and I'll be the DJ and MC for [Couple's names]'s wedding on [date]. You're scheduled to give a toast. Here are a few details:

  • You'll speak [first/second/etc.], during [dinner/after dinner].
  • The couple has asked toasts to run about [X] minutes.
  • I'll introduce you as [relationship], [Name]. Please reply if you'd like that changed or if I should know a pronunciation.
  • I'll hand you a wireless mic. Hold it close to your chin the whole time.

A simple structure that works: introduce yourself and how you know the couple, share one story, say something sincere about them as a pair, and finish by raising a glass.

See you on the day, [Your name]"

Quick Recap

  • Collect names, pronunciations, relationships, and order well in advance.
  • Test the mic at the speaking spot and coach every speaker for 30 seconds.
  • Get the photographer, catering staff, and lighting ready before you start.
  • Keep your introductions warm and your transitions short.
  • Take the mic back between every speaker.
  • Have recovery lines ready, and agree on long-toast rules with the couple beforehand.

Do these consistently and the toast segment becomes one of the easiest parts of the night to run.
