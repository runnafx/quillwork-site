How to Adapt a KS3 Science Text for EAL Students (A2 and B1)

A step-by-step method for rewriting one KS3 science text at two English levels, A2 and B1, so every student learns the same content. It includes a fully worked photosynthesis example, a glossary template and question stems.

You have a Year 8 class reading about photosynthesis. Most of the class can manage the textbook page. Two students joined this term with limited English: one can put together short sentences and the other is developing well. Finding a different, easier text is not the answer, because then those two students learn different science.

A better approach is to keep the content and change the language. This guide shows you how to rewrite one text at two levels, roughly matching CEFR A2 and B1. There is a complete worked example that you can copy.

A2 and B1 in practical terms

These are working guidelines for writing classroom texts, not official CEFR rules. Change them to suit your students.

A2 (elementary)

  • Sentences of about 8-12 words, with one idea in each sentence
  • Mostly present simple. Use past simple for events that happened in order
  • High-frequency words, plus the subject words you are teaching
  • Only "and", "but" and "because" as connectives
  • No passive voice and no pronouns that refer back to something far away ("this", "which", "it")
  • About 150-250 words

B1 (intermediate)

  • Sentences of about 12-20 words. Some can have two clauses
  • Simple passive is fine where science needs it ("water is absorbed")
  • Wider connectives: "so", "when", "after", "however", "this means"
  • Subject vocabulary is used and explained in the text itself
  • About 300-450 words

Both levels cover the same key facts and the same diagram, and share one glossary.

Step-by-step: adapting the text

  1. List the facts that must survive. Before you rewrite anything, write down the 4-6 facts students must leave the lesson knowing. Anything else can be cut.
  2. Choose 10-15 key words. Keep the technical words (chlorophyll, glucose). Students need them, and many have direct equivalents in their home languages. Cut the hard general words instead ("consequently", "utilise", "vital").
  3. Write the B1 version first. Begin with the original text. Split long sentences, replace idioms, and explain each key word the first time it appears.
  4. Write the A2 version from the B1 one. Divide each B1 sentence into one-idea sentences. Change passives to active ("The roots take in water"). Repeat nouns instead of using "it" or "this".
  5. Check that both versions match. Read them side by side. Every fact from step 1 must be in both.
  6. Add support. Use a labelled diagram, a glossary with a home-language column, and questions that move from finding information to explaining it.
  7. Read both versions aloud. If you run out of breath or have to re-read a sentence, shorten it.

Worked example: photosynthesis

Facts that must survive:

  • Plants make their own food (glucose) by photosynthesis
  • This happens mainly in the leaves, in chloroplasts that contain chlorophyll
  • The plant needs light, water (from the roots) and carbon dioxide (from the air)
  • Oxygen is produced and released into the air
  • Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light needed)

Original-style text (typical KS3 textbook level)

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants utilise light energy to synthesise glucose from carbon dioxide and water. It takes place predominantly in the leaves, within organelles called chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll that absorbs light. Water, drawn up from the roots via the xylem, combines with carbon dioxide that diffuses in through the stomata; oxygen is released as a by-product.

B1 version

Plants do not eat food like animals. Instead, they make their own food. This process is called photosynthesis.

Photosynthesis happens mainly in the leaves. Inside the leaf cells there are very small parts called chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain a green substance called chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs (takes in) light energy from the Sun.

The plant needs three things for photosynthesis. First, it needs light. Second, it needs water, which the roots take in from the soil. The water travels up the stem to the leaves. Third, it needs carbon dioxide, a gas from the air. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf through tiny holes called stomata.

In the chloroplasts, the light energy is used to change water and carbon dioxide into glucose. Glucose is a type of sugar. The plant uses glucose for energy and to grow.

Photosynthesis also makes oxygen. The plant does not need all of this oxygen, so it is released into the air through the stomata. This means that animals and people depend on plants for the oxygen they breathe.

We can write photosynthesis as a word equation:

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light is needed)

A2 version

Animals eat food. Plants make their own food. This is called photosynthesis.

Photosynthesis happens in the leaves. Leaves have small parts called chloroplasts. Chloroplasts have a green colour. The green colour is chlorophyll. Chlorophyll takes in light from the Sun.

A plant needs three things:

  • light from the Sun
  • water from the soil
  • carbon dioxide from the air

The roots take in water. The water goes up to the leaves. Carbon dioxide goes into the leaves through very small holes. The small holes are called stomata.

The plant uses light. It changes water and carbon dioxide into glucose. Glucose is a sugar. The plant uses glucose to live and grow.

The plant also makes oxygen. The oxygen goes out into the air. People and animals breathe oxygen.

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

What changed, and why

  • "Utilise light energy to synthesise" became "uses light. It changes..." The A2 version has one verb in each sentence.
  • "Diffuses in through the stomata" became "goes into the leaves through very small holes. The small holes are called stomata." The new word comes after an explanation the student already understands.
  • The A2 version uses a bulleted list for the three things the plant needs. A list is easier to scan than a sentence with three clauses.
  • Xylem was left out of both versions because it was not on the must-survive list. Add it to the B1 version if your scheme of work needs it.
  • The B1 version keeps two passives ("is called", "is released") because students will meet these patterns in every science text.

Comprehension questions at three levels

Use the same questions for both groups, so everyone does the same task and gets the same feedback. A2 students can answer in words or short phrases. B1 students should answer in full sentences.

Find it (literal)

  1. Where does photosynthesis happen?
  2. What three things does a plant need?
  3. What colour is chlorophyll?

Put it together (connecting ideas)

  1. How does water get to the leaves?
  2. What does the plant make? Name two things.

Explain it (reasoning)

  1. Why do people and animals need plants?
  2. A plant is kept in a dark cupboard for two weeks. What do you think happens? Why?

Sentence starters for question 7:

  • A2: "The plant has no ___. It cannot make ___."
  • B1: "I think the plant will ___ because ___. This means ___."

Answers

  1. In the leaves, in the chloroplasts.
  2. Light, water and carbon dioxide.
  3. Green.
  4. The roots take in water from the soil. It goes up the stem to the leaves.
  5. Glucose and oxygen.
  6. Plants make oxygen, and people and animals breathe oxygen.
  7. The plant cannot photosynthesise without light, so it cannot make glucose. It will become weak or die. (Accept any answer that links no light to no food or no glucose.)

Quick checklist before you photocopy

  • [ ] Both versions include every must-survive fact
  • [ ] The A2 version has one idea per sentence and no passives
  • [ ] Every key word is bold and appears in the glossary
  • [ ] Each key word is explained the first time it appears in the text
  • [ ] No idioms or phrasal verbs that students won't know ("work out", "give off"), unless they are taught
  • [ ] The diagram uses the same labels as the glossary
  • [ ] Questions move from finding information, to connecting ideas, to explaining
  • [ ] Sentence starters are ready for the reasoning question
  • [ ] You have read both versions aloud once

Common mistakes

  • Cutting the science, not the language. If the A2 version leaves out oxygen, the student falls behind the class. Simplify the sentences, not the curriculum.
  • Removing every technical word. Students need "chlorophyll" for the test and for the next lesson. Explain the word instead of hiding it.
  • Labelling texts "easy" and "hard" on the page. Use a neutral code, such as a shape or colour in the corner, so students can choose without being singled out.
  • Using the same version all year. Move students to the B1 version, or to the original text, as soon as they can manage it, one topic at a time.

After you have done this twice, one topic takes about an hour to adapt. Keep the must-survive list and the glossary for every topic. They also give you a ready-made vocabulary test and a revision sheet.
