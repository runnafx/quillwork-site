Why Was Christmas Banned? Year 8 Cover Lesson, No Tech + Answers

A complete, print-and-go Year 8 history cover lesson on the 1640s–50s Puritan ban on Christmas. Anyone can run it, and it includes a script for cover staff, tasks with timings and a full answer key.

Every December, someone types "Cromwell banned Christmas" into a search bar. It's a good hook for a lesson, but it isn't quite true, and that's why it works well as a history lesson. Pupils find out what really happened, who decided it, and why a popular story isn't the full picture.

This plan is for a 50–60 minute Year 8 lesson on the English Civil War and Interregnum. It needs no projector and no specialist knowledge. Print one copy of this page for the cover supervisor and one copy of the "Fact Sheet" and "Tasks" sections per pair of pupils.

Cover Supervisor Script

You can read this out word for word.

At the start: "Today's history lesson is about a time in the 1640s and 1650s when Christmas was officially banned in England. By the end, you'll be able to explain who banned it, why, and whether the famous story about Oliver Cromwell is accurate."

Starter: "Write 'True' or 'False' for this statement: *Oliver Cromwell personally banned Christmas.* Don't worry about being right. We'll find out by the end." Then take a quick show of hands and move on. Don't give the answer yet.

For the reading: "We'll read the Fact Sheet together. I'll ask different people to read one paragraph each."

If pupils finish early: they should do the Extension at the bottom of the Tasks.

At the end: collect the Task 3 answers or make sure they're in books, and leave them for the class teacher.

Fact Sheet: The Banning of Christmas

Who were the Puritans? Puritans were Protestants who wanted the Church of England "purified" of anything they saw as unnecessary or Catholic. They believed the Bible didn't give a date for Jesus's birth. They saw the Christmas celebrations of the time (feasting, heavy drinking, games, plays and decorating churches) as wasteful and even sinful.

How was Christmas celebrated before the ban? Christmas could last twelve days. People went to special church services, decorated with holly and ivy, ate large meals, drank, sang and played games. Some places had a "Lord of Misrule" in charge of the fun. For many ordinary people it was one of the few long breaks in the working year.

Why did things change in the 1640s? From 1642 King Charles I fought a civil war against Parliament. Many of Parliament's strongest supporters were Puritans. As Parliament gained power, it began changing religion in England.

The key steps:

  • 1644: Christmas Day fell on a monthly day of fasting that Parliament had ordered. Parliament told people to keep it as a solemn fast, not a feast.
  • 1645: Parliament replaced the Book of Common Prayer with a new "Directory for Public Worship." It recognised no holy days apart from Sunday.
  • June 1647: Parliament passed a law abolishing the feasts of Christmas, Easter and Whitsun. To make up for this, it gave servants, apprentices and students a day off for recreation on the second Tuesday of every month.
  • December 1647: In Canterbury, people angry about the ban rioted on Christmas Day. Disturbances also took place in other towns. Unrest in Kent the following year was part of the Second Civil War.
  • 1650s: Shops and markets were expected to stay open on 25 December. In London, soldiers sometimes broke up illegal Christmas church services. The diarist John Evelyn recorded being held by soldiers after attending a Christmas service in 1657.
  • 1660: The monarchy was restored under Charles II. Laws passed without a king's approval during the Civil War and Interregnum were treated as invalid, so Christmas could be celebrated openly again.

So where does Cromwell come in? Oliver Cromwell was a leading army general and a Puritan. However, the 1647 ban was passed by Parliament, years before Cromwell became Lord Protector in December 1653. The ban carried on while he ruled, and his government enforced it, but he didn't create it on his own.

Did people obey? Many didn't. Lots of families carried on celebrating quietly at home, and some ministers held secret services. Writers published pamphlets mocking the ban. One example is John Taylor's *The Vindication of Christmas* (1652), in which the character of Christmas complains about being driven out.

What about mince pies? You'll often hear that mince pies were specifically made illegal. There was no special law against mince pies. They were caught up in the general ban on celebrating Christmas as a feast.

Tasks

Task 1: Put It in Order (10 min)

Copy these events into the correct order, oldest first, and add the year next to each.

  • A. Christmas riot in Canterbury
  • B. Charles II restored as king and Christmas returns
  • C. Parliament orders Christmas Day to be kept as a fast
  • D. Parliament passes a law abolishing Christmas, Easter and Whitsun
  • E. Cromwell becomes Lord Protector
  • F. The Directory for Public Worship replaces the Prayer Book

Task 2: Myth or Fact? (10 min)

Write each statement and label it Fact, Myth or Partly true. Give a one-line reason for each.

  1. Oliver Cromwell personally banned Christmas.
  2. Puritans thought the Bible did not support celebrating Christmas.
  3. There was a special law banning mince pies.
  4. Some people rioted against the ban.
  5. Everyone stopped celebrating Christmas.
  6. Christmas was legal again after 1660.

Task 3: Extended Answer (15 min)

Answer in a full paragraph (at least 6 sentences):

"Why was Christmas banned in the 1640s, and how successful was the ban?"

Use this writing frame if you need help:

  • Christmas was banned because the Puritans believed...
  • Parliament had the power to do this because...
  • The ban was not completely successful because...
  • One example of resistance was...
  • Overall, I think the ban was (successful / partly successful / unsuccessful) because...

Extension

Imagine you are an apprentice in London in 1648. Write a short diary entry for 25 December. Mention the new monthly day off, and say whether you think it's a fair swap.

Plenary (5 min)

Go back to the starter statement: *"Oliver Cromwell personally banned Christmas."* Each pupil writes one sentence explaining whether that statement is true, false or partly true. Ask two or three pupils to read theirs out.

Answer to share: Partly true at best. Parliament passed the ban in 1647, before Cromwell ruled. Cromwell's government kept and enforced it in the 1650s, so he's linked to it, but he didn't invent it.

Answer Key

Starter

False / partly true. See the plenary answer above.

Task 1

  1. C: Christmas Day kept as a fast (1644)
  2. F: Directory for Public Worship (1645)
  3. D: Law abolishing Christmas, Easter and Whitsun (June 1647)
  4. A: Canterbury riot (December 1647)
  5. E: Cromwell becomes Lord Protector (December 1653)
  6. B: Restoration of Charles II (1660)

Task 2

  1. Partly true / Myth. Parliament passed the ban in 1647. Cromwell's later government enforced it.
  2. Fact. Puritans said the Bible gives no date for Jesus's birth and no command to hold a feast.
  3. Myth. There was no specific mince pie law. They fell under the general ban on Christmas feasting.
  4. Fact. For example, the Canterbury riot of December 1647.
  5. Myth. Many people celebrated secretly at home or went to hidden services.
  6. Fact. After the Restoration, Christmas could be celebrated openly again.

Task 3: What a Good Answer Includes

Give credit for answers that:

  • Explain Puritan beliefs: there was no Bible basis, they disliked drunkenness and excess, and they saw the celebrations as linked to Catholic traditions
  • Explain Parliament's power after the Civil War and name the 1647 law
  • Give evidence of resistance: riots, secret services, pamphlets, people celebrating at home
  • Mention enforcement: shops open, soldiers breaking up services
  • Reach a judgement. "Partly successful" is the strongest answer. The law stayed in place for over a decade, but it never stopped people celebrating and ended in 1660.

Model sentence for a strong conclusion: "The ban was only partly successful: Parliament could close churches on Christmas Day and keep shops open, but it could not change what people did at home, and the tradition returned as soon as the law was lifted in 1660."

Notes for the Class Teacher

This lesson fits the KS3 National Curriculum strand on the development of church, state and society in Britain 1509–1745, and it links directly to a Civil War and Interregnum unit. Task 3 is a good piece to check quickly for causation and judgement skills. The myth-busting angle works well in the last weeks of term because it feels festive but still involves real source-based thinking.
