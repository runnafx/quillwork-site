A Christmas Carol AQA Answer Plan: Worked Example on Scrooge's Greed

A step-by-step way to plan the AQA GCSE English Literature Paper 1 Section B question on A Christmas Carol in about 8 minutes. Includes a full worked plan for a Stave 1 extract and a blank template you can copy.

A Christmas Carol is the 19th-century novel question in Section B of AQA GCSE English Literature Paper 1. Students who know the novella well can still lose marks when they write about the extract and stop there. They also lose marks by retelling the plot of the whole text, or by adding a paragraph of context at the end that has nothing to do with the rest of the answer.

This page sets out a planning method you can teach in one lesson. It's followed by a complete worked plan for a Stave 1 extract and a blank template to copy into exercise books or onto a slide.

What the question looks like

The AQA question gives students a printed extract of roughly a page and asks something in this form:

  • "Starting with this extract, explore how Dickens presents [a character or theme] in A Christmas Carol."
  • Write about how Dickens presents it in this extract.
  • Write about how Dickens presents it in the novel as a whole.

Key facts for students:

  • 30 marks. Section B carries no separate spelling, punctuation and grammar marks. Those are on the Shakespeare question.
  • Closed book. Students must remember quotations from the rest of the novella.
  • Assessment objectives: AO1 (a critical, informed response with references), AO2 (analysis of language, form and structure) and AO3 (context). On this question AO1 and AO2 carry more weight than AO3.
  • Timing: the paper is 1 hour 45 minutes for two sections, so roughly 50 minutes per section is a sensible split. That leaves about 8 minutes to plan and 40 to write.

The 8-minute planning method

  1. Underline the focus word (1 minute). If the question says "greed", the whole plan is about greed. Write the word at the top of the plan and circle it.
  2. Write a one-sentence thesis (1 minute). This is the argument about *why* Dickens presents the focus the way he does. A strong thesis names a purpose, such as "Dickens presents greed as... in order to...".
  3. Annotate the extract for three moments (2 minutes). Find three short quotations of a few words each that you can zoom in on. Look for one method in each: a simile, a list, a verb choice, sentence structure, or a symbol.
  4. Pick two "elsewhere" moments (2 minutes). Choose one from a different stave and one from Stave 5, where Scrooge has changed. Most themes in A Christmas Carol are built around change, so showing the before and after across the whole novel is usually the strongest whole-text material.
  5. Attach context to an idea (1 minute). Link each piece of context to a specific quotation or argument, for example the 1834 Poor Law and workhouses, Malthus and "surplus population", or Dickens writing for a wide middle-class readership. Don't save context for a final paragraph.
  6. Order the paragraphs (1 minute). Put them in sequence: extract first, then whole text, ending on Stave 5 or Dickens's message.

Worked example: greed in Stave 1

Question: Starting with this extract, explore how Dickens presents Scrooge's greed in A Christmas Carol.

Extract (Stave 1, public domain):

"Oh! But he was a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone, Scrooge! a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner! Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster. The cold within him froze his old features, nipped his pointed nose, shrivelled his cheek, stiffened his gait; made his eyes red, his thin lips blue; and spoke out shrewdly in his grating voice... He carried his own low temperature always about with him; he iced his office in the dog-days; and didn't thaw it one degree at Christmas."

Step 1: Focus word

Greed. This means greed as a moral failing, and also what greed does to Scrooge and the people around him.

Step 2: Thesis

"Dickens presents Scrooge's greed as something that has frozen him inside and cut him off from other people. Because Scrooge is later redeemed, Dickens can show his middle-class readers that greed is a choice they can also give up."

Step 3: Three extract moments

  • "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous": a list of present participles (-ing verbs) that are all physical, grabbing actions. The piling up of verbs (asyndetic listing) makes greed feel relentless and ongoing. The final word "sinner" moves from money to morality, so greed is presented as a sin and not only as a habit.
  • "solitary as an oyster": the simile suggests a hard shell that is closed to others. An oyster can also hold a pearl, so a careful reader might argue that Dickens is already hinting there is something worth saving inside Scrooge. This prepares for the redemption arc.
  • "The cold within him... didn't thaw it one degree at Christmas": pathetic fallacy turned inward. Scrooge's coldness comes from inside him, not from the weather. "Thaw" sets up the image of melting that Dickens develops as Scrooge changes.

Step 4: Two elsewhere moments

  • Stave 1, the charity collectors: "If they would rather die, they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population." Scrooge's greed has become a cruel political attitude.
  • Stave 5: Scrooge is "as light as a feather" and "as merry as a schoolboy", and he raises Bob Cratchit's salary. The cold, heavy imagery of Stave 1 has been replaced with lightness, so the change is shown through imagery as well as through events.

Optional third if time allows: Stave 2, Belle, who tells Scrooge that "another idol has displaced me... a golden one". Greed is shown replacing love, which explains how he became the man in Stave 1.

Step 5: Context attached to ideas

  • "Surplus population" links to Thomas Malthus's ideas about population and to the 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act, which made workhouse relief deliberately harsh. Dickens puts these ideas in Scrooge's mouth so that readers who hold similar views hear how cruel they sound.
  • The novella was published in 1843 and aimed at a reading public that could afford to buy it. Scrooge's redemption works as a model for those readers: if Scrooge can change, so can they.

Step 6: Paragraph order

  1. The extract's list of verbs and "sinner": greed as relentless and moral.
  2. The extract's oyster simile and cold imagery: greed isolates Scrooge, with a hint of hidden value.
  3. Stave 1 and Stave 2: greed as cruelty to the poor (Malthus, Poor Law) and as the loss of love (Belle).
  4. Stave 5: lightness replaces cold, and Dickens's message to his readers.

A model opening paragraph

"Dickens opens the novella by presenting Scrooge's greed as relentless and physical, describing him as a 'squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner'. The list of present participles creates a sense of constant, grasping action, as if Scrooge's hands are never still while there is money to be held. However, the list ends with 'sinner', which shifts the description from Scrooge's business habits to his soul. Dickens is signalling to his readers that greed is not simply a personality trait but a moral failing, and this prepares us for a story that will judge it and, eventually, forgive it."

This paragraph quotes precisely, names a method, explains its effect, and links that effect to Dickens's purpose. That covers AO1 and AO2 together.

Quotation bank for common focus themes

Students need memorised quotations for the whole-text half. These short ones fit many questions:

  • Poverty and social responsibility: "Are there no prisons?" and "And the Union workhouses?" (Stave 1)
  • Ignorance and Want: "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both... but most of all beware this boy" (Stave 3)
  • Family and the Cratchits: Tiny Tim's "God bless us, every one!"
  • Redemption: "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year" (Stave 4)
  • Change in Scrooge: "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew" (Stave 5)

Common mistakes to check for

  • Only writing about the extract. The question asks about the novel as a whole as well. Aim for at least one full paragraph beyond the extract.
  • Retelling the plot. Every reference to another stave needs a method and an effect, not just a summary of what happens.
  • Context with no link to an idea. "Dickens lived in Victorian times" earns little. "Scrooge's 'surplus population' echoes Malthus, so Dickens..." earns much more.
  • Long quotations. Three to six words that the student can analyse closely work better than a full sentence they can't unpick.
  • Feature-spotting. Naming "a simile" is the start. The marks come from explaining what it suggests and why Dickens chose it.

Using this in a lesson

Model the six steps live on the board using the worked example above. Then give students a different extract and the blank template, and allow 8 minutes. Before anyone writes a paragraph, have pairs swap plans and check one thing: does the plan include at least two moments from outside the extract? That one check is enough to catch the most common way students lose marks on this question.
