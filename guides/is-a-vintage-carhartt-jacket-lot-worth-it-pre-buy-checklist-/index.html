Is a Vintage Carhartt Jacket Lot Worth It? Pre-Buy Checklist + Math

A step-by-step way to check a lot of used Carhartt duck jackets before you buy it to resell: how to identify each jacket from its photos, how to value it, and how to work out the most you should pay for the lot.

Many resellers buy a "lot of 5 vintage Carhartt jackets" because a single Detroit sells well. A lot price only makes sense when you value each jacket on its own. One lot can mix Detroits, Actives, chore coats and a worn-out coat that is hard to sell at any price.

This page shows how to break a lot down from its photos, value each piece, and get one number: the most you can pay for the whole lot and still make the profit you want. You'll need the listing photos, a calculator and about 20 minutes.

Step 1: Identify every jacket from the photos

Look at each jacket one by one and write down what it is. If you can't tell from the photos, ask the seller for tag photos before you buy.

Find the style number. Look for it on the inside care tag. Common vintage duck styles:

  • J97: Detroit jacket, blanket-lined. This is usually the one resellers want most.
  • J01: also a Detroit jacket. Some guides describe it as the firm-duck version.
  • J130 (also J131, J140): Active jacket, hooded.
  • C-prefix codes (for example C001, C03): coats such as chore coats and Arctic quilt-lined coats. These usually sell for less than Detroits.

Find the date code. Most Carhartt care tags have a four-digit code, often at the top left. The first two digits are the month and the last two are the year. For example, "1102" means November 2002. Some older tags drop the leading zero, so "591" means May 1991.

Note where it was made. Buyers of vintage Carhartt often search for "Made in USA." If the tag says so, write it down and plan to show it in your listing photos.

If a jacket has no visible tag, record it as "unknown." Don't assume it's a Detroit.

Step 2: Grade the condition

Duck canvas is supposed to look worn. Fading, frayed cuffs and a broken-in texture are often part of the appeal, but some damage really does cut the price. Grade each jacket A, B or C:

  • A (sound): worn but whole. Zipper works, lining is intact, no holes bigger than a fingertip, no strong odor.
  • B (honest wear): small holes, stains, paint marks or a torn lining patch, priced as "distressed."
  • C (damaged): big holes or tears, a broken zipper, a missing or shredded lining, or ink names written on the outside.

Automatic red flags. Value these at close to zero unless the price is tiny:

  • Mold or mildew spots, or a seller who mentions "musty" or "basement storage"
  • Any lot listed as "for parts" or "as is, rag grade"
  • Photos that show only a pile with no individual tags or fronts
  • Sizes left out of the listing. Size affects price a lot, so ask.

Step 3: Price each jacket from sold listings, not asking prices

For every jacket in the lot, search eBay for that model, size and condition. Turn on the Sold Items filter and Used condition. Asking prices tell you what sellers hope to get. Sold prices tell you what buyers actually paid.

How to read the results:

  • Ignore the highest sales. Collectors pay extra for rare colors and perfect condition.
  • Use a conservative figure, around the lower-middle of recent sales for that exact model and condition.
  • Price chore coats and Arctic coats from their own sold listings. Don't borrow Detroit numbers for them.
  • If a model shows few recent sales, price it lower and expect it to take longer to sell.

Write one conservative sale price next to each jacket.

Step 4: Fill in the lot worksheet

Copy this and fill it in:

  • Jacket 1: model ___ / size ___ / grade ___ / conservative sale price $___
  • Jacket 2: model ___ / size ___ / grade ___ / conservative sale price $___
  • Jacket 3: model ___ / size ___ / grade ___ / conservative sale price $___
  • Jacket 4: model ___ / size ___ / grade ___ / conservative sale price $___
  • Jacket 5: model ___ / size ___ / grade ___ / conservative sale price $___

Then:

  1. Total sales = add up all the sale prices.
  2. Selling fees = total sales x your eBay fee rate, plus the per-order fee x number of jackets. Check your current rate for the clothing category. Fees change, and some sellers pay different rates.
  3. Outbound shipping you pay = number of jackets x your shipping cost per jacket. Duck jackets are heavy, so weigh one and get a real quote. If buyers pay shipping, put $0 here.
  4. Supplies = mailers, tape and any cleaning, per jacket.
  5. Net after costs = total sales minus lines 2, 3 and 4.
  6. Profit you want = your minimum profit per jacket x number of jackets.
  7. Max lot price = net after costs minus profit you want.

The max lot price is the most you pay, including the lot's shipping and any tax. If the seller is asking more, make an offer at or below that number or walk away.

Worked example (illustrative numbers)

These numbers are made up to show the math. They aren't market data, so use your own sold checks.

A seller lists 5 used Carhartt jackets for $255 shipped. From the photos you find:

  • Jacket 1: J97 Detroit, L, grade A, conservative sale $150
  • Jacket 2: J01 Detroit, XL, grade B, conservative sale $120
  • Jacket 3: J130 Active, L, grade B, conservative sale $100
  • Jacket 4: chore coat, XL, grade B, conservative sale $55
  • Jacket 5: no visible tag, grade C, conservative sale $25

Assume a 13.6% fee rate plus $0.40 per order, $15 shipping per jacket paid by you, $2 supplies per jacket and a target profit of $15 per jacket:

  1. Total sales: 150 + 120 + 100 + 55 + 25 = $450.00
  2. Fees: 450 x 0.136 = 61.20, plus 5 x 0.40 = 2.00, so $63.20
  3. Shipping out: 5 x 15 = $75.00
  4. Supplies: 5 x 2 = $10.00
  5. Net after costs: 450 - 63.20 - 75 - 10 = $301.80
  6. Profit you want: 5 x 15 = $75.00
  7. Max lot price: 301.80 - 75 = $226.80

Result: at $255 this lot falls short of your target. The total profit would be about $46.80, or around $9 per jacket, and that assumes every jacket sells at your conservative price. An offer of around $225 meets your target. Notice that two Detroits carry most of the value. If jacket 2 turned out to be a chore coat, the lot would be a pass.

Quick pre-buy checklist

Tick every box before you pay:

  • [ ] Every jacket shows a readable Carhartt tag, or you've priced untagged ones as "unknown."
  • [ ] You know the style number, or at least the type (Detroit, Active, chore, Arctic), for each jacket.
  • [ ] Sizes are listed or confirmed by the seller.
  • [ ] No mold, mildew or "musty" mentions, and the lot isn't listed for parts.
  • [ ] Each jacket has a condition grade (A, B or C) from the photos.
  • [ ] Each jacket is priced from Sold listings for the same model and condition.
  • [ ] The worksheet uses your real fee rate and a real shipping quote.
  • [ ] The total price (lot + shipping + tax) is at or under your max lot price.
  • [ ] The seller accepts returns, or the price is low enough that you can absorb one bad jacket.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing the whole lot at Detroit prices. Mixed lots often hold only one or two Detroits.
  • Leaving out outbound shipping. A heavy duck jacket costs real money to ship, and you pay that cost on every sale unless the buyer covers it.
  • Trusting the title over the photos. "Vintage Detroit lot" in a title proves nothing, so read the tags.
  • Counting on peak-season prices. Coat demand usually rises in colder months. If you buy late, you may be listing as demand fades.
  • Skipping the photo plan. For every jacket, photograph the style tag, the date code, any "Made in USA" label and every flaw. Clear tag photos help buyers who search by model, and showing flaws upfront reduces "not as described" returns.

Before you list

Once the lot arrives, check each jacket against the photos you were shown, and re-grade it if needed before you set a price. If a jacket is in worse shape than pictured, message the seller right away while you're still inside their return window. Then list your best-graded Detroits first: they're usually the quickest way to get back the money you spent on the lot.
