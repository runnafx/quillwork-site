How to Set GCSE Geography Cover Work a Non-Specialist Can Run

A checklist, a fill-in cover note template and a worked 60-minute example for setting AQA GCSE Geography cover work that keeps an exam class on track with no slides and no tech.

You wake up ill, or a meeting gets moved, and a Year 10 or 11 class needs cover by 8:30. The cover supervisor is probably not a geographer. The projector may not work. You don't want an exam class to lose an hour to a word search.

This page sets out a method you can reuse: what good GCSE cover work includes, a cover note you can copy and fill in, and a fully worked lesson for an AQA Paper 1 topic.

What good GCSE cover work has to do

Cover for an exam class needs to do three jobs at once:

  • Keep students working on the specification. Use content they will be examined on, not filler.
  • Run without you. Someone with no Geography background and no slides should be able to deliver it from one sheet of paper.
  • Produce something you can check quickly. You should get back written answers you can mark or spot-check in a few minutes, ideally in exam format.

If your cover work does all three, the lesson still counts towards the course.

The 10-point cover work checklist

Before you send anything, check it against this list:

  1. One topic, one lesson. Pick a single spec area, such as tectonic hazards, coastal landforms or tropical rainforests. Don't mix topics.
  2. Revision or consolidation, not new content. Non-specialists can't fix misconceptions. Set something students have already been taught, or something a reading sheet can fully explain.
  3. Paper only. Assume there will be no projector, no laptops and no internet. Print everything.
  4. An information sheet students can work from alone. About one side of A4, with key terms in bold and the case-study facts they need.
  5. A clear running order with timings. The supervisor should never have to guess what happens next.
  6. Questions that get harder. Start with recall, then data or figure work, then one extended exam-style question.
  7. At least one exam-style question. Use the command words students meet in the exam: describe, explain, suggest, assess, evaluate, "to what extent".
  8. An extension task. Fast finishers are where behaviour problems start.
  9. Collection instructions. Say exactly what gets handed in and where it goes.
  10. A mark scheme for you, not for the class. Keep it out of the cover pack so answers don't get copied.

Fill-in cover note template

Copy this onto one page and leave it on top of the printed pack.

Class: ______ Room: ______ Period/time: ______ Topic: ______ (AQA Paper 1, Section ___) Seating plan: attached / in top drawer / on the board Students to watch: ______ (names only, no sensitive detail) Resources in this pack: ___ copies of the information sheet, ___ copies of the task sheet, lined paper

Running order:

  • 0–5 min: Register. Students sit in seating-plan places and write the title and date.
  • 5–10 min: Students read the information sheet in silence. Say: "Read the whole sheet before you answer anything."
  • 10–25 min: Section A (knowledge questions). Full sentences.
  • 25–40 min: Section B (figure/data task).
  • 40–55 min: Section C (exam question). Say: "Treat this as exam practice: 12 minutes, in silence, no talking."
  • 55–60 min: Collect task sheets in register order and put them in ______.

If they finish early: extension task at the bottom of the task sheet. If there's a problem: contact ______ on extension ______.

What NOT to do: The supervisor doesn't need to teach or explain the content. If students ask a content question, the answer is "Use the information sheet, then write your best attempt."

Matching cover to AQA Paper 1

AQA GCSE Geography (8035) Paper 1 is "Living with the physical environment". It has three sections:

  • Section A: The challenge of natural hazards. Tectonic hazards, weather hazards and climate change.
  • Section B: The living world. Ecosystems, tropical rainforests, and either hot deserts or cold environments.
  • Section C: Physical landscapes in the UK. Students answer on two of coastal, river and glacial landscapes.

Make sure you set the options your class actually studies. A cover lesson on glacial landscapes is no use to a class that took rivers and coasts. Check the current specification and your own scheme of work, too. This page summarises the structure and doesn't replace AQA's documents.

Worked example: a 60-minute cover lesson on waterfall formation

This is a complete example you can adapt. Topic: River landscapes, waterfall formation (Section C).

Information sheet (summary of what to include)

  • Key terms in bold: hydraulic action, abrasion, erosion, resistant rock, less resistant rock, overhang, plunge pool, gorge, undercutting.
  • The process, in order: A river flows over a band of resistant rock lying on top of less resistant rock. The softer rock erodes faster, through hydraulic action and abrasion, which undercuts the hard rock and leaves an overhang. The overhang becomes unsupported and collapses. The fallen rock is swirled around in the plunge pool and deepens it by abrasion. As this repeats, the waterfall retreats upstream and leaves a steep-sided gorge behind it.
  • A labelled diagram description, so students can draw it themselves: hard rock layer on top, soft rock below, overhang, plunge pool, direction of retreat arrow, gorge downstream.
  • A named UK example your class has studied, with two or three facts from your own notes.

Section A: Knowledge check (15 minutes)

  1. Define "hydraulic action".
  2. Define "abrasion".
  3. Why does the less resistant rock erode faster than the resistant rock?
  4. What is an overhang, and why does it collapse?
  5. What is a plunge pool?
  6. Name a UK waterfall and its river.

Section B: Figure task (15 minutes)

Students draw a labelled cross-section of a waterfall from the description on the information sheet. Give them a checklist of labels: resistant rock, less resistant rock, overhang, plunge pool, direction of retreat, gorge. Award one tick per correct label in the right place.

Paper-only cover suits diagram tasks well. They take time, they're calm, and they're easy to check at a glance.

Section C: Exam-style question (15 minutes)

"Explain how a waterfall changes over time to form a gorge. [6 marks]"

Tell students to write about 12 minutes' worth and to use at least four key terms.

Mark scheme (keep this for yourself)

This is a levels-style scheme written for this example, in the format teachers commonly use for 6-mark explain questions. It isn't an official AQA mark scheme.

  • Level 3 (5–6 marks): A clear, sequenced explanation that links the processes to the outcomes: differential erosion, then undercutting, then overhang collapse, then a deeper plunge pool, then retreat upstream, then a gorge. Accurate use of key terms throughout.
  • Level 2 (3–4 marks): Several correct stages and some explanation, but the links are partial or the order is unclear. Some key terms used.
  • Level 1 (1–2 marks): Isolated points, such as "the rock falls down" or "water erodes the rock", with little explanation or sequence.
  • 0 marks: Nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content: hard rock on top of soft rock; soft rock eroded faster by hydraulic action and abrasion; undercutting creates an overhang; the overhang is unsupported and collapses; the debris deepens the plunge pool by abrasion; repeated collapse makes the waterfall retreat upstream; the retreat leaves a steep-sided gorge.

Extension task

"Suggest why some waterfalls retreat faster than others." (Possible ideas: rock type, river discharge, the size of the plunge pool, freeze-thaw weathering on the face.)

Five mistakes that waste a cover lesson

  • Setting a "research task". Without devices or textbooks, students have nothing to research from.
  • Leaving instructions in your planner or on your laptop. The supervisor can't use what they can't see. Put everything in the printed pack.
  • Setting too little. A 20-minute task in a 60-minute lesson invites trouble. Always include an extension.
  • Giving the answers away. A mark scheme left in the pack ends up on every desk.
  • Not collecting the work. If nothing comes back, students learn that cover doesn't count. Say where the sheets go, and follow up on the work in your next lesson.

Reusing this for any Paper 1 topic

You can use the same frame for every topic:

  • Tectonic hazards: information sheet on plate margins plus one named earthquake. Data task: a table comparing the effects of two earthquakes. Exam question: "Explain why the effects of earthquakes vary."
  • Climate change: information sheet on natural and human causes. Data task: describe a trend from a table of temperature values. Exam question: "Assess the evidence that climate is changing."
  • Tropical rainforests: information sheet on adaptations and deforestation. Data task: a table of deforestation causes. Exam question: "Evaluate strategies to manage rainforests sustainably."
  • Coastal landforms: information sheet on headlands, caves, arches and stacks. Figure task: a sequence diagram. Exam question: "Explain the formation of a stack."

If you build one pack per topic and keep them in a labelled folder in your department office, a short-notice absence takes a two-minute phone call: "Folder three, thirty copies, Room 12."
