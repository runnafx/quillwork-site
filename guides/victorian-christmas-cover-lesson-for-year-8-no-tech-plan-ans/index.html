Victorian Christmas Cover Lesson for Year 8: No-Tech Plan + Answers

A free, print-and-go KS3 history cover lesson asking "Did the Victorians invent Christmas?" It includes a script for non-specialist cover staff, five source cards, three tasks and a full answer key.

You need a history lesson for the last weeks of the autumn term. It should be real learning rather than a word search, and someone who isn't a historian should be able to run it. This lesson asks one question: "Did the Victorians invent Christmas?" It fits a Year 8 Industrial Revolution or Victorian Britain unit. You can also use it with Year 9 as a review.

Everything below is on this page. Print the source cards and the task sheet, then hand the answer key to whoever is covering.

What You Need

  • One copy of the five source cards per pair (or project them, if the room has a working screen)
  • One task sheet per student (the three tasks below)
  • Lined paper or exercise books
  • No laptops, video or specialist knowledge needed

Lesson Timing (55 minutes)

  1. Starter, true or false: 5 minutes
  2. Read the source cards in pairs: 15 minutes
  3. Task 1, sorting grid: 10 minutes
  4. Task 2, Dickens and the factory child: 10 minutes
  5. Task 3, judgement paragraph: 12 minutes
  6. Plenary, hands-up vote: 3 minutes

Script for the Cover Teacher

Read this aloud at the start:

"Today's question is: did the Victorians invent Christmas? Queen Victoria reigned from 1837 to 1901. Many things we link with Christmas became popular during her reign. Your job is to work out which things were truly new, which were older traditions that became more popular, and which only happened because of new technology. At the end you will give your own judgement and back it up with evidence."

Starter: True or False?

Students write T or F for each statement. Don't give the answers until the plenary.

  1. Prince Albert was the first person to bring a Christmas tree to Britain.
  2. The first Christmas card sold to the public was printed in 1843.
  3. A Christmas Carol was written by Charles Dickens.
  4. Boxing Day has been an official bank holiday since medieval times.
  5. People in Britain celebrated Christmas long before Queen Victoria.

The Source Cards

Card A: The Christmas Tree Decorating a tree at Christmas was a German custom. Queen Charlotte, the German-born wife of King George III, set up a Christmas tree for a royal party at Windsor around 1800. Queen Victoria married the German Prince Albert in 1840, and decorated trees became part of royal Christmases. In 1848 the Illustrated London News printed a picture of the royal family standing around a decorated tree. Pictures like this spread the idea to middle-class families, who wanted to copy the royals.

Card B: The Christmas Card In 1843 Henry Cole, a civil servant, paid the artist John Callcott Horsley to design a printed Christmas card. About 1,000 copies were printed for sale. A few years earlier, in 1840, the Penny Post had been introduced, so a letter could be sent anywhere in Britain for one penny. Cheap post and cheaper colour printing later in the century meant that sending cards became a normal Christmas habit.

Card C: Crackers, Food and Shops In the 1840s Tom Smith, a London sweet-maker, sold sweets wrapped in twists of paper. He later added a small "bang" and little gifts, and the Christmas cracker was born. Feasting at Christmas was not new: rich households had eaten Christmas feasts for centuries. In A Christmas Carol, the poor Cratchit family eat a goose. Turkey became more common in the Victorian period, but for many years it was a luxury for most families.

Card D: Dickens and A Christmas Carol Charles Dickens published A Christmas Carol on 19 December 1843. Earlier that year he had read a government report on the terrible conditions of children working in mines and factories, and it made him angry. In the story, the miser Scrooge learns to be generous. Tiny Tim, a poor disabled boy, says "God bless us, every one!" A ghost shows Scrooge two starving children called Ignorance and Want, and warns him to beware them both. The book sold very well and helped turn Christmas into a time for family, kindness and charity.

Card E: Old Traditions and New Holidays Christmas had been celebrated in Britain for over a thousand years. Medieval people had feasts, carols, greenery such as holly and ivy, and twelve days of celebration. In the 1640s and 1650s Puritan governments tried to stop Christmas celebrations, but they returned after 1660. In early Victorian factories many workers got very little time off, often only Christmas Day. The Bank Holidays Act of 1871 made Boxing Day an official bank holiday in England, Wales and Ireland. Railways also made it easier for families to travel home for Christmas.

Task 1: Sorting Grid

Draw three columns headed "Truly new", "Old tradition made more popular" and "Made possible by new technology or laws". Put each item below in the column you think fits best. Some items could go in more than one column, so pick the best fit and add a reason.

  • Christmas trees in British homes
  • Christmas cards
  • Christmas crackers
  • Christmas feasts
  • Carols and holly decorations
  • Families travelling home by train
  • Boxing Day as a bank holiday
  • Christmas as a time for charity to the poor

Task 2: Dickens and the Factory Child

Use Card D to answer in full sentences.

  1. When was A Christmas Carol published? (1 mark)
  2. What had Dickens read earlier in 1843 that made him angry? (1 mark)
  3. Why might Dickens have put two starving children, Ignorance and Want, into a Christmas story? (2 marks)
  4. Using Cards B and D, explain one way that new technology helped Victorian Christmas ideas spread. (2 marks)

Task 3: Judgement Paragraph

Answer the big question: "How far did the Victorians invent Christmas?"

Use these sentence starters:

  • "On one hand, the Victorians did invent parts of Christmas, such as..."
  • "This is shown by Card..., which says..."
  • "On the other hand, many traditions were much older, for example..."
  • "Overall, I think the Victorians [invented / reinvented / popularised] Christmas because..."

Challenge: explain the difference between *inventing* something and *popularising* it.

Plenary: Hands-Up Vote

Ask: "Hands up if you think the Victorians invented Christmas. Hands up if you think they reshaped an older festival." Ask two students to give one piece of evidence each. Then read out the starter answers.

Answer Key (For Cover Staff)

Starter Answers

  1. False. Queen Charlotte had a royal Christmas tree around 1800. Albert made trees fashionable but did not bring the first one.
  2. True. Henry Cole's card was printed in 1843.
  3. True.
  4. False. Boxing Day became a bank holiday in 1871.
  5. True. Christmas had been celebrated for over a thousand years.

Task 1: Suggested Sorting

Accept other placements if the student gives a sensible reason.

  • Truly new: Christmas cards (1843) and Christmas crackers (1840s onwards)
  • Old tradition made more popular: Christmas trees (an older German custom that the royal family made fashionable), feasts, carols and holly, and charity at Christmas (old, but strengthened by Dickens)
  • Made possible by new technology or laws: train travel home (railways), Boxing Day bank holiday (1871 Act), and cards becoming common (Penny Post and cheaper printing). Cards can also go here.

Task 2 Answers

  1. 19 December 1843.
  2. A government report on the conditions of children working in mines and factories.
  3. Any two of these, 1 mark each: to make readers feel sorry for poor children; to warn rich readers that ignoring poverty is dangerous; to use a popular, festive story to spread a serious message about child poverty to more people than a report would reach.
  4. One clear explanation, up to 2 marks. For example, the Penny Post (1840) made posting cheap, so printed cards could be sent widely. Or cheaper printing meant books like A Christmas Carol and pictures like the 1848 royal tree could reach many readers.

Task 3: What a Good Answer Looks Like

A strong answer:

  • gives at least one new thing with a date (cards in 1843, crackers in the 1840s, the bank holiday in 1871)
  • gives at least one older tradition (feasts, carols, holly, the twelve days, or the 1800 royal tree)
  • names a card as evidence
  • reaches a clear judgement, usually that the Victorians reinvented or popularised Christmas rather than inventing it from nothing

Model conclusion: "Overall, I think the Victorians reshaped Christmas rather than invented it. Feasts and carols were centuries old, but new things like cards and crackers, plus the Penny Post, railways and Dickens's book, turned it into the family-centred festival we know today."

Tips for the Cover Teacher

  • If students finish early: ask them to design a Victorian-style Christmas card for 1843. The back should explain which new invention made sending it possible.
  • If reading is a struggle: pair weaker readers with a confident reader, and only ask them to do Cards A, B and D.
  • For marking: collect Task 3 paragraphs so the class teacher can check the judgements when they return.
