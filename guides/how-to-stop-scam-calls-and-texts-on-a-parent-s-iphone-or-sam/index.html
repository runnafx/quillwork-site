How to Stop Scam Calls and Texts on a Parent's iPhone or Samsung

A checklist for cutting down scam calls and texts on an older parent's iPhone or Samsung Galaxy using settings already on the phone. It also includes a short family plan and a fill-in "Is this a scam?" card to leave by the phone.

If your parent's phone rings several times a day with "your car warranty," "your bank account is locked" or "Grandma, it's me, I'm in trouble," you can cut that down a lot in about 20 minutes. Use the settings the phone already has. You don't need a paid app to start.

No setting stops every scam. The goal is fewer scam calls getting through, plus a simple habit for the ones that still do. This page covers both.

This guide uses recent iPhone (iOS 18 and iOS 26) and Samsung Galaxy (One UI 7 and 8) menus. Menu names can change between updates. If a step doesn't match what you see, open Settings, tap the search bar at the top and type the setting's name, such as "unknown callers" or "spam."

Before you start:

  • Sit with your parent and make the changes together. Explain what each one does. It's their phone, so they decide.
  • Ask first: "Are you waiting on calls from numbers you don't know?" A new doctor, a hospital, a contractor or a delivery driver might call from an unknown number. The answer tells you how strict to be in Step 2.
  • Make sure the numbers they care about are saved in Contacts first: family, doctors, pharmacy, neighbors, the pharmacy's refill line. Most filters let saved contacts through and treat everyone else as a stranger.

Step 1: Update the phone

Updates fix security holes and often improve spam filtering.

  • iPhone: Settings → General → Software Update. Install any update shown, then turn on Automatic Updates.
  • Samsung: Settings → Software update → Download and install. Turn on Auto download over Wi-Fi. Then open Play Store → profile picture → Manage apps & device → Update all.

Step 2: Filter calls from unknown numbers

iPhone (iOS 26): Settings → Apps → Phone → Screen Unknown Callers. You'll see two useful choices:

  • Ask Reason for Calling: the iPhone answers the call, asks the caller who they are and why they're calling, and shows the reply on screen before the phone rings. This is a good middle ground for parents who get legitimate calls from new numbers.
  • Silence: calls from numbers not in Contacts go straight to voicemail without ringing. This is the strictest option.

iPhone (iOS 18): Settings → Apps → Phone → Silence Unknown Callers → On.

Samsung: open the Phone app → ⋮ (three dots, top right) → Settings → Caller ID and spam protection → On. If you see options to block spam or scam calls, turn them on. Samsung doesn't have one switch that silences every number outside Contacts. Block numbers → Block unknown/private numbers only blocks callers who hide their number.

Tell your parent the trade-off: silenced or screened calls still show up in Recents and can still leave voicemail. Show them where both are, and ask them to check once a day.

Step 3: Filter scam texts

  • iPhone: Settings → Apps → Messages → find the Unknown Senders section and turn on filtering. (On iOS 18 it's called Filter Unknown Senders.) Texts from strangers go into a separate list instead of the main inbox.
  • Samsung with Google Messages: tap the profile picture → Messages settings → Spam protection → On.
  • Samsung with Samsung Messages: ⋮ → Settings → Block numbers and spam → turn on spam protection if offered.

Not sure which messages app the Samsung uses? Open it and look at the top. Google Messages shows a round profile picture. Samsung Messages shows three dots.

Step 4: Teach them to block a number

They'll get scam calls you didn't catch, so practice blocking one together using a real number from their recent calls.

  • iPhone: Phone → Recents → tap the blue "i" next to the number → scroll down → Block this Caller. For a text, open it and tap Report Junk if it appears.
  • Samsung: Phone → Recents → tap the number → "i" (Details) → Block. For a text, press and hold the conversation → Block or Report spam.

Blocking one number won't stop a scammer who calls from a different one. It still helps with repeat callers, and it gives your parent a way to act on their own.

Step 5: Check the free tools from their phone company

The major US carriers offer call-filtering apps or settings, and most have a free tier (AT&T ActiveArmor, Verizon Call Filter and T-Mobile Scam Shield, for example). Paid upgrades are optional. Before you install anything:

  • Check what the free version does. Don't sign up for a monthly charge by accident.
  • Install the carrier's app only from the App Store or Play Store, not from a link in a text.
  • Don't stack several call-blocking apps. They can conflict, and they make the phone confusing to use.

Step 6: Register on the Do Not Call list (and know its limits)

In the US, you can add their number to the National Do Not Call Registry run by the FTC. Search for it by name and use the official government site. Registration is free. Any site that charges for it is not the real registry.

Be honest about what it does: it reduces calls from legitimate telemarketers. Scammers ignore it. It's worth doing, but it isn't protection.

Step 7: Make a family plan for the calls that get through

Settings catch a lot, but the strongest protection is a simple routine your parent can follow when they're unsure. Agree on these together:

  1. Pick a family code word. If someone calls saying they're a grandchild in trouble, your parent asks for the code word. No code word, no money.
  2. Hang up, then call back yourself. If "the bank" or "Medicare" calls, hang up and call the number on the card, statement or official letter. Never use a number the caller gives.
  3. Never read out a code that arrives by text. Real companies don't call and ask for it.
  4. Gift cards, wire transfers, crypto or cash couriers mean it's a scam. Real agencies and companies don't ask for payment this way.
  5. "Don't tell your family" or "You must act now" means hang up. Real problems can wait 10 minutes while they call you.
  6. Name one person to call when they're unsure, and save that person as a Favorite so they're one tap away.

Tell them clearly that hanging up on someone who turns out to be real is fine. The bank will call back. Nobody will be upset with them.

Fill-In Card: "Is This a Scam?" (Print and Keep by the Phone)

Copy this onto a card in large letters, or print it at 18pt or bigger.

  • Before I pay anyone or share any code, I call: ______________________ at ______________________
  • Our family code word is: ______________________ (keep this card somewhere private, not in a wallet you might lose)
  • My bank's real number (from my card): ______________________
  • My phone company's help number: ______________________
  • My doctor's office: ______________________
  • My pharmacy: ______________________

I hang up if the caller:

  • ☐ says I must act right now
  • ☐ asks for gift cards, a wire transfer, crypto or cash pickup
  • ☐ asks me to read out a code from a text
  • ☐ says not to tell my family
  • ☐ says a grandchild is in jail or in the hospital and needs money

Then I call the person at the top of this card.

Quick Checklist

  • ☐ Phone updated, automatic updates on
  • ☐ Important numbers saved in Contacts
  • ☐ Unknown-caller filtering set (Ask Reason, Silence, or Samsung spam protection)
  • ☐ Scam-text filtering on
  • ☐ Parent has practiced blocking a number once
  • ☐ Parent knows where Recents and voicemail are
  • ☐ Carrier's free tool checked (no paid sign-up by mistake)
  • ☐ Number added to the Do Not Call Registry
  • ☐ Family code word chosen
  • ☐ "Is This a Scam?" card filled in and left by the phone

If something has already happened

If your parent has already paid a scammer or shared a code or password, call their bank or card company right away using the number on the card. Then change the password for the affected account. In the US, you can also report it to the FTC, and to local police if money was lost. Acting fast can sometimes limit the damage, but there's no guarantee money can be recovered.
