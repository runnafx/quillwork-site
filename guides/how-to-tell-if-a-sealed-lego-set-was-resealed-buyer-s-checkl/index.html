How to Tell if a Sealed LEGO Set Was Resealed: Buyer's Checklist

A checklist for checking a "factory sealed" retired LEGO set before you bid and again once it's in your hands. It also covers what to do, and how to list it honestly, if something looks wrong.

Retired sealed LEGO sets sell for more than opened ones, so some people open a box, swap or take out parts, and close it up again. If you buy sets at auctions, estate sales or local marketplaces to resell on eBay, a resealed box is a real risk. Your buyer may open it, find a problem and send it back as "not as described."

No visual check is perfect, and this page can't promise that a box is untouched. What it can do is help you catch the common signs before you pay, and give you a fair way to handle a doubtful box afterwards. These tips come from what experienced LEGO collectors and resellers commonly report. They are not official guidance from LEGO.

Part 1: Before you bid (photos only)

Auction and marketplace photos are often taken from the front at an angle. Those are the photos least likely to show a problem. Ask for these, or look for them in the gallery:

  1. Both ends of the box, straight on. Most resealing happens at the ends, where the flaps or seal stickers are.
  2. Close-ups of every seal. You want to see the edges of each round or strip seal clearly.
  3. The bottom of the box. Look for bulges, crushed corners or flaps that don't sit flat.
  4. The top edge and back. Look for cut lines or tears from a knife.
  5. Any price or clearance stickers. Some sellers put stickers over damage or over a cut seal.

If the seller won't or can't show the ends and seals, treat the set as "possibly opened" when you work out your max bid. Small regional auction houses often say they can't take extra photos. That's normal, but it is still a risk, and the price you're willing to pay should reflect it.

Red flags in photos:

  • A seal that looks cloudy, wrinkled or has bubbles under it, while the other seals look clear
  • A seal that sits off-center or crooked compared with the matching seal at the other end
  • Two seals stacked on one spot, or the edge of an older cut seal showing from under a newer one
  • Flaps that are bowed, lifted or sitting at different heights
  • Clear packing tape over the end flaps. A few sellers tape a genuine box shut for shipping, so this isn't proof, but it hides what you need to see

Part 2: When the set arrives (hands-on checklist)

Check the box before you take any listing photos and before you do anything else to it. Keep the outer shipping packaging until you're done, in case you need to file a claim with the auction house or marketplace.

Seals and flaps

  • [ ] Clarity: collectors report that factory seals are usually very clear, and that seals that have been peeled and stuck back down often look hazy. Compare each seal with the others on the same box.
  • [ ] What's under the seal: in good light, look for uneven glue, dust or fibers, or little scraps of torn cardboard printing caught under the seal.
  • [ ] Alignment: factory seals sit in matching spots on both ends. One that's shifted or angled on only one end is worth a closer look.
  • [ ] Non-matching seals: collectors say resealers often use generic clear stickers or tape. If a seal's size, shape or shine is different from the others, write it down.
  • [ ] Cut marks: run a fingernail gently along the edge of each seal. A clean slit through the seal, or along the flap line, means the box was cut open.
  • [ ] Glued flaps, if the box has them: some older boxes close with glued flaps. Look for extra glue squeezed out at the edges, cardboard that's lifted or uneven where the flap meets the box, or torn fibers along the glue line.

Box shape and contents

  • [ ] Bulge test: set the box on a flat table. A sealed box with its original bags usually sits flat. A bulge or rounded panel can mean bags were put back loosely, or that something else is inside.
  • [ ] Shake test: shake it gently. You should hear bagged parts moving as groups. Lots of single pieces rattling loose, or almost no sound in a large box, is a warning sign. This test is not conclusive by itself, because bags can tear in transit.
  • [ ] Weight check: if a trusted LEGO catalog lists a box weight for the set, weigh yours on a kitchen or postal scale. Treat the listed weight as approximate. Only a big gap (a box that feels clearly light) should worry you.
  • [ ] Window or cutout, if the box has one: look through it for parts that obviously don't belong to the set.

What NOT to count as proof

  • Small creases or crushed corners on the bottom. These often come from shipping, shelving or warehouse handling and don't mean the box was opened.
  • Sun fading or shelf wear. That's a condition issue, not a sign of resealing. Describe it in your listing all the same.
  • A seal that has lifted slightly at one edge while the rest is clear and smooth. Old adhesive can loosen with age. Look at the whole box before you decide.

Part 3: Scoring the box

Count the checks that raised a concern, then sort the box:

  • 0 concerns: List as new and sealed. Photograph every seal anyway (see Part 4).
  • 1 concern that has a harmless explanation (for example, a lifted seal edge on an old box): List as sealed, but name the issue in the description and show it in a photo.
  • 2 or more concerns, or any clear cut, stacked seal or generic tape: don't list it as factory sealed. Use one of the options in Part 5.

Part 4: Photos that protect you as the seller

Even a genuine sealed box can bring a return claim. Good photos taken before you ship are your best record of what you sent:

  1. Front and back, flat and straight on
  2. Every end panel, showing each seal up close
  3. The bottom panel
  4. Close-ups of any wear, dents or fading you mention in the description
  5. The box on your scale, if you weighed it
  6. The packed box inside its shipping carton, just before you tape the carton shut

Keep these photos until the return window for that sale has closed.

Part 5: What to do with a box that fails

You still have honest choices:

  • Ask for a remedy. Some auction houses accept claims for items that don't match the lot description, but many sell everything "as-is, where-is." Read the auction terms before you bid, so you know which kind you're dealing with.
  • Open it and check the contents. If you have the time, open the box, check every bag and part against the instructions or a parts inventory, and sell it as "new, open box, contents verified." It sells for less than a sealed copy, but you won't get a not-as-described return.
  • List it as "box opened, contents not verified." This brings the lowest price, but it's accurate, and buyers who want parts or a cheap copy will still bid.
  • Never list a doubtful box as factory sealed. If the buyer opens it and finds parts missing, you'll probably lose the sale, the shipping both ways and a feedback point.

Fill-in note for your inventory sheet

Copy this into your tracking spreadsheet for each sealed set you buy:

  • Set number and name: ______
  • Where bought / lot number: ______
  • Seals: clear / hazy / cut / generic (circle one per end)
  • Flaps: flat / lifted / extra glue
  • Bulge: yes / no
  • Shake: bagged / loose / quiet
  • Weight vs catalog: ______ g vs ______ g
  • Decision: sealed / sealed with note / open and verify / sell as opened
  • Photos taken (date): ______

Factor this into your max bid

Unless the seller shows clear end and seal photos, build a reseal risk into your numbers. Work out your max bid twice: once at the sealed resale value, and once at the price an opened copy of the same set sells for. If the deal still breaks even at the opened price, a surprise reseal costs you time but not money. If it only works at the sealed price, you're betting on seals you haven't seen yet. Bid lower, or skip the lot.
