How to Write a Letter From Santa: Fill-In Template + Example

A step-by-step guide to writing a personal letter from Santa for a child aged 3 to 8. It includes a fill-in template, a full worked example, and wording for tricky situations.

Most kids get a Santa letter that could be for anyone. A letter that names their dog, their street and the day they finally rode a bike without help can feel real to them. You don't need to be a good writer to make one. You need about 15 minutes of notes and a simple structure.

This page walks you through it: what to collect, the order to write it in, a template you can fill in, a finished example, and what to say when the year has been hard.

Step 1: Collect the details (10 minutes)

Write these down before you start. Short notes are fine.

  • The child's first name and age. Use the name family actually calls them. If everyone says "Bea", don't write "Beatrice".
  • What they call Santa. Santa, Santa Claus, Father Christmas or Saint Nick.
  • Pets, siblings and grown-ups at home, first names only.
  • One place detail. Your town, your street, a nearby park, or "the house with the blue door".
  • Two or three real wins from this year. Pick small, specific ones a child would remember. "Learned to swim across the pool" works better than "was good".
  • One gentle goal for next year. Something they already care about, like reading a chapter book or being patient with a little brother.
  • One Christmas detail. The cookie they leave out, a family tradition, or the gift they asked for. You can mention the gift without promising it.
  • Anything to avoid. A recent move, a pet that died, divorce, illness, or religion if you want the letter to stay secular.

Step 2: Use this five-part structure

A good Santa letter for a young child is short. Aim for 150 to 250 words. That fits on one page in a large, readable font and holds a 5-year-old's attention when it's read aloud.

  1. Greeting and proof Santa is watching. Use one detail only your family would know.
  2. Two specific wins. Say what they did and why Santa noticed.
  3. A North Pole moment. An elf, a reindeer or Mrs. Claus reacting to the child. This is where the magic comes in.
  4. A gentle goal. Frame it as Santa believing in them, not as a warning.
  5. Christmas Eve sign-off. Mention the cookies, the chimney or the tradition, then a warm closing and signature.

Step 3: Fill in the template

Copy this and replace each bracket. Delete any line that doesn't fit your child.

---

Dear [NAME],

Greetings from the North Pole! The snow here is [DEEP / SPARKLY / HIGHER THAN THE ELVES' HATS], and the workshop has been busy since [MONTH]. I wanted to write to you, because I have been hearing wonderful things about a [AGE]-year-old in [TOWN / STREET / "the house with the blue door"].

First, I have to tell you how proud I am that you [WIN #1, specific]. That takes [COURAGE / PATIENCE / PRACTICE], and I saw how hard you tried. I also heard that you [WIN #2]. [PET'S NAME] must have been very impressed too.

Here at the workshop, [ELF NAME] [REACTION, e.g. "did a cartwheel" / "wrote it in the Big Book in gold ink"]. Even [REINDEER NAME] [SMALL FUNNY DETAIL].

Next year, I think you are ready to [GENTLE GOAL]. I know you can do it, because you already [EVIDENCE FROM THIS YEAR].

I know you'd love [GIFT THEY ASKED FOR], and the elves have your letter. On Christmas Eve I'll be watching for your [COOKIES / CARROTS / TRADITION]. Please give [SIBLING / PET] a hug from me.

Your friend at the North Pole, [SANTA / FATHER CHRISTMAS]

P.S. [ONE TINY SECRET, e.g. "Rudolph says your drawing of him is on the stable wall."]

---

Step 4: A worked example

Here is the template filled in for a made-up child. Maya is 6, has a dog named Pickles and lives on Maple Street. This year she learned to ride a bike and helped her little brother Theo with his shoes.

---

Dear Maya,

Greetings from the North Pole! The snow here is higher than the elves' hats, and the workshop has been busy since July. I wanted to write to you, because I have been hearing wonderful things about a 6-year-old on Maple Street.

First, I have to tell you how proud I am that you learned to ride your bike without training wheels. That takes courage, and I saw how many times you got back on. I also heard that you've been helping Theo put on his shoes in the mornings. Pickles must have been very impressed too.

Here at the workshop, an elf named Juniper wrote your name in the Big Book in gold ink. Even Dasher wanted to know if you'd teach him to ride a bike.

Next year, I think you're ready to read a whole chapter book by yourself. I know you can do it, because you already read Theo his bedtime story some nights.

I know you'd love a new art set, and the elves have your letter. On Christmas Eve I'll be watching for your chocolate chip cookies. Please give Pickles a scratch behind the ears from me.

Your friend at the North Pole, Santa

P.S. Rudolph says to leave the porch light on. His nose works best with a little help.

---

Wording for tricky situations

When the child asked for something they won't get. Don't promise the gift. Write "the elves have your letter" or "I'm keeping your list right by my desk." That shows you heard them without setting up a letdown on Christmas morning.

After a hard year (a move, illness, a new baby, a divorce). Praise what they managed, not the event. For example: "You've had a lot of big changes this year, and you've been so brave." Don't mention details the child may not fully know.

Two homes. Santa can say he knows "both of your homes" or "wherever you are sleeping on Christmas Eve." Don't name one house as the place Santa visits unless you've agreed that with the other parent.

A pet that died. Leave it out unless you're sure the child would find it comforting. If you include it, keep it to one gentle line, such as "I know you miss Biscuit, and I know Biscuit loved you very much."

Behavior struggles. Skip the naughty list. Threats in a Santa letter can make an anxious child more anxious and rarely change behavior. Turn the problem into the gentle goal: "I think next year you're ready to use your calm words when you're upset."

Faith or no faith. For a secular letter, stick to snow, elves and reindeer. For a faith-based letter, add one line in your family's own language, such as a blessing at the close.

Siblings. Give each child their own letter if you can. If they share one, give each child the same number of lines and their own specific win. Children compare.

Step 5: Make it look like it came from the North Pole

  • Paper. Thicker paper or cream cardstock feels special. Parchment-style paper is sold at most office supply stores.
  • Font. Use a clear script or a large, readable serif. Skip anything a child can't sound out or you can't read aloud easily.
  • Signature. Sign in ink with your non-writing hand, or have another adult sign so it doesn't look like your handwriting.
  • Small touches. A sprinkle of glitter in the envelope, a "North Pole" return address written on the front, or a sticker seal.
  • Delivery. Leave it by the fireplace, tuck it in with the advent calendar, or have it "arrive" in the mailbox. Some families save it for Christmas morning next to the stocking.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Too long. Past about 250 words, young children stop listening. Cut the middle first.
  • Generic praise. "You've been very good" is forgettable. "You were brave at the dentist" is not.
  • Wrong names. Check the spelling of every name and nickname, including the pet's.
  • Adult jokes. Keep humor at a child's level: reindeer snoring, elves eating too many cookies.
  • Too much about the gift. The letter should be about the child, not the delivery.

Quick checklist before you print

  • Child's name spelled the way they know it
  • At least one detail only your family would know
  • Two specific wins from this year
  • One gentle goal, framed as belief, not a warning
  • No promises about specific gifts
  • No sensitive topics you meant to avoid
  • Under one page when printed
  • Signed and ready to deliver

Keep a copy for yourself. Many families say a Santa letter becomes a keepsake, and reading it years later shows what mattered to your child that year.
