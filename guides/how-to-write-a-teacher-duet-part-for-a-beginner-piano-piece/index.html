How to Write a Teacher Duet Part for a Beginner Piano Piece

A step-by-step method for adding a simple teacher accompaniment under a beginner's melody, with a fully worked Jingle Bells example and a fill-in planning template you can reuse for any piece.

A beginner melody played alone can sound thin, especially at a recital. When you play a simple part underneath it, the same five notes sound like real music. Your student also gets practice keeping a steady beat with another player.

You don't need training in arranging to do this. You need the melody, three chords and a few accompaniment patterns. This guide takes you through the process step by step. It ends with a fully worked example and a template you can copy.

What a good teacher part does (and doesn't do)

Before writing anything, keep these five rules in mind:

  • Stay out of the student's way. Play in the lower half of the keyboard, at least an octave below the student's lowest note. Your hands should never collide with theirs.
  • Keep the beat obvious. A beginner follows you, so your pattern should make each beat easy to hear.
  • Don't copy the melody. If you double the tune in the same octave, any wrong note the student plays stands out. Leave the melody to the student.
  • Repeat your pattern. One rhythm pattern used all the way through is easier to follow than a part that changes every bar.
  • Make room for mistakes. Your part should be easy enough that you can watch the student and wait for them, or jump ahead with them, when they stumble.

Step 1: Write out the student melody and find its range

Write down the student's melody bar by bar, with note names. Then note the lowest and highest notes.

If the melody sits in middle C position (C D E F G above middle C), your part should mostly stay below the C an octave lower, which is the C below middle C. This also tells you where to sit. The teacher normally sits on the left and plays the lower part, which is called the secondo. The student sits on the right and plays the upper part, the primo.

Step 2: Choose a chord for each bar

Most beginner pieces in C major can be harmonized with just three chords:

  • C major (C E G): fits bars where the melody uses mostly C, E or G
  • F major (F A C): fits bars where the melody uses mostly F, A or C
  • G major or G7 (G B D, plus F for G7): fits bars where the melody uses mostly D, F or B, and the bar just before the final C

Here's a simple way to decide: look at the notes on the strong beats (beats 1 and 3 in 4/4). Choose the chord that contains those notes. If two chords could work, choose the one that sounds right when you play it slowly against the melody. Trust your ear.

Most beginner tunes end with G or G7 resolving to C. Check the last two bars first, because they are almost always G then C.

Step 3: Choose an accompaniment pattern to match the student's level

Choose one pattern and keep it the whole way through. These are listed from easiest to hardest:

  1. Whole-note chords. Play the chord once per bar and hold it. This is best for a student's very first duet, because there's almost nothing to follow except the downbeat.
  2. Root and fifth on beats 1 and 3. For C, play low C then G. This gives a clear pulse without much movement.
  3. Bass note plus chord ("oom-pah"). Play the bass note on beats 1 and 3 and a chord in close position on beats 2 and 4. This has a lively, march-like feel and suits Jingle Bells, Up on the Housetop and Jolly Old St. Nicholas.
  4. Broken chord in quarter notes. For C, play C G E G. This flows well for slow, gentle pieces like Silent Night (in 3/4, play C G E across the bar).
  5. Waltz pattern for 3/4 pieces. Play the bass note on beat 1 and the chord on beats 2 and 3.

A tip for the low register: close chords played very low on the keyboard sound muddy. Keep three-note chords around the octave below middle C, and play single notes or open fifths (C plus G) in the octave below that.

Step 4: Write an introduction and a count-in

Beginners do much better if they hear the tempo before they start. Give your part a two-bar introduction: play your pattern on the opening chord (usually C) for two bars, then the student comes in.

Practise a spoken cue as well, such as "ready, go" on beats 3 and 4 of the last intro bar, so the student knows exactly when to start.

Step 5: Plan the ending

The ending is where duets most often fall apart. Keep it simple:

  • On the final bar, stop your pattern and hold a single low C chord while the student holds their last note.
  • If you want a slow-down at the end (a ritardando), rehearse it. Agree that you'll lift your hands together when you nod.

Step 6: Mark the pedal, or don't use it

If you use the sustain pedal, change it on each new chord, usually on beat 1. In fast, bouncy pieces it's often cleaner to play without pedal at all. For a slow carol, change the pedal once per bar to keep the sound warm without blurring the harmony.

Step 7: Rehearse in this order

  1. The student plays the melody alone at the target tempo, with no stops.
  2. You play your part alone while the student taps the beat on their knee.
  3. Play together at about two-thirds of the target tempo.
  4. Play together at full tempo with the introduction and ending.
  5. Do one rehearsal where you deliberately keep going whatever happens. This prepares the student for recital nerves.

Worked Example: Jingle Bells Chorus in Middle C Position

Student part: right hand in middle C position, thumb on middle C (1 = C, 2 = D, 3 = E, 4 = F, 5 = G). The melody uses only C, D, E, F and G, which makes it ideal for a first duet.

Key: C major. Time: 4/4. Teacher pattern: bass note plus chord ("oom-pah"), the octave below middle C and lower.

Here is the chorus, one bar per line, with the melody notes and the teacher's chord:

  1. E E E (half note on the last E): C
  2. E E E (half): C
  3. E G C D: C
  4. E (whole note): C
  5. F F F F: F
  6. F E E E: C
  7. E D D E: G (D7 is common here too, but G is easier)
  8. D (half), G (half): G7
  9. E E E (half): C
  10. E E E (half): C
  11. E G C D: C
  12. E (whole): C
  13. F F F F: F
  14. F E E E: C
  15. G G F D: G7
  16. C (whole): C

What the teacher actually plays in each bar:

  • C bars: left hand plays the C two octaves below middle C on beats 1 and 3. Right hand plays C-E-G just below middle C on beats 2 and 4.
  • F bars: left hand plays a low F on beats 1 and 3. Right hand plays C-F-A (second inversion, so your hand barely moves) on beats 2 and 4.
  • G and G7 bars: left hand plays a low G on beats 1 and 3. Right hand plays B-D-G, or B-F-G for G7, on beats 2 and 4.
  • Bar 16: hold a low C plus a C-E-G chord for the whole bar. Don't play the oom-pah.

Introduction: two bars of the C oom-pah pattern. Say "ready, go" on beats 3 and 4 of the second bar.

Why the chords move so little: C-E-G, C-F-A and B-D-G all sit within the same hand position. The teacher's right hand stays in one place, which leaves attention free to watch the student.

For a younger student: use pattern 1 instead. Play one whole-note chord per bar, using the same chord symbols.

Rehearsal checklist:

  • [ ] Student plays the melody alone with no stops
  • [ ] Teacher part checked alone while the student taps the beat
  • [ ] Slow play-through together
  • [ ] Full tempo with intro and ending
  • [ ] "Keep going no matter what" run-through

Quick Troubleshooting

  • The student speeds up: make your beats 2 and 4 a little more definite, and simplify to root-fifth if needed.
  • The student stops after a mistake: practise "find the next beat 1" by stopping and restarting at the next bar together.
  • It sounds muddy: move your chords up an octave, or use the pedal less.
  • Your hands bump: move your whole part down an octave. If the student's melody goes below middle C, shift the student up an octave instead.

Once you've used this method on one carol, it works on almost any beginner melody that fits C, F and G chords.
