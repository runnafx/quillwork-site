How to Answer a Client Who Asks "Did You Use AI?" (Scripts)

A step-by-step way for freelancers and small agencies to answer the "did you use AI?" question honestly and quickly. Includes copy-and-paste replies for common situations and a fill-in answer sheet for vendor forms.

Clients now ask this all the time. It shows up in emails, on sales calls, in contract clauses and on vendor onboarding forms. The question is usually not an accusation. Most clients are trying to work out one of four things:

  • Confidentiality: did their material go into a tool that might store or reuse it?
  • Quality: did a person who understands their brief actually check the work?
  • Originality: is the work their own, and could it look like someone else's?
  • Value: if a tool did part of the work, should it cost less?

When you know which concern sits behind the question, your answer gets much easier. This guide gives you a quick process, sample replies you can adapt, and a fill-in sheet you can reuse.

Step 1: Know your own answer before anyone asks

Write down the facts of how you work first. If you make it up on the spot, your answers will drift from client to client, and inconsistent answers do more damage than any one honest answer. Spend 15 minutes on these:

  1. Which tools do you use, and for which tasks? Be specific. For example: "brainstorming headlines", "first-draft outlines", "transcribing interviews", "resizing images", or "none".
  2. Which tasks do you never use AI for? Final copy, client strategy and anything containing personal data are common examples.
  3. What goes into the tools? Do you ever paste in client documents, names, unreleased product details or customer data?
  4. What are the tool settings? Look at each tool's current data and training settings and its terms of service. Don't rely on memory. These terms change.
  5. Who reviews the output? Write down what a human check involves for you: fact-checking, editing for voice, comparing against the brief, running a plagiarism or similarity check.
  6. What do your contracts already say? Read your own terms and any client agreement you've signed. Some clients forbid AI use, or require you to tell them about it, and that clause outranks anything in this guide. If the contract or legal side is unclear, ask a qualified professional.

Step 2: Reply with the facts, then address the concern

A good reply has three parts, and short is fine:

  1. The direct answer. Yes, no, or "yes, for these specific tasks".
  2. The safeguard behind it. Human review, how you handle confidentiality, how you check originality.
  3. An offer. Tell them how to set their own preference, such as "If you'd prefer no AI on your project, just tell me."

Avoid vague phrasing like "I only use it a little" or "it's just a tool like spellcheck." Clients read vagueness as hiding something. Never deny AI use you actually rely on. If the client finds out later, that harms the relationship far more than the honest answer would have.

Step 3: Pick the reply that matches the situation

These are starting points, so edit them to sound like you. Square brackets mark the parts you fill in.

"Did you use AI on this?" (you did, for part of the work)

"Yes, for part of it. I used [tool type] for [specific task, e.g. generating headline options / a rough outline]. Everything you received was written, edited and checked by me against your brief: [list checks, e.g. facts, tone, your style guide]. If you'd like a project with no AI tools at all, tell me and I'll work that way."

"Did you use AI on this?" (you didn't)

"No. This was written entirely by me without AI writing tools. I'm happy to put that in writing for this project if it's useful for your records."

If you used a spellchecker or a grammar tool that has AI features, say so. "No AI" should mean exactly that.

"Is our information safe if you use AI?"

"Good question. I don't paste [confidential material / personal data / unreleased details] into AI tools. Where I do use them, I work from [general prompts / anonymised summaries], and I've turned off [the setting that allows conversations to be used for training], as far as each tool's settings allow. If your company has its own rules on this, send them over and I'll follow them."

Only promise what you actually do and what you've checked in the tool's settings.

"Is this original? Will it pass a plagiarism check?"

"The final work is mine, written for your brief. Before delivery I [run it through a similarity checker / check quotes and statistics against their sources]. I'll send you that report with the files if you'd like it."

Don't promise that the work will "pass" any particular AI detector. Those tools are inconsistent, and you can't control what they report.

"If you use AI, shouldn't it be cheaper?"

"My rate covers the result and my judgement: understanding your audience, deciding what to say, and checking that it's accurate and on-brand. Tools sometimes speed up early drafts, and that time goes into more editing and review on your project. If budget is the concern, I can suggest a smaller scope that fits."

"We don't allow AI. Can you confirm you won't use it?"

"Understood. I'll do this project without AI tools for [writing / design / research]. Just to be clear, I'll still use [standard tools, e.g. a word processor's spellcheck, stock photo libraries]. Let me know if any of those are a problem."

Then follow it. Make a note in the project file so you don't forget halfway through.

On a sales call, said out loud

"Yes, I use AI tools for a few early-stage tasks, like [task]. Nothing goes out without me writing, checking and approving it, and I don't put confidential material into those tools. If you'd prefer I don't use them at all for your work, that's fine. Just tell me."

Step 4: Fill in a standard answer sheet for vendor forms

Onboarding forms and supplier questionnaires ask the same few questions again and again. Fill in this sheet once and copy from it each time. Keep it accurate and update it when your tools change.

  • Do you use generative AI in delivering services? [Yes, for: ___ / No]
  • Which tools or categories of tools? [e.g. "a general-purpose AI writing assistant; an AI transcription tool"]
  • Is client data entered into AI tools? [No / Only anonymised or non-confidential material / Yes, with client approval only]
  • Are AI outputs reviewed by a human? [Yes. Describe: ___]
  • Is AI-generated content delivered without disclosure? [No. I disclose AI involvement on request / in my proposal / on every delivery]
  • Can the client opt out of AI use? [Yes, on request, at no change to the agreed scope]
  • Who owns the deliverables? [Refer to your contract terms. Don't improvise this on a form.]
  • Contact for questions about AI use: [Your name and role]

If a form question is unclear or asks for a legal warranty, write "Please see our contract terms" or ask the client what they need. Don't tick a box you aren't sure about.

Step 5: Disclose before you're asked

You'll get the question less often, and it will sound less awkward, if you raise it yourself at three points:

  1. In your proposal: add one line, for example: "I use AI tools for [tasks]; all deliverables are written and reviewed by me. Tell me if you'd prefer no AI use."
  2. At kickoff: ask, "Does your company have a policy on AI tools or confidential material?" Note the answer in the project file.
  3. At delivery: if AI played a meaningful part, add a short line to your handover note so it's on record.

Quick checklist before you hit send

  • My answer is accurate for this specific project, not just in general.
  • I named specific tasks rather than saying "a little".
  • I said what a human did: writing, editing, checking.
  • I didn't promise anything about confidentiality that I haven't checked in the tool's settings.
  • I didn't guarantee results from AI detectors or make legal promises.
  • I checked the contract for any AI or confidentiality clause.
  • I offered the client a way to opt out or set a preference.
  • I saved this reply so my next answer stays consistent.

The short version

Find out what the client is really worried about, answer directly with specific tasks, describe the human review, and give them a choice. If you keep one accurate answer sheet and reuse it, you'll answer the same way every time, whether the question comes by email, on a call or on a form.
