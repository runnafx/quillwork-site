How to Set KS3 RE Cover Work a Non-Specialist Can Run

A checklist, a fill-in cover-sheet template and a complete worked RE cover lesson you can copy tonight. Written for KS3 RE teachers who have to leave work for a cover supervisor who isn't an RE specialist.

It's 9pm, you'll be off tomorrow, and the person covering your Year 8 RE class may never have taught RE. Good RE cover work is not the same as good RE teaching. The cover supervisor has to be able to run it with no subject knowledge and no preparation, and a sensitive topic must not go wrong while you're away.

This page gives you three things: a checklist to test any cover lesson against, a cover sheet you can fill in, and a full lesson you can use as it is.

The 8-point RE cover checklist

Check your cover work against each point before you send it.

  1. It fits on one printed page for the adult. If the supervisor needs to read a slideshow to understand the lesson, they won't have time to.
  2. Every task has a time next to it. For example: "10 min: starter", "15 min: read and answer". Timings stop a 15-minute task from being stretched across the whole hour.
  3. The supervisor does not need to explain anything. Instructions and key words go on the student sheet, not in the adult's head.
  4. No discussion of personal belief is required. Students can share their views, but no one should feel pressure to say what they or their family believe.
  5. Sensitive points are flagged in advance. If a topic might touch on death, conflict or identity, write one line telling the supervisor what to say if it comes up.
  6. There is an extension task. Fast finishers are the most common cause of low-level disruption in cover lessons.
  7. The work is collected in a form you can check quickly. One written answer or a completed table is easier to mark than a loose worksheet.
  8. It works if the technology fails. Print it. Assume the projector won't be working.

Fill-in cover sheet template

Copy this, fill in the brackets and leave it on top of the printed worksheets.

Class: [Year / set] Room: [ ] Period: [ ] Topic: [one line, e.g. "The Golden Rule in world religions"] Seating plan: [location, e.g. "in the red folder on the desk"]

What students need: [exercise books / worksheets / pens]. Spare pens are [location].

Lesson plan:

  • [0-5 min] Settle, take the register, write the title and date.
  • [5-15 min] Starter: [task]
  • [15-40 min] Main task: [task]
  • [40-50 min] Review: [task]
  • [Last 5 min] Collect [what], tidy up.

If a student asks a question you can't answer: "That's a great question. Write it at the bottom of your page and your teacher will answer it next lesson."

Sensitivity note: [one line, or "None expected"]

Students to be aware of: [names and short notes on seating or support needs; keep private details off the sheet]

Please leave: [where completed work goes] and a note on behaviour or anything that came up.

The line about questions the supervisor can't answer is the most useful one on the sheet. It means a non-specialist never has to guess at a fact about a religion.

Worked example: a complete one-period cover lesson

Here is a full lesson built with the checklist. Copy it as it is or adapt it.

Topic: The Golden Rule across six world religions (Years 7-9)

Lesson aim (for the student sheet): To compare how different religions teach people to treat others, and to decide whether one rule could work for everyone.

Key words (print these on the sheet):

  • Golden Rule: the idea that you should treat others the way you would want to be treated.
  • Ethics: thinking about what is right and wrong.
  • Sacred text: writing that a religion treats as holy or especially important.
  • Universal: something that applies to everyone, everywhere.

Student sheet: the six teachings

These are commonly quoted English versions. Translations vary, so present them as paraphrases rather than exact wording.

  • Christianity: "Do to others as you would have them do to you." (Luke 6:31; see also Matthew 7:12)
  • Judaism: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour." (a teaching of Rabbi Hillel, recorded in the Talmud; see also Leviticus 19:18, "Love your neighbour as yourself")
  • Islam: "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." (a hadith, a saying of the Prophet Muhammad)
  • Hinduism: "One should never do to another that which one would regard as injurious to oneself." (from the Mahabharata)
  • Buddhism: "Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful." (from the Udanavarga)
  • Sikhism: "No one is my enemy and no one is a stranger; I get along with everyone." (from the Guru Granth Sahib, often quoted as a teaching about treating all people as equals)

Lesson plan with timings

0-5 min: settle. Students write the title "The Golden Rule" and the date.

5-12 min: starter. On the board: "Write down one rule you think EVERYONE in the world should follow. Explain why in one sentence." Take three or four answers by hands up. Don't judge them; just say "interesting, thank you."

12-35 min: main task (comparison table). Students draw a table with three columns: *Religion | What the teaching says in my own words | Positive or negative?*

"Positive" means the teaching tells you to do something good, like Christianity's. "Negative" means it tells you not to do something harmful, like Hillel's. Students fill in a row for each of the six religions.

35-45 min: written answer. Students answer this in at least five sentences: "*Could the Golden Rule be a rule for everyone, religious or not?* Give one reason for and one reason against, then your own conclusion."

Put these sentence starters on the sheet:

  • "Some people would agree because..."
  • "However, others might argue..."
  • "In my opinion..."

45-50 min: review. Ask two students to read out their conclusions. Collect the books open at today's page.

Extension (fast finishers): "Think of a situation where following the Golden Rule could go wrong, for example if someone wants to be treated in a way other people don't. How might a religious believer respond?"

Supervisor notes

  • No RE knowledge is needed. Everything students need is on the sheet.
  • Sensitivity: students may mention their own faith or say they have none. Both are fine. If a student makes a dismissive comment about any religion, use the usual school language: "We can disagree respectfully in this room."
  • Don't settle debates. If students argue about which religion is "right," say: "Today's question is about what they have in common. Write your view in your answer."

Five more topics that work well as RE cover

These topics suit a non-specialist because the content can sit fully on the sheet:

  1. Big questions: "Is there a purpose to life?" Students sort questions into ones science can answer, ones religion tries to answer, and ones nobody can fully answer.
  2. Sacred places compared: a church, mosque, gurdwara, mandir, synagogue and Buddhist temple. Students match features to buildings from a labelled sheet.
  3. Festivals of light: Diwali, Hanukkah, Christmas and Vesak. Students fill in a table of what each festival celebrates and how light is used.
  4. Values in action: Martin Luther King Jr and Malala Yousafzai. Students read short factual profiles, then link each person's actions to a value such as justice, courage or education.
  5. Ethics starter: "Is it ever right to lie?" Students work through scenario cards and give for and against reasons. No religious knowledge is needed, so it works when a lesson has to be set in a rush.

Topics to avoid for cover

Keep these for lessons you teach yourself, where you can handle the questions that come up:

  • Death and the afterlife, especially if you don't know the class's recent experiences
  • Current conflicts involving religious groups
  • Religious attitudes to sexuality, abortion or euthanasia
  • Anything that asks students to rank or criticise a specific religion

None of these is off-limits in RE. They just need a specialist in the room.

Before you hit send: quick final check

  • Is everything printed, or saved where the cover team can find it?
  • Is there one page for the adult and one for the students?
  • Does every task have a time?
  • Is there a sensitivity line, even if it says "None expected"?
  • Is there an extension task?
  • Do they know where to leave the books?

If you can tick all six, your cover lesson will run smoothly without you.
