GCSE Christmas Science Quiz: 15 Stretch Questions + Worked Answers

A free Christmas science quiz for Year 10 and 11, with 15 GCSE-level questions across biology, chemistry and physics. Full worked answers, mark scheme and a 50-minute running plan are included, all on this page.

Most Christmas science quizzes are written for Year 7. If you teach Year 10 or 11 in the last week of term, a picture round about reindeer won't hold them for long. This quiz keeps the festive theme but asks real GCSE skills: equations, calculations, required content and explanations. Students can still use the lesson as revision.

Everything you need is below: 15 questions in three rounds, a mark scheme with working, and a plan for a 50–60 minute lesson. You only need a board and paper. A calculator helps for Round 3.

How to Run It in One Lesson

  1. Make teams of 3–4 (5 minutes). Mix abilities so every team has someone confident with equations.
  2. Read out or display each round (about 10 minutes per round). Teams write answers on a single sheet.
  3. Swap sheets and mark after each round (3–4 minutes). Read the worked answers aloud. The marking is where most of the learning happens.
  4. Tiebreak (see the end of the page) if two teams finish level.

Scoring: the marks for each question are shown in brackets. The quiz totals 30 marks. For calculations, give the method mark even if the final answer is wrong. This rewards working and matches how GCSE papers are marked.

Quick differentiation: if a group struggles, give them the equation for each Round 3 question. If a team finishes early, ask them to write a 16th question for another team.

Round 1: Christmas Chemistry (10 marks)

  1. Flaming pudding. Brandy poured over a Christmas pudding is lit. The fuel that burns is ethanol, C2H5OH. Write the balanced symbol equation for the complete combustion of ethanol. (2)
  1. Firework colours. A firework gives a green flame. Which metal ion is most likely responsible? Name one other metal ion and the flame colour it gives. (2)
  1. Sugar maths. Table sugar used in Christmas baking is sucrose, C12H22O11. Calculate its relative formula mass (Mr). Use Ar: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16. (2)
  1. Satsuma fizz. Satsuma juice is added to sodium hydrogencarbonate and it fizzes. Name the gas produced and describe the test for it, including the result. (2)
  1. Gritting roads. Salt is spread on icy roads. Explain in terms of melting point why this helps. (2)

Round 2: Biology of Winter (10 marks)

  1. Out in the cold. A student waits outside for the school Christmas bus and gets very cold. Describe two ways the body responds to reduce heat loss or produce more heat. (2)
  1. Mistletoe. Mistletoe has green leaves but grows attached to the branches of trees, taking water and mineral ions from its host. Is mistletoe able to photosynthesise? Give a reason for your answer. (2)
  1. The Christmas tree. Write the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis, which a Christmas tree carries out when it is still growing. (2)
  1. Turkey test. Name the reagent used to test for protein in a sample of turkey. State the colour change for a positive result. (2)
  1. Short days. In the UK the shortest day of the year falls around 21 December. Give one way a plant's rate of photosynthesis is affected in winter, and name the limiting factor responsible. (2)

Round 3: Festive Physics (10 marks)

  1. Fairy lights. A set of mains fairy lights draws a current of 0.05 A from a 230 V supply. Calculate the power. (2)
  1. Left on all evening. The same lights are left on for 6 hours. Calculate the energy transferred in joules. (2)
  1. Cup of tea for Santa. Calculate the energy needed to heat 0.5 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg °C. (2)
  1. Jingle bells. A bell produces a sound wave with a frequency of 2000 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the wavelength. (2)
  1. Bauble reflections. A shiny bauble works as a convex mirror. Describe the image you see of your face in it. Give two features. (2)

Worked Answers and Mark Scheme

Round 1

  1. C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O. 1 mark for the correct products (carbon dioxide and water). 1 mark for correct balancing. A common error is forgetting the oxygen atom already in the ethanol, which makes students write 3.5O2 or 4O2.
  1. Copper ions (Cu²⁺) give a green flame. 1 mark. Any one of the following for the second mark: lithium is crimson, sodium is yellow, potassium is lilac, calcium is orange-red.
  1. Mr = 342. Working: (12 × 12) + (22 × 1) + (11 × 16) = 144 + 22 + 176 = 342. 1 mark for the working and 1 for the answer.
  1. Carbon dioxide. 1 mark. Bubble the gas through limewater, and the limewater turns milky or cloudy. 1 mark. Satsumas contain citric acid, and an acid plus a hydrogencarbonate gives a salt, water and carbon dioxide.
  1. Salt lowers the melting point of the ice (1 mark), so the ice melts at temperatures below 0 °C, or water does not refreeze as easily (1 mark). Pure water melts at a fixed 0 °C, but a mixture melts at a lower temperature. This links to the idea of purity.

Round 2

  1. Any two, 1 mark each:
  • Shivering. Muscles contract rapidly, and respiration in them releases heat.
  • Vasoconstriction. Blood vessels supplying skin capillaries narrow, so less blood flows near the surface and less heat is lost.
  • Hairs stand up. This traps a layer of insulating air. It has little effect in humans, but accept it.
  • Sweating stops or is reduced.

Students should not say that "veins move" or that "blood vessels move away from the skin". The vessels narrow. They do not move.

  1. Yes (1 mark). It has green leaves, so it contains chlorophyll and can absorb light (1 mark). Mistletoe is often called a hemiparasite: it photosynthesises but takes water and mineral ions from the host tree.
  1. 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2. 1 mark for the correct formulae and 1 for balancing. Accept "light" written over the arrow.
  1. Biuret reagent (1 mark). It changes from blue to purple or lilac (1 mark).
  1. The rate of photosynthesis is lower. 1 mark. Limiting factor, 1 mark: light intensity (fewer daylight hours and weaker light) or temperature (enzymes work more slowly in the cold). Accept either one, as long as it is linked to the effect.

Round 3

  1. P = IV = 0.05 × 230 = 11.5 W. 1 mark for the substitution and 1 for the answer with its unit.
  1. Time = 6 × 3600 = 21,600 s. E = Pt = 11.5 × 21,600 = 248,400 J. 1 mark for converting hours to seconds and 1 for the answer. Students often get this wrong by using 6 instead of 21,600. Allow error carried forward from Q11.
  1. E = mcΔθ = 0.5 × 4200 × 80 = 168,000 J (168 kJ). 1 mark for ΔT = 80 °C and 1 for the answer.
  1. Rearrange v = fλ to λ = v ÷ f. λ = 340 ÷ 2000 = 0.17 m. 1 mark for the rearrangement and 1 for the answer with its unit.
  1. Any two, 1 mark each: virtual, upright, diminished (smaller than the object). A convex mirror always forms this type of image. For extra credit, ask why the edges of your face look stretched or curved.

Tiebreak Questions

Use these in order until one team wins. The answer can be a number or a single word.

  • Closest guess wins: how many seconds are there in the 24 hours of Christmas Day? *(86,400)*
  • Christmas crackers snap because of a very small amount of an explosive compound on the strip. Name the metal in it. *(Silver. The compound is silver fulminate.)*
  • Ice floats on water. Is ice more or less dense than liquid water? *(Less dense)*

Teacher Tips

  • Keep the equations visible after the quiz. A photo of the board with all the worked answers makes a useful revision handout for the start of January.
  • Watch for unit errors in Round 3. Hours not converted to seconds (Q12) and missing units (Q11, Q14) lose more marks here than misunderstanding does. Point this out when you mark.
  • Adapt for your exam board. All the content is common GCSE combined science. Check the wording of the flame test colours against your own specification. Some boards describe copper as "blue-green" rather than "green", so accept both.
  • For a full hour, add a picture or anagram round of your own, or let each team present the question they wrote while waiting.
  • Lower-attaining groups: give the equations for Round 3 and offer a choice of answers for Q2 and Q9. The content stays the same, but students can get started more easily.

Some answers are biology or physics explanations rather than numbers. When a team's wording is different but scientifically correct, give the mark. The aim is to check understanding, not to make students copy the mark scheme word for word.
