Free KS3 Binary Cover Lesson: No-Computer Plan + Answer Key

A complete one-hour binary and number lesson for Years 7-9 that a non-specialist cover supervisor can run with paper and a board. It includes a script, timings, a worksheet you can copy out, and a full answer key.

Binary is a good topic for Computing cover. Pupils need nothing but paper and a pen, every answer is simply right or wrong, and the supervisor doesn't need to know any Computing to mark it. This page gives you the whole lesson: what to write on the board, what to say, a worksheet to copy out, and every answer.

Suitable for: Year 7 to Year 9 (KS3). Year 7 can stop after Part B. Year 9 should reach the extension. Time: 60 minutes. Needs: a whiteboard and pens. Pupils need pen and paper. There are no computers, slides or printing, unless you'd like to print the worksheet section below.

Before the lesson (2 minutes)

Write this row of numbers across the top of the board and leave it up all lesson:

128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1

That row is all the "subject knowledge" you need. Every question in the lesson is solved with it.

Lesson timings at a glance

  1. Settle and starter: 10 minutes
  2. Explanation and worked examples: 10 minutes
  3. Part A, binary to normal numbers: 12 minutes
  4. Part B, normal numbers to binary: 13 minutes
  5. Part C, thinking questions, and the secret message: 10 minutes
  6. Go through the answers and plenary: 5 minutes

Cover supervisor script

Starter (10 minutes)

Say: "Today's lesson is on binary. That's how every computer stores numbers, pictures and text using only 0s and 1s. You don't need a computer, just your brain."

Write on the board: "How high can you count on one hand?" Give pupils one minute to talk to a partner. Most will say 5. Then say: "Computers can count to 31 on five 'fingers', because each finger is either up (1) or down (0) and each finger is worth a different amount. By the end of today you'll know how."

Explanation (10 minutes)

Point to the row on the board. Say: "Each column is worth double the one to its right. A 1 means 'count this column'. A 0 means 'ignore it'. Add up the columns with a 1 and you have the number."

Work through these two examples on the board, slowly:

  • 00001101: the 1s sit under 8, 4 and 1. 8 + 4 + 1 = 13
  • 01000010: the 1s sit under 64 and 2. 64 + 2 = 66

Then show the reverse direction with 37:

  • Say: "Start at the left. Does 128 fit into 37? No, so write 0." Do the same for 64 (0) and 32 (yes, so write 1, leaving 5).
  • 16? No (0). 8? No (0). 4? Yes (1, leaving 1). 2? No (0). 1? Yes (1, leaving 0).
  • Answer: 00100101. Check it: 32 + 4 + 1 = 37.

Say: "Always check your answer by adding the columns back up."

Behaviour tips

  • Let pupils work in pairs for Part A, then on their own for Part B.
  • Early finishers go on to Part C and then the extension. Nobody should be sitting with nothing to do.
  • If a pupil says "I don't get it", point at the row on the board and ask "Which columns have a 1?" That one question solves most problems.

Pupil worksheet (copy onto the board or print)

Part A: Turn binary into a normal (denary) number

  1. 00000101
  2. 00001010
  3. 00010001
  4. 00101010
  5. 01100100
  6. 10000000
  7. 11001000
  8. 11111111

Part B: Turn the normal number into 8-bit binary

  1. 3
  2. 9
  3. 20
  4. 33
  5. 64
  6. 99
  7. 150
  8. 250

Part C: Thinking questions

  1. What is the biggest number you can make with 8 bits? How many different numbers (including 0) can 8 bits store?
  2. What is the biggest number you can make with only 4 bits?
  3. Write 6 in binary. Now move every digit one place left and add a 0 on the end. What number do you get? What always happens?
  4. Look at your answers to Part B. How can you tell from the binary alone whether a number is odd or even?

Secret message

Use the code A = 1, B = 2, C = 3 … Z = 26. Each letter is written as a 5-bit binary number (columns 16, 8, 4, 2, 1). Decode the word:

00010 01001 01110 00001 10010 11001

Then write your own name in 5-bit binary and swap it with a partner to decode.

Extension: Binary addition

Add these by changing each one to a normal number, adding, then changing the answer back to binary. Fast pupils can try adding column by column, remembering that 1 + 1 = 10 in binary (write 0, carry 1).

  1. 00000101 + 00000011
  2. 00010110 + 00001101

Answer key

Part A

  1. 00000101 = 5 (4 + 1)
  2. 00001010 = 10 (8 + 2)
  3. 00010001 = 17 (16 + 1)
  4. 00101010 = 42 (32 + 8 + 2)
  5. 01100100 = 100 (64 + 32 + 4)
  6. 10000000 = 128
  7. 11001000 = 200 (128 + 64 + 8)
  8. 11111111 = 255 (all columns added together)

Part B

  1. 3 = 00000011
  2. 9 = 00001001
  3. 20 = 00010100
  4. 33 = 00100001
  5. 64 = 01000000
  6. 99 = 01100011 (64 + 32 + 2 + 1)
  7. 150 = 10010110 (128 + 16 + 4 + 2)
  8. 250 = 11111010 (128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 2)

Accept answers without the leading zeros (for example, 11 for 3) if the digits are otherwise correct. But do remind pupils that computers store a full 8 bits.

Part C

  1. The biggest is 255. 8 bits can store 256 different values (0 to 255).
  2. The biggest with 4 bits is 15 (8 + 4 + 2 + 1).
  3. 6 = 00000110. Shifted left it becomes 00001100 = 12. Shifting left by one place always doubles the number, as long as no 1 falls off the left end.
  4. If the last (rightmost) bit is 1, the number is odd. If it is 0, it is even. Only the 1 column is odd.

Secret message

00010 = 2 = B, 01001 = 9 = I, 01110 = 14 = N, 00001 = 1 = A, 10010 = 18 = R, 11001 = 25 = Y. The word is BINARY.

Extension

  1. 5 + 3 = 8 = 00001000
  2. 22 + 13 = 35 = 00100011

Plenary (5 minutes)

Read out the answers to Part A and Part B and have pupils mark their own work. Then ask three quick questions, with hands up or mini-whiteboards if you have them:

  • "What does a 1 in a column mean?" (Count that column.)
  • "What's the biggest 8-bit number?" (255.)
  • "Is 10010111 odd or even?" (Odd, because the last bit is 1.)

Finish by saying: "Every photo, song and message on your phone is stored as patterns like these."

Note for the class teacher

Collect the worksheets or ask pupils to stick them in their books. Pupils who finished the extension are ready for hexadecimal or binary addition with carries next lesson. Pupils who struggled with Part B need more practice going from denary to binary using the "does it fit?" method.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Reading the columns the wrong way round. The 1 column is always on the far right.
  • Writing fewer than 8 digits and getting the columns out of line. Tell pupils to write the 128-to-1 row above their answer.
  • Using a column twice. Each column can only be 0 or 1. There is no "2" in binary.
  • Not checking. Every Part B answer can be checked by adding the columns back up, so pupils can mark their own work before you go through the answers.
