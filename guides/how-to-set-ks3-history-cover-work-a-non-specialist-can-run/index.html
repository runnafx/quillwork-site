How to Set KS3 History Cover Work a Non-Specialist Can Run

A checklist, a cover note template you can fill in, and a complete no-tech Magna Carta lesson with answers. Use it to leave a one-hour KS3 history cover lesson that works even when the supervisor knows no history.

It's late in the evening, you won't be in tomorrow, and you have Year 8 history period 3. The person covering may be a cover supervisor, a supply teacher or a colleague from maths. They may have no projector or laptop, and they will see your note about two minutes before the class arrives.

Good history cover work has to work for that person and that situation. This page gives you three things:

  • a checklist for setting it
  • a cover note template you can copy
  • a full worked example: a one-hour Magna Carta lesson with the reading, a source task, questions and an answer key

The checklist: before you send the cover work

Go through this list before you email the cover work or leave it on your desk.

Logistics

  • [ ] Class, year group, room and period are on the first line of the cover note
  • [ ] The total number of copies is stated, with one per student plus three spare
  • [ ] Everything is on paper. Nothing needs a login, projector, video or website
  • [ ] You've said where to collect the work and where to leave it afterwards
  • [ ] Seating plan attached, or you've said where to find it

The task itself

  • [ ] Students can start in the first two minutes without anyone explaining history
  • [ ] The reading is on the sheet, so no textbook page numbers that might be wrong
  • [ ] Instructions are written for the students, not for the supervisor
  • [ ] Every task has a time guide (for example, "15 minutes")
  • [ ] There is an extension task for fast finishers
  • [ ] An answer key is included so the supervisor can help without guessing

Behaviour and ending the lesson

  • [ ] One line on any students who need particular support or seating
  • [ ] Students know what to hand in (book, sheet or both) and what "finished" looks like
  • [ ] There's a last-five-minutes task so the lesson doesn't fizzle out

If you can only manage three of these, choose: reading on the sheet, an answer key, and a clear "hand in" instruction.

What makes history cover go wrong

History cover often fails in a few common ways:

  • "Continue from last lesson." The supervisor has no idea where last lesson ended, and students say they finished it.
  • Source work with no context. A primary source with no background reading leaves students stuck, and the supervisor can't help.
  • "Research..." tasks. These need devices, or turn into copying from whatever is nearby.
  • One big essay question. Weaker writers stall at minute five. Split the hour into short timed chunks.

A standalone lesson that teaches one event from start to finish avoids all four. It doesn't matter where the class is in your scheme of work. Retrieval and source skills are worth practising in any unit.

The cover note template (copy and fill in)

---

COVER: Year __ History | Class: ____ | Room: ____ | Period: ____

Copies: ___ reading/question sheets (pack is in __________)

Seating: Seating plan is ____________ / students sit as normal

What students do (60 minutes):

  1. Starter (5 min): Students answer the starter question in their books. No discussion needed.
  2. Reading (15 min): Students read the sheet in silence. Underline three key facts.
  3. Source task (10 min): Answer the three source questions under the source box.
  4. Questions (15 min): Questions 1-8 in full sentences in books.
  5. Paragraph (10 min): "How far do you agree...?" Use the sentence starters.
  6. Plenary (5 min): Write one thing they learned and one question they still have.

Fast finishers: ____________________________

Students to be aware of: ____________________

At the end: Books collected and left ____________. Sheets ______ (collected / glued in).

Answer key: Last page of the pack.

Thank you. — ____________

---

Worked example: a complete Magna Carta cover lesson (Year 7 or 8)

Everything below can go straight onto two sides of A4.

Starter (5 minutes)

*Should a ruler ever be above the law? Write two sentences giving your opinion and a reason.*

Reading (15 minutes)

King John and the barons

King John ruled England from 1199 to 1216, and by 1215 many of his most powerful nobles, the barons, were furious with him.

There were several reasons. John had lost Normandy, a large area of land in France, in 1204. To pay for wars to win it back, he demanded heavy taxes from the barons. One of these was "scutage", a payment barons made instead of providing soldiers. John also quarrelled with the Pope, and for several years church services across England were banned. Many barons felt John punished people unfairly and ignored the customs that were supposed to limit a king.

In 1215 a group of rebel barons took control of London. John was forced to negotiate. On 15 June 1215, at Runnymede, a meadow beside the River Thames near Windsor, John agreed to a document of demands. It later became known as Magna Carta, Latin for "Great Charter".

Magna Carta had 63 clauses. Many dealt with problems that mattered only to barons at the time, such as taxes and inheritance. A few were much bigger ideas. The most famous said that free men could not be imprisoned or punished except by lawful judgement or by the law of the land. In other words, even the king had to follow the law.

The agreement didn't last. Within weeks John asked the Pope to cancel it, and Pope Innocent III declared it invalid in August 1215. War broke out between John and the barons. John died in October 1216, and the charter was reissued in a changed form under his young son, Henry III, in 1216, 1217 and 1225.

Today, four original copies from 1215 survive. Many people see Magna Carta as an early step towards the idea that nobody, not even a ruler, is above the law. Historians point out that at the time it mainly protected the rich and powerful, and most ordinary people were not "free men" in its terms.

Source task (10 minutes)

Source A: Clause 39 of Magna Carta, 1215, in a modern English translation.

> "No free man shall be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights or possessions, or outlawed or exiled... except by the lawful judgement of his equals or by the law of the land."

  1. Content: What does this source say the king cannot do?
  2. Origin: Who wrote this, and when? (Use the reading.)
  3. Purpose: Why did the barons want this clause included?

Retrieval questions (15 minutes)

  1. Who was King of England in 1215?
  2. What land in France did John lose in 1204?
  3. Give two reasons the barons were angry with John.
  4. Where and when did John agree to Magna Carta?
  5. What does "Magna Carta" mean?
  6. How many clauses did it have?
  7. What happened to Magna Carta in August 1215?
  8. Name the king who reissued it.

Extended paragraph (10 minutes)

*"Magna Carta was a failure." How far do you agree?*

Sentence starters:

  • *Some people might agree because...*
  • *However, others would disagree because...*
  • *Overall, I think... because...*

Plenary (5 minutes)

Look back at your starter answer. Has your opinion changed? Write one sentence explaining why or why not.

Extension for fast finishers

Write the three clauses you would have added to Magna Carta if you had been a peasant in 1215, with a reason for each.

Answer key (for the supervisor)

Source task

  1. The king cannot imprison, outlaw, exile or take things from a free man without a lawful judgement or the law of the land.
  2. Written by the rebel barons and agreed by King John, in 1215.
  3. To stop John punishing them unfairly and to make the king follow the law.

Retrieval questions

  1. King John.
  2. Normandy.
  3. Any two of: heavy taxes such as scutage; losing Normandy; his quarrel with the Pope; unfair punishments.
  4. Runnymede, near Windsor, on 15 June 1215.
  5. Great Charter.
  6. 63.
  7. Pope Innocent III declared it invalid, and war broke out.
  8. Henry III.

Paragraph: Good answers give both sides. On the "failure" side: it was cancelled within weeks and war followed. On the other side: it was reissued several times and became a symbol of the idea that rulers must follow the law. The best answers end with a clear judgement and a reason.

Reusing this structure for any topic

The same six-part shape works for almost any KS3 history topic:

  1. starter opinion question
  2. a 500-700 word reading
  3. a short source with content, origin and purpose questions
  4. eight retrieval questions
  5. a "how far do you agree" paragraph with sentence starters
  6. a plenary that refers back to the starter

Topics that suit it well include 1066, the Black Death, the Gunpowder Plot, child labour in the Industrial Revolution and the Suffragettes. Write one lesson and keep a few printed copies in a labelled "emergency cover" folder in your department, and the late-evening scramble gets much easier.
